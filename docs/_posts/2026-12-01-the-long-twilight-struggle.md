## The Man Who Didn't Press the Button

Shortly after midnight on 26 September 1983, in a secret bunker called Serpukhov-15, south of Moscow, an alarm began to wail. Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov of the Soviet Air Defence Forces looked up at the big screen in front of him. The word on it, in red, was START. The Soviet Union's new satellite early-warning system was reporting that an American intercontinental ballistic missile had been launched towards the USSR. Then it reported another. Then three more.

Petrov's job was to pass the warning up the chain of command, where it could end, within minutes, in a decision to launch Soviet missiles in retaliation. Relations between the superpowers were the worst they had been in twenty years. Three weeks earlier, a Soviet fighter had shot down a Korean airliner that had strayed into Soviet airspace, killing all 269 people on board, and Washington and Moscow were trading furious accusations. The Soviet leadership half believed that the United States was preparing a surprise attack.

Petrov did not believe it. Why, he reasoned, would the Americans start a nuclear war with five missiles, when they had more than a thousand? The system was new and he did not trust it. The ground radars, which would detect real missiles once they rose over the horizon, showed nothing. With, as he later said, a funny feeling in his gut, he reported a system malfunction. Then he waited. If he was wrong, the first warheads would arrive in about twenty minutes. They never came. The satellites had mistaken sunlight glinting off high clouds for the flare of rocket engines.

Petrov was not rewarded. He was reprimanded for poor paperwork, and the incident was kept secret until after the Soviet Union collapsed. He died in 2017, in a small flat outside Moscow, having spent decades insisting that he was not a hero, just a man who had done his job.

This essay is about the world in which a lieutenant colonel's gut feeling was all that stood between humanity and catastrophe. It was called the Cold War, and it lasted from the end of the Second World War until the Soviet Union dissolved on 26 December 1991. For forty-four years, two superpowers and their allies glared at each other across a divided planet, built enough nuclear weapons to destroy civilisation several times over, fought each other through proxies on almost every continent, raced each other to the Moon, spied on each other with extraordinary ingenuity, and somehow never fought the direct war that everybody feared.

It was a frightening time. It was also, at moments, a ridiculous one, and the story is full of characters who seem to have walked out of a spy novel, because many of the spy novels were based on them.

## What Was It, Exactly?

The phrase "cold war" was used by George Orwell in an essay of October 1945 called "You and the Atom Bomb", in which he predicted that atomic weapons would produce a world of super-states locked in a permanent "peace that is no peace". It was popularised in 1947 by the American financier Bernard Baruch, in a speech written for him by a journalist called Herbert Bayard Swope, and by the commentator Walter Lippmann, who published a book called *The Cold War* the same year. A cold war, the idea went, was one in which the great powers were enemies in every way except open, direct fighting with each other.

The two sides were led by the United States and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, the USSR, which was ruled from Moscow and included Russia, Ukraine, the Baltic states, Belarus, the Caucasus and Central Asia. They had been allies against Nazi Germany, but they were divided by almost everything else.

The United States was a capitalist democracy, with private property, competitive elections, a free press and a market economy. The Soviet Union was a one-party communist state, in which the Communist Party controlled the government, the economy was owned and planned by the state, and dissent was punished, often with prison camps or death. Each believed that its system was the future of humanity and that the other's was a dangerous lie. Each feared that the other wanted to spread its system across the world by force or subversion. Both, at times, were right.

The conflict was also about something older than ideology: power, territory and security. Russia had been invaded from the west three times in little more than a century, by Napoleon in 1812 and by Germany in 1914 and 1941. The Second World War had killed perhaps 27 million Soviet citizens. Stalin was determined that it would never happen again, and his idea of security was a ring of friendly, which is to say controlled, states along the Soviet Union's western border. To Americans and western Europeans, that ring looked like conquest.

## From Allies to Enemies

### Yalta and Potsdam

In February 1945, with Germany close to defeat, the "Big Three" met at Yalta, a resort in Crimea on the Black Sea. Winston Churchill, the British prime minister, Franklin D. Roosevelt, the American president, and Joseph Stalin, the Soviet dictator, sat down to plan the post-war world.

![The "Big Three" at Yalta in February 1945: Winston Churchill, Franklin D. Roosevelt and Joseph Stalin seated in the courtyard of the Livadia Palace.](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/yalta.jpg)
*Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin at the Yalta Conference, February 1945. US Army Signal Corps, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

They agreed that Germany would be divided into zones of occupation, as would its capital, Berlin, which lay deep inside the Soviet zone. They agreed to set up the United Nations. Stalin promised to join the war against Japan, and to allow free elections in the countries of eastern Europe that the Red Army had liberated, or was about to liberate, from the Nazis. That last promise was the one that mattered, and Stalin had no intention of keeping it in any form the West would recognise. Roosevelt, visibly ill, died two months later. Critics later accused him of "selling out" eastern Europe at Yalta, but the truth is starker: the Red Army was already there, and nothing short of another war was going to remove it.

By the time the Allies met again, at Potsdam outside Berlin in July 1945, the cast had changed. Harry S. Truman, a former haberdasher from Missouri who had been vice-president for less than three months and had been told almost nothing, was now president. Churchill was replaced halfway through the conference by Clement Attlee, after Labour won the British general election. And Truman had a secret: the United States had just successfully tested an atomic bomb in the New Mexico desert. On 24 July he casually mentioned to Stalin that America had "a new weapon of unusual destructive force". Stalin showed no particular interest. He did not need to; Soviet spies inside the Manhattan Project had already told him all about it.

Less than three weeks later, atomic bombs destroyed Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and the Second World War ended. The Cold War had, in a sense, already begun.

### The Long Telegram and the iron curtain

In February 1946 an American diplomat in Moscow called George Kennan sent Washington a famously long cable, the **Long Telegram**, explaining how the Soviet leadership thought. Soviet hostility to the West, he argued, came not from anything the West had done but from the regime's need for an external enemy to justify its dictatorship at home, layered on top of a traditional Russian sense of insecurity. The USSR could not be charmed or reasoned with, but it was cautious, and it would back down in the face of firm, patient resistance. Kennan's ideas, later published anonymously in an article signed "X", became the basis of the American strategy that dominated the whole Cold War: **containment**. The idea was not to destroy the Soviet Union but to stop it from expanding, and wait for it to change or decay from within.

A month later, on 5 March 1946, Winston Churchill, now out of office, gave a speech at Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri, with Truman sitting on the platform. Its most famous line gave the age its defining image: "From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent." Behind that line, Churchill said, lay the ancient capitals of central and eastern Europe, all now in the Soviet sphere. Stalin called the speech a call to war. Many Americans thought it alarmist. Within two years it looked like prophecy.

### Doctrine, dollars and a coup in Prague

In 1947 Britain, exhausted and nearly bankrupt, told Washington that it could no longer afford to support the government of Greece in its civil war against communist insurgents, or to prop up Turkey against Soviet pressure. On 12 March 1947 Truman asked Congress for money to take over the job, declaring that it must be the policy of the United States to support "free peoples" resisting subjugation by armed minorities or outside pressure. This became known as the **Truman Doctrine**, and it turned containment into a global commitment.

Three months later the Secretary of State, George C. Marshall, announced a plan to rebuild the shattered economies of Europe with American aid. Over the next four years the **Marshall Plan** gave roughly 13 billion dollars, a vast sum at the time, to the countries of western Europe, including Britain, France, West Germany, Italy and Norway. It was generous, and it was also clever: prosperous democracies were far less likely to vote communist, and they would buy American goods. The offer was formally open to the Soviet Union and its satellites as well, but Stalin rejected it and forced the eastern European governments to do the same, seeing it, not unreasonably, as an attempt to draw them into America's economic orbit.

The last independent government in eastern Europe went in February 1948, when the Czechoslovak communists seized power in Prague. Two weeks later the foreign minister, Jan Masaryk, a popular non-communist and son of the country's founder, was found dead in the courtyard below his bathroom window. The official verdict was suicide. Very few people believed it. Prague has a long and grim tradition of political defenestration, and this one became a symbol of how the iron curtain had closed.

## Berlin: The First Showdown

### The blockade

Of all the places where the two sides met, none was stranger than Berlin. The old German capital was divided into four sectors, American, British, French and Soviet, but it sat about 160 kilometres inside the Soviet zone of Germany, an island of the West surrounded by communism.

In June 1948 the Western powers introduced a new currency, the Deutschmark, in their zones of Germany, a vital step towards economic recovery and, as Stalin rightly saw, towards a separate West German state. Stalin responded by cutting off all road, rail and canal links between the western zones and West Berlin. More than two million people were suddenly cut off from food, coal and medicine. The message was simple: abandon Berlin, or watch it starve.

The Western allies had two bad options: give up the city, or send in tanks to break the blockade and risk war. They chose a third, which almost nobody thought would work. They would supply the entire city from the air.

### The airlift

The **Berlin Airlift** began on 26 June 1948. American and British transport aircraft, joined by crews from Australia, New Zealand, Canada and South Africa, flew in a constant stream along three narrow air corridors into West Berlin's airports at Tempelhof and Gatow, and later a new airport at Tegel, built in about three months by Berliners, many of them women, working around the clock. They carried flour, milk powder, medicine and, above all, coal, because Berlin needed heat and electricity, and coal was heavy.

![US Air Force C-47 and US Navy R4D transport aircraft unloading at Tempelhof airport, West Berlin, during the Berlin Airlift of 1948-49.](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/airlift.jpg)
*C-47s at Tempelhof airport during the Berlin Airlift, 1948. US Air Force, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

At first it seemed hopeless. But the operation was steadily organised into a precise, relentless machine, under the American general William Tunner, who timed every landing, unloading and take-off and banned crews from leaving their aircraft on the ground. On the "Easter Parade" of 16 April 1949, the airlift flew nearly 1,400 flights in 24 hours, about one a minute, carrying more than 11,000 tonnes. By the time it wound down in September 1949, there had been some 278,000 flights carrying over two million tonnes of supplies. Around a hundred people, most of them aircrew, died in crashes and accidents.

The airlift produced one of the Cold War's gentlest heroes. An American pilot called Gail Halvorsen, chatting to a group of children at the fence at Tempelhof, was struck that they did not beg for anything. He promised to drop sweets for them the next day, and told them they would know his plane because he would wiggle its wings. He tied chocolate bars and chewing gum to tiny handkerchief parachutes and dropped them over the approach to the airport. Soon other crews joined in, American confectioners donated tonnes of sweets, and children all over Berlin were waiting for the "*Rosinenbomber*", the raisin bombers. Halvorsen became known as the "Candy Bomber". He died in 2022, at the age of 101.

Here is the kind of conversation that took place by the fence. The words are imagined; the scene is real.

> **A Berlin boy:** Are you American? Do you fly the big planes?
>
> **Halvorsen:** I do. I bring in flour. Sometimes coal.
>
> **Boy:** My father says if the Americans leave, the Russians will take everything.
>
> **Halvorsen:** We're not leaving. Listen: tomorrow, when you see a plane wiggle its wings, watch the sky.
>
> **Boy:** Why?
>
> **Halvorsen:** You'll see.

On 12 May 1949 Stalin gave up and reopened the land routes. The blockade had been a disaster for him. It had turned the Germans of the western zones, who four years earlier had been the enemy, into grateful allies, and it had persuaded the Western democracies that they needed a permanent military alliance.

### Two Germanys, one alliance, one bomb

1949 was the year the Cold War took its fixed shape. In April, twelve countries, including the United States, Canada, Britain, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Denmark, Iceland, Portugal and Norway, founded the **North Atlantic Treaty Organisation**, NATO. Its core promise, in Article 5, was that an armed attack against one member would be considered an attack against them all. In May the Federal Republic of Germany, West Germany, was founded in the western zones, with its capital in the small Rhineland city of Bonn. In October the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic, East Germany, which was neither democratic nor, in any real sense, a republic.

Then came two shocks. On 29 August 1949 the Soviet Union tested its first atomic bomb, years earlier than American experts had predicted, thanks partly to information supplied by spies such as the German-born British physicist Klaus Fuchs. America's nuclear monopoly was over. And on 1 October 1949 Mao Zedong stood on the Gate of Heavenly Peace in Beijing and proclaimed the People's Republic of China. The most populous country on Earth had gone communist. In Washington, the question "Who lost China?" poisoned politics for years.

In 1950 a secret American policy paper, **NSC-68**, called for a massive military build-up to meet a Soviet threat that it described in apocalyptic terms. It might have stayed a paper. Then, in June 1950, a war broke out in Korea.

## Korea: The Cold War Turns Hot

Korea had been a Japanese colony until 1945. At the end of the war, almost as an afterthought, two young American officers were given about half an hour to propose a line dividing the Soviet and American zones of occupation, and picked the 38th parallel of latitude, because it put the capital, Seoul, in the American zone. The line was meant to be temporary. By 1948 it divided two hostile states: a communist North under Kim Il Sung, backed by Moscow, and an authoritarian, anti-communist South under Syngman Rhee, backed by Washington. Both leaders wanted to unify the country on their own terms.

On 25 June 1950, with Stalin's approval and Mao's support, the North Korean army crossed the 38th parallel and swept south. Within weeks the South Korean and American forces had been pushed into a small pocket around the port of Pusan at the south-eastern tip of the peninsula. The United Nations Security Council voted to send forces to defend South Korea, a vote that was possible only because the Soviet delegate was boycotting the council at the time, in protest at the UN's refusal to give China's seat to Mao's government. It was one of the great diplomatic own goals of the century.

Then the American commander, General Douglas MacArthur, gambled. On 15 September 1950 he landed troops at Inchon, far behind North Korean lines near Seoul, despite tides so extreme that the landing craft had only a few hours of deep water each day. It worked brilliantly. The North Korean army collapsed and fled north, and UN forces pursued it across the 38th parallel, almost to the Yalu River on the Chinese border.

![First Lieutenant Baldomero Lopez of the US Marine Corps leads his platoon over the seawall at Inchon on 15 September 1950. He was killed minutes later, throwing himself on a grenade to protect his men.](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/inchon.jpg)
*First Lieutenant Baldomero Lopez scaling the seawall at Inchon, 15 September 1950. US Marine Corps, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Mao had warned that China would not tolerate American troops on its border. MacArthur did not believe him. In October and November 1950, hundreds of thousands of Chinese "volunteers" poured across the Yalu and drove the UN forces back down the peninsula in one of the longest retreats in American military history. MacArthur demanded permission to bomb China and talked publicly about using nuclear weapons; in April 1951 Truman sacked him for insubordination, which was hugely unpopular at the time and is generally regarded by historians as entirely correct.

The war then settled into two years of bloody stalemate around the 38th parallel, while negotiators argued, among other things, about whether prisoners of war who did not want to go home should be forced to. An armistice was signed on 27 July 1953. No peace treaty has ever been signed. Perhaps three million people died, most of them Korean civilians, and the peninsula remains divided along a demilitarised zone close to where the war began. Korea set the pattern for the Cold War's hot wars: fought by proxies and allies, limited deliberately to avoid a superpower clash, and never quite finished.

## The Bomb, the Bigger Bomb and the Biggest Bomb

The atomic bombs that destroyed Hiroshima and Nagasaki released energy by splitting heavy atoms of uranium or plutonium. By the late 1940s, scientists on both sides knew that a far more powerful weapon was possible, using a fission bomb as a trigger to fuse together light atoms of hydrogen, the process that powers the Sun. The scientists argued bitterly about whether to build it. J. Robert Oppenheimer, who had led the Manhattan Project, opposed it, which later helped his enemies strip him of his security clearance. Truman ordered it built anyway.

On 1 November 1952 the United States exploded the first hydrogen device, codenamed **Ivy Mike**, on an island in the Pacific. Its yield was about 10 megatons, roughly 700 times the bomb dropped on Hiroshima. The island of Elugelab simply disappeared, leaving a crater more than a kilometre and a half wide. The Soviets tested their own thermonuclear weapons in 1953 and 1955.

On 1 March 1954 the Americans tested a device codenamed **Castle Bravo** at Bikini Atoll. Its designers expected a yield of about 6 megatons. Because they had misunderstood a nuclear reaction involving one of the lithium isotopes in its fuel, it produced 15 megatons, the largest explosion the United States has ever set off. Radioactive fallout drifted over inhabited islands in the Marshall Islands and over a Japanese tuna-fishing boat, the *Daigo Fukuryū Maru* (Lucky Dragon No. 5), whose crew were covered in white radioactive ash. Its radio operator died months later. The incident caused outrage in Japan and helped inspire, among other things, the film *Godzilla* later that year.

![The Castle Bravo thermonuclear test at Bikini Atoll on 1 March 1954. Its yield of 15 megatons was more than twice what its designers had predicted.](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/bravo.jpg)
*The Castle Bravo explosion, Bikini Atoll, 1954. US Department of Energy, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The record was set in 1961, when the Soviet Union dropped the **Tsar Bomba** over the Arctic archipelago of Novaya Zemlya. It yielded about 50 megatons, more than all the explosives used in the Second World War put together, and its flash could be seen around a thousand kilometres away. It was a weapon of propaganda rather than war: too big to be practical, and deliberately scaled down from its full design yield of 100 megatons.

### The logic of madness

By the 1960s both superpowers had enough nuclear weapons, on bombers, missiles and submarines, to survive a surprise attack and still destroy the attacker. This produced a strange and terrifying stability that came to be known as **mutually assured destruction**, abbreviated, with perfect gallows humour, as MAD. Neither side could win a nuclear war, so neither side would start one. It is an argument that sounds sensible right up until the moment someone makes a mistake.

The arsenals grew to absurd sizes. The American stockpile peaked in 1967 at over 31,000 warheads. The Soviet stockpile peaked in 1986 at around 40,000. At the height of the arms race there were roughly 70,000 nuclear weapons in the world.

Ordinary people were encouraged to believe they could survive. In 1951 American schoolchildren were shown a civil-defence film starring a cartoon tortoise called Bert, who sang that when danger threatened, you should "duck and cover": dive under a desk and cover your head. As protection against a hydrogen bomb, this was of very limited value, though it would have helped against flying glass at a distance. Britain later produced a series of public information films and a booklet called *Protect and Survive* (1980), advising families to build a "fall-out room" with doors taken off their hinges and leant against an inside wall. Many people found it more frightening than reassuring.

![Bert the Turtle from the 1951 American civil-defence film Duck and Cover, which taught schoolchildren to dive under their desks if they saw the flash of an atomic bomb.](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/duckcover.png)

## Thaw and Freeze

### A dictator dies

Stalin died on 5 March 1953, after a stroke. His terrified colleagues had been so afraid to disturb him that he lay on the floor of his dacha for hours before anyone dared to call a doctor, and many of the leading doctors were in prison at the time, accused of plotting to murder Soviet leaders. After a brief and lethal power struggle, in which the secret police chief Lavrentiy Beria was arrested and shot, the leadership passed to Nikita Khrushchev, a former miner from the Ukrainian borderlands: earthy, impulsive, funny, boastful and occasionally reckless.

In February 1956, at a closed session of the Communist Party congress, Khrushchev delivered a four-hour speech denouncing Stalin's crimes: the purges, the torture, the deportations, the cult of personality. The "Secret Speech" did not stay secret for long; a copy reached the West within weeks. It shook communists all over the world, and in eastern Europe it encouraged people to believe that the regime might loosen its grip.

### Budapest, 1956

In October 1956 students in Budapest marched in support of reformers in Poland. The protests grew into a national uprising. Crowds pulled down a giant statue of Stalin, leaving only his boots on the plinth. A reforming communist, Imre Nagy, became prime minister, and Soviet troops withdrew from the city. For a few days it looked as though Hungary might win its freedom. Then Nagy announced that Hungary would leave the Warsaw Pact, the military alliance of communist states set up the previous year, and become neutral.

![Budapest in 1956: damaged buildings and debris near the Kilián barracks at the junction of Üllői út and Ferenc körút, scene of some of the fiercest fighting of the uprising.](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/budapest.jpg)
*Üllői út and Ferenc körút, Budapest, 1956. Photographer unknown, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

On 4 November Soviet tanks returned in force. Hungarian fighters, many of them teenagers armed with rifles and petrol bombs, fought them in the streets, and the radio broadcast desperate appeals for Western help. None came. The West was distracted by the Suez crisis, in which Britain, France and Israel had just invaded Egypt, and in any case no Western government was willing to risk nuclear war over Hungary. Around 2,500 Hungarians were killed and some 200,000 fled abroad. Nagy was later tried in secret and hanged in 1958. The lesson was brutally clear: the West would protest, but it would not fight, inside the Soviet sphere.

That same month Khrushchev told Western ambassadors at a reception in Moscow: "We will bury you." He later explained that he meant communism would outlast capitalism, not that the USSR would attack; it is a phrase that loses something in translation. He was, however, also given to dramatic gestures. At the United Nations in 1960, he is famously supposed to have banged his shoe on his desk during a debate. Witnesses disagree about whether he actually banged it or just waved it, and the photographs are inconclusive. Either way, the image stuck.

## The Race to Space

At 10.28 p.m. Moscow time on 4 October 1957, a Soviet R-7 rocket lifted off from a launch site on the steppe of Kazakhstan and put the world's first artificial satellite into orbit around the Earth. **Sputnik 1** was a polished metal sphere 58 centimetres across, weighing 83.6 kilograms, with four long antennae. It did very little except go round the Earth every 96 minutes, sending out a radio signal, *beep-beep-beep*, which amateur radio enthusiasts all over the world could hear.

![A replica of Sputnik 1, the world's first artificial satellite, launched by the Soviet Union on 4 October 1957.](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/sputnik.png)
*Replica of Sputnik 1. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

That was enough to cause something close to panic in the United States. If the Soviets could put a satellite in orbit, they could put a nuclear warhead on the same rocket and drop it on New York. Americans had assumed they were technologically superior; now it seemed they were behind. A month later the Soviets launched Sputnik 2, carrying a dog called Laika, a stray from the streets of Moscow, who became the first animal to orbit the Earth and died of overheating within hours. The first American attempt to launch a satellite, in December 1957, exploded on the launch pad on live television. The newspapers called it "Flopnik".

The American response was enormous. Congress created NASA in 1958, poured money into science education, and set up the agency that would later fund the ARPANET, an ancestor of the internet. But the Soviets kept winning the early laps. On 12 April 1961 Yuri Gagarin, a 27-year-old former foundry worker and fighter pilot, became the first human being in space, orbiting the Earth once in 108 minutes in his capsule *Vostok 1*. He shouted "*Poyekhali!*", meaning "Let's go!", as he lifted off, and became the most famous man in the world.

Six weeks later the new American president, John F. Kennedy, told Congress that the United States should commit itself to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to Earth before the decade was out. It was an astonishing goal, and an expensive one: the Apollo programme cost about 25 billion dollars at the time. On 20 July 1969 Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed in the Sea of Tranquillity. Hundreds of millions of people around the world watched on television. The Soviet Union, whose own Moon rocket exploded repeatedly in testing, never sent a person to the Moon, and quietly pretended it had never been trying.

The space race was, at bottom, a contest of missiles and prestige. But it also gave us weather satellites, communications satellites, satellite navigation, and the famous photograph of the Earth rising over the Moon taken by the crew of Apollo 8 at Christmas 1968, which arguably did more for environmental awareness than any speech.

## The Wall

### The leaky border

By 1961 East Germany had a problem that threatened its very existence: its people kept leaving. Since 1949, some three million East Germans, around one in six of the population, had fled to the West, and they were disproportionately young, skilled and educated: doctors, engineers, teachers. Most of them went through Berlin, where the border between the Soviet sector and the western sectors could still be crossed on foot or by underground train.

In June 1961 the East German leader, Walter Ulbricht, told a press conference that nobody had any intention of building a wall. Two months later, in the early hours of Sunday 13 August 1961, East German soldiers and police began stringing barbed wire and putting up barriers along the entire border between East and West Berlin. Within days, the wire was being replaced with concrete blocks. Families were split overnight. Some people jumped from the windows of buildings that stood right on the border, into the nets of West Berlin firemen. An East German border guard, Conrad Schumann, was photographed leaping over the barbed wire into the West in full uniform, an image that went around the world.

Over the following years the barrier grew into a fearsome system: two walls with a "death strip" between them, raked sand to show footprints, watchtowers, dog runs, trip-wire alarms and guards with orders to shoot. The East German government called it the "Anti-Fascist Protection Rampart", as if it were designed to keep Western invaders out rather than its own citizens in. The wall around West Berlin was about 155 kilometres long. At least 140 people were killed at the Berlin Wall trying to cross it or in connection with it; the last to be shot dead was Chris Gueffroy, aged twenty, in February 1989.

In October 1961 a dispute over the right of American officials to enter East Berlin without showing papers led to one of the most dangerous moments of the Cold War: American and Soviet tanks faced each other at **Checkpoint Charlie**, the famous crossing point on Friedrichstrasse, just a hundred metres or so apart, engines running, for sixteen hours. Then, after back-channel contacts between Kennedy and Khrushchev, the Soviet tanks pulled back a few metres, and the American tanks did the same, and the moment passed.

### "Ich bin ein Berliner"

Kennedy came to West Berlin on 26 June 1963 and spoke to a crowd of hundreds of thousands in front of the Schöneberg town hall. He told them that in the ancient world the proudest boast was "civis Romanus sum", and that today, in the world of freedom, the proudest boast was "Ich bin ein Berliner". The crowd roared.

![President John F. Kennedy speaking in front of Rathaus Schöneberg in West Berlin on 26 June 1963, when he told the crowd "Ich bin ein Berliner".](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/kennedy.jpg)
*Robert Knudsen, President Kennedy's address in West Berlin, 26 June 1963. White House photograph, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

There is a much-repeated story that Kennedy accidentally told the Berliners he was a jam doughnut, because a "Berliner" is a kind of doughnut and the correct way to say "I am a Berliner" is supposedly "Ich bin Berliner", without the "ein". It is a myth. The phrase Kennedy used was perfectly correct German for the meaning he intended, nobody in the crowd laughed, and in Berlin the doughnut is generally called a *Pfannkuchen* anyway.

## Thirteen Days

The closest the world has ever come to nuclear war, as far as we know, was in October 1962.

In April 1961 a CIA-backed force of Cuban exiles had landed at the Bay of Pigs in an attempt to overthrow Fidel Castro, who had taken power in Cuba in 1959 and moved steadily towards the Soviet camp. It was a fiasco: the invaders were defeated within three days, and Kennedy was humiliated. Khrushchev concluded that the young president was weak. He also had other reasons for wanting to put nuclear missiles in Cuba: to protect Castro from another invasion, and to answer the American Jupiter missiles recently placed in Turkey, right on the Soviet Union's doorstep. In the summer of 1962, in great secrecy, Soviet ships began carrying ballistic missiles, warheads and tens of thousands of troops to Cuba.

On 14 October 1962 an American U-2 spy plane photographed missile sites being built near San Cristóbal in western Cuba. When the photographs were shown to Kennedy on 16 October, the crisis began. From Cuba, medium-range missiles could reach Washington in minutes.

![A U-2 reconnaissance photograph of 14 October 1962 showing Soviet military vehicles and equipment near San Cristóbal, Cuba: the evidence that began the Cuban Missile Crisis.](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/cuba.jpg)
*U-2 reconnaissance photograph of San Cristóbal, Cuba, 14 October 1962. US Air Force, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

For a week Kennedy and a small group of advisers, known as the ExComm, debated what to do in secret. The military chiefs wanted air strikes followed by an invasion. Kennedy chose something more limited: a naval "quarantine" (the word "blockade" was avoided because a blockade is an act of war) to stop further Soviet weapons reaching Cuba, combined with a demand that the missiles already there be removed. On 22 October he announced it on television. Soviet ships carrying missiles turned back on 24 October, prompting the Secretary of State, Dean Rusk, to remark that the two sides had been eyeball to eyeball, and he thought the other fellow had just blinked.

But the missiles already in Cuba were nearly ready to fire, and the crisis was far from over. Saturday 27 October, "Black Saturday", was the most dangerous day of all. A U-2 was shot down over Cuba, killing its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson. Another U-2 strayed into Soviet airspace over Siberia, and Soviet fighters scrambled to intercept it. And in the Sargasso Sea, American destroyers dropped practice depth charges on a Soviet submarine, B-59, to force it to surface. What the Americans did not know was that B-59 carried a torpedo with a nuclear warhead. Out of contact with Moscow, sweltering in temperatures above 45 degrees Celsius, its exhausted captain believed that war might already have begun and wanted to fire it. Launching it required the agreement of the senior officers on board, and one of them, Vasily Arkhipov, the flotilla's chief of staff, refused. The submarine surfaced. We only learnt about this decades later.

That night Robert Kennedy, the president's brother, met the Soviet ambassador, Anatoly Dobrynin, in Washington. The deal they worked out was this: the Soviet Union would remove its missiles from Cuba; the United States would promise publicly not to invade Cuba; and, secretly, the United States would remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey a few months later, on condition that this part was never made public. On the morning of 28 October Radio Moscow announced that the missiles would be withdrawn.

Kennedy was hailed as the winner, because the secret part of the deal stayed secret for years. Khrushchev was quietly ousted by his colleagues two years later, his "adventurism" in Cuba being one of the charges against him. Castro, who had not been consulted, was furious.

Both leaders had been badly frightened. In 1963 they set up a direct communications link between the Kremlin and the White House, so that they could talk in a crisis. It is known in popular culture as the "red telephone", but it was actually a teleprinter link, and later a fax and computer link. The same year, Britain, the US and the USSR signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty, ending nuclear tests in the atmosphere, underwater and in space. Kennedy did not live to see much more of the Cold War: he was assassinated in Dallas in November 1963.

## The Wider War

### Fighting by proxy

Because the superpowers could not fight each other directly without risking annihilation, they fought through others, and the Cold War's real body count was overwhelmingly in Asia, Africa and Latin America.

The largest of these wars was in **Vietnam**. After the French were defeated at Dien Bien Phu in 1954, Vietnam was divided, like Korea, into a communist North under Ho Chi Minh and an anti-communist South. Successive American presidents, haunted by the "domino theory", the belief that if one country fell to communism its neighbours would topple one after another, poured in money, advisers and, from 1965, combat troops. By 1968 there were more than half a million American soldiers in Vietnam.

![A US Army soldier directs a Bell UH-1D "Huey" helicopter in to land in Vietnam in 1966. The Huey became the most recognisable symbol of the war.](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/huey.jpg)
*A US soldier directs a UH-1 Huey to land, Vietnam, 1966. US Air Force, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The war could not be won on the terms the Americans had set. The Tet Offensive of January 1968, when communist forces attacked cities across South Vietnam, was a military defeat for the North but a political catastrophe for Washington, because it showed Americans watching on television that official promises of imminent victory were hollow. Anti-war protests swept American campuses. The United States withdrew its combat forces in 1973, and Saigon fell to the North on 30 April 1975, as helicopters lifted the last Americans and some of their Vietnamese allies from the roof of a building near the embassy. About 58,000 Americans died in the war. Vietnamese deaths, military and civilian, are estimated at between one and three million, and the war spilled into Laos and Cambodia, where the Khmer Rouge regime that seized power in 1975 went on to kill something like a quarter of the population.

The Soviet Union had its own Vietnam. In December 1979 Soviet troops invaded **Afghanistan** to prop up a faltering communist government. They stayed for nearly ten years, fighting Islamist guerrillas, the *mujahideen*, who were armed and funded by the United States, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and others; from 1986 the CIA supplied them with Stinger shoulder-launched anti-aircraft missiles that took a heavy toll on Soviet helicopters. About 15,000 Soviet soldiers died, along with perhaps a million or more Afghans, and millions became refugees. Some of the fighters the West armed later turned their guns on the West. History has a way of sending the bill late.

Elsewhere, the competition was fought through coups, aid and covert action. The CIA helped overthrow governments in Iran in 1953 and Guatemala in 1954, and the United States supported the forces that brought down Chile's elected socialist president, Salvador Allende, in 1973, although the extent of its direct role in the coup itself is still debated. The Soviet Union crushed reform in Czechoslovakia in 1968, backed Marxist movements and governments in Angola, Ethiopia and elsewhere, and in Angola sent Cuban troops by the tens of thousands. Both sides supported vicious dictators as long as they were *their* vicious dictators.

Many newly independent countries tried to stay out of it altogether. At a conference in Bandung, Indonesia, in 1955, and at the founding of the **Non-Aligned Movement** in Belgrade in 1961, leaders including India's Jawaharlal Nehru, Egypt's Gamal Abdel Nasser, Indonesia's Sukarno and Yugoslavia's Josip Broz Tito declared that they would align with neither bloc. In practice many leaned one way or the other, and played the superpowers off against each other for aid. This is where the term "Third World" originally came from: not poverty, but a third option, neither the capitalist First World nor the communist Second.

### The spies

No account of the Cold War is complete without the spies, partly because they were so important and partly because they were so entertaining.

The Soviets had the early advantage. The **Cambridge Five**, a group of upper-class Englishmen recruited as students at Cambridge in the 1930s, rose to senior positions in British intelligence and diplomacy and passed secrets to Moscow for years. Two of them, Guy Burgess and Donald Maclean, fled to Moscow in 1951. The most damaging, Kim Philby, who had at one point headed the British secret service's anti-Soviet section and acted as its liaison with the CIA in Washington, was not unmasked until he defected from Beirut in 1963. The atomic spies, including Klaus Fuchs in Britain and the network that included Julius Rosenberg in America, helped the Soviet bomb programme; Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were executed in 1953. These discoveries fed the anti-communist hysteria in the United States led by Senator Joseph McCarthy, who from 1950 made wild accusations of communists in the government and destroyed many careers before the Senate censured him in 1954.

The West had its successes too. Oleg Penkovsky, a colonel in Soviet military intelligence, passed the British and Americans crucial information about Soviet missiles that helped Kennedy during the Cuban crisis; he was caught and executed in 1963. Oleg Gordievsky, a KGB officer working secretly for British intelligence, told London in the early 1980s how genuinely the Soviet leadership feared an American surprise attack. When he came under suspicion in 1985, MI6 smuggled him out of the Soviet Union in the boot of a diplomatic car, across the Finnish border and on to Norway, in an operation with the wonderfully British codename *Pimlico*. His escape signal was to stand on a Moscow street corner holding a Safeway carrier bag.

Spies were also swapped. On 10 February 1962 the American U-2 pilot Francis Gary Powers, who had been shot down over the Soviet Union in May 1960, was exchanged for the Soviet spy Rudolf Abel on the Glienicke Bridge between West Berlin and Potsdam. The bridge, which hosted several later exchanges, became known as the "Bridge of Spies".

### Culture wars

The Cold War seeped into everything. Ian Fleming published the first James Bond novel, *Casino Royale*, in 1953, and his hero spent most of the next decade fighting Soviet agents and their proxies. John le Carré, himself a former intelligence officer, wrote a much bleaker kind of spy fiction, beginning with *The Spy Who Came in from the Cold* (1963), in which the two sides are barely distinguishable morally. Stanley Kubrick's *Dr Strangelove* (1964) turned nuclear annihilation into black comedy, complete with a mad general, a doomsday machine and a cowboy pilot riding a falling bomb.

Even domestic appliances became weapons. In July 1959, at an American exhibition in Moscow, the vice-president, Richard Nixon, and Khrushchev had an impromptu argument in a model American kitchen about the merits of capitalism and communism, the dishwasher and the washing machine serving as exhibits. It became known as the **Kitchen Debate**.

Sport was war by other means. In 1972 the American chess genius Bobby Fischer defeated the Soviet world champion Boris Spassky in Reykjavik, ending decades of Soviet domination of the game, after a match full of tantrums, demands and arguments about camera noise. At the 1980 Winter Olympics in Lake Placid, a team of American college ice hockey players beat the mighty Soviet team in what Americans still call the "Miracle on Ice". The United States and many allies boycotted the 1980 Moscow Olympics over Afghanistan; the Soviet bloc retaliated by boycotting the 1984 Los Angeles Games.

## Détente and Its Discontents

By the late 1960s the two superpowers had reasons to calm things down. The Americans were bogged down in Vietnam and divided at home. The Soviets had reached rough nuclear parity and wanted Western technology and grain. And the communist world itself had split: the Soviet Union and China, once allies, had fallen out over ideology, leadership and territory, and in March 1969 their soldiers fought bloody clashes over a disputed island in the Ussuri River on their long border.

President Nixon and his national security adviser, Henry Kissinger, saw an opportunity. In February 1972 Nixon, who had built his career as a fierce anti-communist, made a historic visit to China and met Mao in Beijing. Three months later he went to Moscow and signed the first Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty, **SALT I**, and the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty with the Soviet leader, Leonid Brezhnev. This period of reduced tension became known by the French word **détente**.

![Leonid Brezhnev and Richard Nixon in talks during Brezhnev's visit to the United States in June 1973, at the height of détente.](/assets/essays/the-long-twilight-struggle/images/nixonbrezhnev.png)
*Robert L. Knudsen, Leonid Brezhnev and Richard Nixon, June 1973. White House photograph, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

In 1975 the United States, Canada, the Soviet Union and almost every country in Europe signed the **Helsinki Final Act**. Moscow got what it wanted: Western recognition of Europe's post-war borders. In return, almost as an afterthought, the Soviets agreed to respect human rights, including freedom of thought and the free movement of people. They did not intend to honour this. But dissidents across the Soviet bloc, such as the physicist Andrei Sakharov in Moscow and the playwright Václav Havel in Prague, began to hold their governments to it, and to set up groups monitoring their compliance. A clause that the Kremlin regarded as harmless window-dressing became a slow-acting poison.

Détente did not last. The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan at the end of 1979 killed it, and the 1980s brought what some historians call the "Second Cold War".

## The Most Dangerous Year

In January 1981 Ronald Reagan, a former Hollywood actor and governor of California, became president, determined to confront the Soviet Union rather than manage it. He launched an enormous military build-up. In a speech in March 1983 he called the Soviet Union an "evil empire". Two weeks later he announced the Strategic Defense Initiative, a plan to build a shield of space-based weapons to shoot down Soviet missiles, which the press promptly nicknamed "Star Wars". Most scientists thought it could not work. The Soviet leadership feared that it might, and that it was designed to let America strike first without fear of retaliation.

In Britain, the women's peace camp at Greenham Common protested against the arrival of American cruise missiles, and the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament drew huge crowds. In Moscow, the ailing Soviet leadership, now under the former KGB chief Yuri Andropov, became convinced that the United States was planning a surprise nuclear attack, and ordered the KGB to watch for signs of it: blood banks stocking up, lights burning late in government offices in London and Washington.

Then 1983 delivered a succession of near-disasters. On 1 September a Soviet fighter shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007, which had strayed into Soviet airspace. On 26 September Stanislav Petrov decided that his satellites were wrong. And in November NATO held a large command-post exercise called **Able Archer 83**, which rehearsed the procedures for releasing nuclear weapons, using new communications formats and with some senior political figures briefly involved. Some Soviet officials appear to have feared that it might be cover for a real attack, and some Soviet forces were put on higher alert. Historians still debate exactly how close this came to disaster; a later secret American review concluded that the Soviet fears had been real and that the United States might have inadvertently put its relationship with the USSR "on a hair trigger". What is clear is that it was Gordievsky's reports to London about the Soviet panic that helped persuade Margaret Thatcher and, through her, Reagan, that their rhetoric was frightening Moscow more than they had realised.

That same November, an American television film called *The Day After*, about the effects of a nuclear attack on a town in Kansas, was watched by an audience estimated at around 100 million. Reagan saw it in advance and wrote in his diary that it left him greatly depressed. Something was shifting.

## The End

### Gorbachev

Soviet leaders kept dying. Brezhnev died in November 1982; Andropov in February 1984; his successor, Konstantin Chernenko, in March 1985. ("How can I make peace with them," Reagan is reported to have asked, "if they keep dying on me?") The man chosen next was different. Mikhail Gorbachev was 54, energetic, educated, and fully aware that the Soviet economy was stagnating, that the war in Afghanistan was unwinnable, and that the arms race was bankrupting the country.

Gorbachev introduced two words the world quickly learnt: ***glasnost***, openness, allowing more freedom of speech and information, and ***perestroika***, restructuring of the economy and later of the political system. The catastrophe at the Chernobyl nuclear power station in Ukraine in April 1986, which the authorities at first tried to hide, convinced him further that the system of secrecy was itself a danger.

He also reached out to the West. Reagan and Gorbachev met in Geneva in 1985, and in October 1986, at Höfði House in Reykjavik, they came astonishingly close to agreeing to abolish all their nuclear weapons, before the talks collapsed over Reagan's refusal to give up his space defence programme. But the personal trust was there. In December 1987 they signed the **Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty**, which eliminated an entire category of nuclear missiles for the first time.

### 1989

The decisive change came when Gorbachev made it clear that the Soviet Union would no longer use force to keep its eastern European satellites in line. A Soviet spokesman jokingly called this the "Sinatra Doctrine": they could do it their way.

They did. In Poland, the independent trade union Solidarity, founded in the Gdańsk shipyards in 1980 and later banned, was legalised again, and in June 1989 it won almost every seat it was allowed to contest in semi-free elections. Hungary began dismantling its border fence with Austria, and on 19 August 1989, at a "Pan-European Picnic" on the border, some 600 East Germans walked through an opened gate into the West. Thousands more followed through Hungary and Czechoslovakia. In Leipzig, peaceful Monday-night demonstrations grew from a few thousand to tens of thousands; on 9 October some 70,000 people marched chanting "*Wir sind das Volk*", "We are the people", and the security forces, to the surprise of many, did not shoot.

The Berlin Wall fell almost by accident. On the evening of 9 November 1989, Günter Schabowski, an East German Politburo member, gave a rambling press conference about a new, slightly relaxed travel regulation. He had not been properly briefed. When a journalist asked when it came into effect, he shuffled his papers and said that, as far as he knew, it applied immediately, without delay. Television broadcast the news. That night, thousands of East Berliners gathered at the checkpoints demanding to cross. At Bornholmer Strasse, around 11.30 p.m., the officer in charge, Harald Jäger, with no orders and a crowd growing by the minute, gave up and opened the gate. Within hours, Berliners were standing on the Wall at the Brandenburg Gate, chipping at it with hammers, while strangers hugged and wept and handed each other bottles of sparkling wine.

Let us imagine the scene at Bornholmer Strasse. The details are true; the words are invented.

> **A woman in the crowd:** Schabowski said it on the television! Immediately, he said. Open the gate!
>
> **A border guard:** We have no orders. Nobody has told us anything.
>
> **The woman:** Then ring someone!
>
> **The guard:** We have rung everyone. Nobody knows anything.
>
> **The woman:** Then what are you waiting for?

The rest of the bloc fell like the dominoes the Americans had once feared, but in the opposite direction. Czechoslovakia's peaceful "Velvet Revolution" made the dissident playwright Václav Havel president by the end of the year. Bulgaria's leader was pushed out. In Romania, the dictator Nicolae Ceaușescu was booed by a crowd he had summoned to cheer him, fled Bucharest by helicopter, and was captured, tried and shot with his wife on Christmas Day 1989. Germany was reunified on 3 October 1990.

### The last day of the Soviet Union

Gorbachev had hoped to reform the Soviet Union, not dissolve it. But glasnost had released long-suppressed nationalisms in the Baltic states, Ukraine, the Caucasus and elsewhere, and perestroika had wrecked the old planned economy without building a new one. In August 1991, hardliners in his own government put Gorbachev under house arrest at his holiday home in Crimea and tried to seize power. The coup collapsed within three days, after crowds gathered to defend the Russian parliament in Moscow and its president, Boris Yeltsin, climbed onto a tank to denounce the plotters. But Gorbachev's authority never recovered. One by one the republics declared independence.

On 25 December 1991 Gorbachev resigned as president of the Soviet Union in a televised address. That evening the red flag with the hammer and sickle was lowered over the Kremlin for the last time, and the Russian tricolour was raised in its place. The next day, the Soviet Union formally ceased to exist. The Cold War was over, and almost nobody had predicted that it would end like that: not with a bang, nor even much of a whimper, but with a resignation speech on Christmas Day.

## Why Did It Stay Cold?

There are two great questions about the Cold War. The first is why it never became a full-scale war between the superpowers.

Part of the answer is nuclear deterrence: the bomb made the stakes so high that both sides became, in a crisis, very cautious indeed. Part of it is that both sides learnt, the hard way, to set up rules and channels: hotlines, arms-control treaties, tacit understandings about each other's spheres of influence. Part of it is that the leaders, however much they hated each other's systems, were not suicidal. And part of it, frankly, is luck: a Soviet submarine officer who said no in 1962, a lieutenant colonel who trusted his gut in 1983, and a number of other close calls that we probably still do not know about.

The second question is why the West won. The fundamental answer most historians give is economic: the Soviet planned economy could build rockets and tanks but could not keep up with the consumer economies of the West, and by the 1980s it was stagnating, while the information revolution was passing it by entirely. Reagan's admirers credit his military build-up with forcing the issue; his critics argue that the Soviet system was collapsing from within anyway and that his rhetoric made the world more dangerous. Many give the largest share of credit to Gorbachev, who chose not to use force to save the empire, a choice his predecessors would not have made, and for which he is admired abroad and widely resented in Russia. The dissidents, the workers of Gdańsk, the crowds in Leipzig and Prague: they mattered too. Empires are not only defeated; they are also abandoned by the people who are supposed to live in them.

## Living in the Afterglow

In 1989 the American political scientist Francis Fukuyama published an essay asking whether we had reached "the end of history", meaning that liberal democracy had won the battle of ideas for good. For a decade or so, it looked that way to many people. It doesn't now. But the Cold War's legacies are everywhere.

**The map of Europe and its alliances.** NATO, founded to deter the Soviet Union, did not dissolve when the Soviet Union did. It expanded eastwards, taking in most of the former Warsaw Pact and the Baltic states, and more recently Finland and Sweden. Its supporters see this as the free choice of nations who had been occupied and wanted protection. Russia's leaders, above all Vladimir Putin, a former KGB officer who was stationed in Dresden when the Wall fell and who has called the collapse of the Soviet Union a great geopolitical catastrophe, see it as betrayal and encirclement. That argument runs straight through Russia's wars in Ukraine since 2014.

**The bombs.** There are still roughly 12,000 nuclear warheads in the world, the great majority belonging to the United States and Russia. That is far fewer than the 70,000 of the mid-1980s, but more than enough. Several of the arms-control treaties that ended the Cold War, including the INF Treaty, have since collapsed.

**Unfinished business.** Korea is still divided along the line where the armistice was signed in 1953, and North Korea now has nuclear weapons of its own. Cuba is still governed by the Communist Party and still subject to an American embargo. Taiwan's status, a legacy of China's civil war and the Cold War that froze it, is one of the most dangerous questions in world politics.

**Technology.** The Cold War built much of the modern technological world. The space race gave us satellites for weather, communications and television; the American military's Global Positioning System, designed to guide weapons and troops, now guides delivery drivers and hikers. The ARPANET, funded by the Pentagon's research agency and switched on in 1969, grew into the internet. (The popular story that it was built to survive a nuclear war is an exaggeration, though its designers were influenced by research into survivable communications networks.) Much of the computing industry grew up on defence contracts.

**The words.** We still talk about iron curtains, superpowers, proxy wars, brinkmanship, defectors and the domino effect. And the phrase "a new Cold War" is now used routinely about the rivalry between the United States and China, and about the West's relationship with Russia. Whether the analogy is useful is hotly debated: China is far more deeply woven into the world economy than the Soviet Union ever was. But the fact that the phrase reaches so easily for our tongues says something about how deep the original went.

## The Twilight Struggle

In his inaugural address in January 1961, John F. Kennedy described what his generation faced as "a long twilight struggle", year in and year out. It is a good phrase for it. The Cold War was not a war of great battles and clear victories, but a long contest of nerves, ideas, economies and accidents, conducted in a kind of permanent dusk, with the knowledge always present that the lights could go out for good at any moment.

That they did not is the most important fact about it. Somewhere in a museum there should be a monument to all the people who, at the crucial moment, did not do the dramatic thing: Vasily Arkhipov in his overheated submarine, Stanislav Petrov in his bunker, Harald Jäger at his gate, and the soldiers in Leipzig who kept their fingers off their triggers. History mostly remembers the people who acted. The Cold War is a reminder to remember, too, the people who held back.
