## A Language With a Lot of Explaining to Do

Try reading this sentence aloud: *Though the tough cough and hiccough plough me through, I ought to bough to the dough.*

If you are a native speaker of English, you probably managed it, apart from that slightly suspicious "bough", which is a branch rather than a bow, and the "hiccough", which is just an old spelling of "hiccup". If you learnt English as a second language, you may have hurled this essay across the room. The letters "ough" have at least seven different pronunciations in that sentence: "oh", "uff", "off", "up", "ow", "oo" and "or". There is no rule that tells you which is which. You just have to know.

There is a well-known joke that the English word "fish" could be spelt *ghoti*: "gh" as in "enough", "o" as in "women" and "ti" as in "nation". It is often attributed to George Bernard Shaw, who campaigned for spelling reform, but the earliest known example is in a letter of 1855, written the year before Shaw was born. It is, of course, a cheat, since English never uses "gh" for an "f" sound at the start of a word. But it makes a point that every learner of English already knows: the spelling of this language is a mess.

So is much else about it. Why do we say "one mouse, two mice" but "one house, two houses"? Why is the past tense of "go" "went", a word that has nothing to do with "go" at all? Why do we have "kingly", "royal" and "regal", three words for the same thing? Why do we eat "beef" but keep "cows"? Why is there a silent "b" in "debt" and a silent "s" in "island"? Why do Americans write "color" while the British write "colour", when they agree on "error"?

Every one of these oddities has a story behind it, and together they add up to one of the strangest and most entertaining histories of any language on Earth. English is what happens when a small Germanic dialect is carried to an island, battered by Viking invaders, conquered by French-speaking Normans, flooded with Latin and Greek by Renaissance scholars, frozen in print halfway through a dramatic change in its pronunciation, standardised by a few stubborn dictionary-makers, and then exported to every corner of the planet, where it picked up words from everyone it met. Its peculiarities are fossils. Each one records something that happened to the people who spoke it.

Let's go and dig them up.

## Before English

For most of human history, nobody on the island of Britain spoke English. When Julius Caesar raided Britain in 55 and 54 BC, and when the Romans conquered most of it from AD 43, the island's people spoke Celtic languages, the ancestors of modern Welsh, Cornish and Breton. The Romans brought Latin, which was spoken by officials, soldiers and the urban elite, though most Britons probably continued to speak their Celtic tongues.

The Celts left surprisingly few words in English: a handful of everyday ones such as "brock", an old word for a badger, and "crag", and a great many place names. Many British rivers carry Celtic names: the Thames, the Severn, the Trent. The several rivers called Avon have a name that simply means "river", from the same root as the modern Welsh *afon*, so "River Avon" means "River River". The Romans left their mark mostly on places too. Their word *castra*, a military camp, survives in every town ending in -chester, -caster or -cester: Manchester, Lancaster, Gloucester, Worcester.

When Roman rule collapsed in the early fifth century, Britain was left to fend for itself. And then the English arrived.

## The Anglo-Saxons: Where English Begins

### Angles, Saxons and Jutes

In the fifth and sixth centuries, groups of settlers crossed the North Sea from what is now northern Germany, Denmark and the Netherlands. According to the monk Bede, who wrote his *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* in 731, they were three peoples: the Angles, from the region of Angeln at the base of the Jutland peninsula; the Saxons, from northern Germany; and the Jutes, from Jutland. Modern historians think the reality was messier, involving many groups and a mixture of conquest, migration and intermarriage over a long period, but Bede's tidy picture has stuck.

The settlers spoke closely related West Germanic dialects, and from those dialects grew the language we now call **Old English**. They called it *Englisc*, after the Angles, and over time the land itself became *Englaland*, the land of the Angles. Their nearest linguistic relatives were the Frisians, who still live along the North Sea coast of the Netherlands and Germany. Frisian is, broadly speaking, the closest living relative of English, and there is an old rhyme in Frisian whose words sound startlingly like English: "*Bûter, brea en griene tsiis*", "butter, bread and green cheese".

The Celtic-speaking Britons were pushed west and north, or absorbed. The Anglo-Saxon word for them was *wealas*, meaning foreigners or strangers, which is where the word "Welsh" comes from. There is a certain irony in the newcomers calling the natives foreigners in their own land, but history is full of that sort of thing.

### What Old English looked like

If you could travel back to Anglo-Saxon England, you would not understand a word anyone said. Old English is as foreign to a modern English speaker as German or Dutch, and in some ways more so. Here is the beginning of the Lord's Prayer as an Anglo-Saxon would have said it:

> *Fæder ure þu þe eart on heofonum, si þin nama gehalgod.*

That means "Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name." You can just about pick out *Fæder* (father), *ure* (our), *heofonum* (heaven) and *nama* (name). The odd letters are part of the fun. Old English used several letters that modern English has lost:

- **þ**, called *thorn*, and **ð**, called *eth*, both used for the "th" sound;
- **æ**, called *ash*, a vowel between "a" and "e", like the vowel in modern "cat";
- **ƿ**, called *wynn*, used for "w".

Thorn survived into the age of printing, when it was often written in a way that looked very like a "y". That is why fake-old pub signs say "Ye Olde Tea Shoppe". The "Ye" was always pronounced "the". Nobody in medieval England ever said "yee olde" anything.

Old English grammar was much more complicated than modern English. Nouns had three grammatical genders: masculine, feminine and neuter. These did not necessarily follow biological sex: the word for "woman" or "wife", *wif*, was neuter, while *wifmann*, the compound from which our word "woman" comes, was masculine, because its last element, *mann*, was. Nouns, adjectives and pronouns also changed their endings according to their role in the sentence, as in modern German or Latin. "The stone" might be *se stan*, *þone stan*, *þæs stanes* or *þæm stane*, depending on what it was doing. Because the endings showed who was doing what to whom, word order was fairly free.

Modern English has lost almost all of this. Our nouns have kept only two endings: the plural "-s" and the possessive "-'s". Our pronouns have held on to a few more forms (I/me/my, he/him/his, who/whom, though "whom" is now gasping for breath). How English shed so much of its grammar is one of the great puzzles of its history, and we will come to some of the suspects shortly.

### The poetry of the word-hoard

What Old English lacked in simplicity it made up for in poetry. Its greatest surviving work is *Beowulf*, an epic of more than 3,000 lines about a hero who fights a monster called Grendel, Grendel's even more alarming mother, and finally a dragon. It survives in a single manuscript, copied around the year 1000, which was damaged in a fire at the library of Sir Robert Cotton in 1731 and was very nearly lost forever. It begins:

> *Hwæt! We Gardena in geardagum, þeodcyninga, þrym gefrunon...*

"Listen! We have heard of the glory of the Spear-Danes, of the kings of the people, in days of yore..." That opening *Hwæt*, the ancestor of "what", is a call for attention, something like "Listen!" or, as the poet Seamus Heaney rendered it in his celebrated 1999 translation, "So."

Old English poetry did not rhyme. It alliterated, with each line built around words beginning with the same sound, and it loved **kennings**: compressed metaphors that describe something by what it does or is like. The sea was the *hronrad*, the whale-road. The body was the *banhus*, the bone-house. A sword was a *beadoleoma*, a battle-light. The Anglo-Saxon poet's vocabulary was called his *wordhord*, his word-hoard, which is still a rather lovely way to think about a language.

### The monks bring Latin

In 597 a monk called Augustine arrived in Kent, sent by Pope Gregory the Great to convert the Anglo-Saxons to Christianity. The conversion brought monasteries, books and the Roman alphabet, and with them a wave of Latin words for church matters: altar, candle, priest, monk, nun, school, verse, and dozens more.

The monks also wrote English down. The earliest English poem whose author we can name, a short hymn of praise to God, is attributed by Bede to Cædmon, a cowherd at the abbey of Whitby who, according to the story, could not sing until he was visited by an angel in a dream. And in the tenth century a priest called Aldred added a word-by-word Old English translation between the lines of the Latin text of the magnificent Lindisfarne Gospels. It is the oldest surviving version of the Gospels in any form of English.

## The Vikings: They, Them and Their Eggs

### Raiders from the north

On 8 June 793 (the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle gives the date, though some historians prefer January), raiders from Scandinavia attacked the monastery on Lindisfarne, the very place where those Gospels had been made. They killed monks, stole the treasure and sailed away. The scholar Alcuin of York, writing from the court of Charlemagne, said that never before had such a terror appeared in Britain. The Viking Age had begun.

For decades the Vikings raided. Then, from 865, a large Danish force, which the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle called the *micel here*, the Great Heathen Army, came to conquer and stay. It overran the kingdoms of Northumbria, East Anglia and most of Mercia. Only Wessex, in the south and west, held out, under its young king Alfred.

Alfred is the only English monarch called "the Great", and he earned it. After being driven into hiding in the Somerset marshes, where he is supposed (on no good evidence) to have been scolded by a peasant woman for letting her cakes burn, he rallied his forces and defeated the Danish leader Guthrum at the Battle of Edington in 878. The two men agreed to divide England between them. The north and east, roughly everything beyond a line from London to Chester, became the **Danelaw**, where Danish law and custom held sway.

![A silver penny of Alfred the Great, king of Wessex from 871 to 899, who held off the Vikings and championed the use of English.](/assets/essays/how-english-got-so-strange/images/alfredpenny.jpg)
*Silver offering penny of Alfred the Great (871-899). Photo: PHGCOM, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Alfred was also a champion of the English language. Lamenting that hardly anyone in England could read Latin any more, he arranged for important books to be translated into English, translated some himself, and is associated with the beginning of the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, a year-by-year history of England written in English. In an age when serious writing across most of Europe was done in Latin, this was unusual. It is one reason why English has such a long written history.

### Speaking Norse in Yorkshire

The Scandinavian settlers in the Danelaw spoke Old Norse, the ancestor of modern Norwegian, Danish, Swedish, Icelandic and Faroese. Old Norse and Old English were related languages, both descended from the same Germanic ancestor, and speakers of the two could probably understand each other to some extent, rather as Norwegians and Swedes can today, especially when talking about simple things such as cattle, weather and prices.

Let us imagine a conversation in a market in Jórvík, the Viking name for York, in about 950. The scene is invented; the words in italics are the real forms of the time, more or less.

> **An English farmer:** How much for the knife?
>
> **A Norse trader:** Two of your pennies. It is a good *knífr*. Look at the edge.
>
> **Farmer:** Two pennies? For that I could buy a *cu*.
>
> **Trader:** A *kýr* is worth far more, my friend. And you cannot cut your bread with a cow.
>
> **Farmer:** My wife says the sky looks like rain. She wants to be home before it comes.
>
> **Trader:** *Ský*? That is cloud, not sky. You English have the strangest words.

That last line contains a small joke. The English word "sky" is borrowed from Old Norse *ský*, which meant "cloud", and in modern Norwegian *sky* still means "cloud". The Anglo-Saxons had their own word for the heavens, *heofon*, which gave us "heaven". Over time, the Norse word took over the everyday meaning and "heaven" was left to God and poets.

### What the Vikings gave us

The Norse influence on English was not just a matter of a few exotic words for longships. It went right down into the basic, everyday core of the language, which is very unusual. A language normally borrows words for new things: foods, inventions, ideas. It rarely borrows words for things it already has names for. English borrowed Norse words for all sorts of ordinary things, which tells us that English and Norse speakers were living side by side, marrying each other and raising children who grew up with both.

Among the words English took from Old Norse are: *sky, skin, skull, skirt, egg, knife, window, husband, law, leg, wing, ugly, anger, happy, cake, call, take, get, give, die* and *want*. "Window" is a particularly beautiful one: it comes from the Norse *vindauga*, "wind-eye", a hole in the wall that let the wind in. Many words beginning with "sk-" or "sc-" pronounced "sk" are Norse, and sometimes the Norse and English versions of the same original word survived side by side with different meanings. "Shirt" is the English version and "skirt" the Norse; both originally meant a short garment.

Most astonishingly, English borrowed some of its **pronouns** from Norse. The Old English words for "they", "them" and "their" were *hie*, *him* and *hiera*, which were confusingly similar to the words for "he", "him" and "her". Over the Middle English period, they were replaced by the Norse forms *they*, *them* and *their*, spreading slowly from the north and east down towards London. Languages almost never borrow pronouns. That English did is a sign of how deeply the two peoples were intertwined.

The Vikings also left a map. Hundreds of English place names end in Norse elements: *-by*, meaning a farm or village (Derby, Whitby, Grimsby, Selby); *-thorpe*, a smaller settlement (Scunthorpe); *-thwaite*, a clearing (Braithwaite); and *-toft*, a homestead (Lowestoft). Plot them on a map and they trace the boundaries of the Danelaw with remarkable accuracy.

Some linguists also believe that the mixing of Old English and Old Norse helps explain why English lost so many of its grammatical endings. The two languages shared many word roots but used different endings, so when English and Norse speakers talked to each other, the endings were the bits that caused confusion and could be dropped without losing too much meaning. The north and east, where the Danelaw lay, were indeed where English grammar simplified first. It is an attractive theory, though scholars still debate how much weight it can bear.

## 1066 and All That: The Normans Arrive

### A French-speaking England

On 14 October 1066, near the town now called Battle in Sussex, the army of Duke William of Normandy defeated the English under King Harold Godwinson. Harold was killed, possibly by an arrow in the eye, as the famous Bayeux Tapestry appears to show, although historians argue about whether the figure with the arrow is Harold at all, and whether the arrow was part of the original embroidery or added by later restorers. William was crowned on Christmas Day in Westminster Abbey. He became known, with good reason, as William the Conqueror.

![The death of King Harold at the Battle of Hastings in 1066, from the Bayeux Tapestry. The Latin above reads "Harold rex interfectus est", "King Harold is killed".](/assets/essays/how-english-got-so-strange/images/bayeux.jpg)
*The Bayeux Tapestry, scene 57: the death of Harold. Photo: Myrabella, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The Normans were descendants of Vikings who had settled in northern France in the early tenth century, but within a few generations they had become thoroughly French in language and culture. After 1066 the English aristocracy was almost entirely replaced by a Norman one. Within twenty years, nearly every great landowner, bishop and abbot in England was a Norman or another Frenchman. The language of the king's court, of the nobility, of the law courts and, increasingly, of literature was **Anglo-Norman French**. The language of the Church and of learning remained Latin. English became, for a while, the language of the people who did the actual work: peasants, servants, craftsmen.

This had two great effects. The first was on vocabulary. Over the next three centuries, around 10,000 French words entered English, and about three-quarters of them are still in use. The second was on English itself. Because it was no longer the language of government and literature, English had nobody policing its grammar and spelling. It was left to evolve freely in the mouths of ordinary people, and it changed faster in the two centuries after the Conquest than in any period before or since.

### Beef, pork and mutton

The French words that came in tell us very clearly who was in charge. Words connected with power, government and law are overwhelmingly French: *crown, throne, sovereign, government, parliament, state, court, judge, jury, justice, attorney, prison, crime, sentence, property, estate*. So are words connected with war (*army, battle, soldier, enemy*), with the Church hierarchy (*religion, sermon, prayer*), with fashion (*dress, fashion, embroidery*) and with fine living (*dinner, supper, feast, banquet*).

The most famous example is meat. The animals in the fields kept their English names, because the English peasants looked after them: *cow, ox, pig, swine, sheep, calf, deer*. The meat that appeared on the table of the Norman lord got French names: *beef* (from *boeuf*), *pork* (*porc*), *mutton* (*mouton*), *veal* (*veau*), *venison* (*venaison*). The contrast was made famous by Sir Walter Scott, in his novel *Ivanhoe* (1819), where the jester Wamba explains to the Saxon swineherd Gurth that the swine is a Saxon while it needs tending, but becomes a Norman called pork when it reaches the castle hall. Linguists point out that the reality was a bit more complicated, since the French words could at first mean either the animal or the meat. But the pattern is real, and it is a little social history lesson on every menu.

The result of all this borrowing is that English has, for many ideas, two or even three words, one from each of its main sources, and each with a slightly different flavour. The Old English word is usually the plainest and homeliest, the French word a little more refined, and the Latin word, borrowed later, the most formal or technical:

| Old English | French | Latin |
|---|---|---|
| kingly | royal | regal |
| ask | question | interrogate |
| rise | mount | ascend |
| fire | flame | conflagration |
| holy | sacred | consecrated |
| fear | terror | trepidation |

This is one of the great glories of English. It gives writers a choice of register for almost anything they want to say. "Help!" is a cry; "aid" is what a government sends; "assistance" is what a call centre offers you. The difference between "a hearty welcome" and "a cordial reception" is the difference between Saxon and Norman.

### How the scribes rearranged the spelling

The Norman scribes also left their fingerprints on English spelling, and some of the oddities that puzzle learners today are their work. They were trained in French spelling habits, and they applied them to English words.

- Old English *cw* became **qu**, as in French: *cwen* became *queen*, *cwic* (alive) became *quick*.
- The Old English sound written *h* in the middle of words such as *niht* and *liht*, a throaty sound like the "ch" in Scottish "loch", came to be written **gh**: *night*, *light*, *through*, *daughter*. That sound later vanished from most English pronunciation, but the "gh" stayed on the page like a ghost.
- The vowel "u" was often written **o** next to letters made of short vertical strokes (*m*, *n*, *u*, *v*), because in medieval handwriting a run of such strokes was almost impossible to read. So Old English *sunu* became *son*, *cuman* became *come*, *lufu* became *love*, and *munuc* became *monk*. That is why "come", "some", "love", "son" and "money" are spelt with an "o" but pronounced with a "u" sound.
- The long "oo" sound in words such as *hus* (house) and *mus* (mouse) was written **ou**, as in French.

### English bounces back

French did not, in the end, replace English. The Normans were always a small minority, perhaps a few per cent of the population, and their children, raised by English nurses and surrounded by English servants, grew up speaking English. In 1204 King John lost Normandy to the French crown, and the English aristocracy gradually came to see itself as English rather than Norman. The long wars with France in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries made French the language of the enemy.

Then came the Black Death of 1348-49, which killed perhaps a third or more of England's population and made the surviving labourers, almost all English speakers, more valuable and more assertive. In 1362 Parliament passed the Statute of Pleading, which said that cases in the law courts should be pleaded in English, because French was "much unknown" in the realm. The statute itself, with fine irony, was written in French. Henry IV, who seized the throne in 1399, is often said to have been the first king since the Conquest whose mother tongue was English. Lawyers, being lawyers, kept using a peculiar fossilised "Law French" for certain legal purposes until a statute of 1731 finally required English in legal proceedings. That is why English law is still full of French: *plaintiff*, *defendant*, *mortgage*, *bailiff*, and the word *oyez*, "hear ye", still occasionally shouted to open a court.

## Middle English: Chaucer's Pilgrims

### Whan that Aprill

The English that emerged from all this is called **Middle English**, spoken from roughly 1150 to 1500. It had lost most of the old grammatical endings and genders, absorbed thousands of French words, and begun to look recognisably like the language we speak. Its greatest writer was Geoffrey Chaucer, a London wine merchant's son who became a courtier, diplomat, customs official and, in his spare time, the finest poet of the age. In the last years of his life, before his death in 1400, he worked on *The Canterbury Tales*, a collection of stories told by a group of pilgrims on their way from Southwark to the shrine of St Thomas Becket at Canterbury. It begins:

> *Whan that Aprill with his shoures soote*
> *The droghte of March hath perced to the roote...*

"When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root." With a little help, a modern reader can follow most of it. It is in many ways the first great literary work that feels as though it belongs to us.

![Geoffrey Chaucer on horseback as one of his own Canterbury pilgrims, from the Ellesmere manuscript of The Canterbury Tales, made shortly after his death in 1400.](/assets/essays/how-english-got-so-strange/images/chaucer.jpg)
*Portrait of Chaucer in the Ellesmere manuscript of The Canterbury Tales (c. 1400-1410), Huntington Library, folio 153v. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

But it is not quite our English. Chaucer's spelling was more phonetic than ours, because it was written before the great changes in pronunciation that were still to come. The "e" at the end of words such as *soote* and *roote* was usually pronounced, and almost every letter was sounded. *Knight* was pronounced something like "k-nikht", with the "k" and the throaty "gh" both audible.

### A country of dialects

There was no single correct Middle English. People in different parts of the country spoke and wrote very differently, and a Londoner could struggle to understand a Yorkshireman. Chaucer himself made fun of northern speech in one of his tales, in which two Cambridge students from the north speak with exaggerated northern forms. A writer in Kent might spell a word quite differently from one in Lancashire, and each was correct by his own lights.

Gradually, though, the English of London and the south-east Midlands, the region of the capital, the court and the universities of Oxford and Cambridge, became the prestige form. From around the 1430s, the clerks of the royal Chancery, the government's main writing office, began to use a fairly consistent form of written English in official documents. This so-called **Chancery Standard** was not quite the origin of modern written English, as scholars once believed, but it was an important step towards a standard spelling that everyone, wherever they lived, would eventually be expected to use.

## The Printing Press and the Shifting Vowels

### Caxton and the eggs

In 1476 William Caxton, a Kentish merchant who had spent many years in Bruges and learnt the new art of printing in Cologne, set up the first printing press in England, near Westminster Abbey. Among the first books he printed were Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* and Thomas Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur*. Printing changed everything about English writing, but it confronted Caxton with an immediate and practical problem: which English should he print?

![Daniel Maclise's Victorian imagining of William Caxton showing the first specimen of his printing to King Edward IV and Queen Elizabeth Woodville at Westminster.](/assets/essays/how-english-got-so-strange/images/caxton.jpg)
*Daniel Maclise, Caxton Showing the First Specimen of His Printing to King Edward IV at the Almonry, Westminster (1851). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

He told a wonderful story about this in the preface to a book he printed in 1490. Some merchants sailing down the Thames were becalmed in Kent and went ashore to look for food. One of them, a man called Sheffield, asked a woman at a house for some *egges*. She replied that she could not speak French. The merchant was offended, because he could not speak French either. Then another of his party asked for *eyren*, and the good woman said she understood him well. The northern word *egges* came from Old Norse; the southern *eyren* was the old English plural of *ey*. "Loo," wrote Caxton, "what sholde a man in thyse dayes now wryte, egges or eyren?" It is hard to please every man, he concluded, because of the diversity and change of language. (*Egges* won.)

Caxton and the printers who followed him made decisions, often inconsistently, that gradually fixed English spelling. Some of their choices added new oddities. Caxton's compositors were often Flemish, and they are traditionally blamed for the "h" in **ghost**, which in Middle English was *gost*; Flemish has *gheest*. The habit of spelling a word however you liked, which had been normal for centuries, slowly came to look like ignorance.

### The great vowel shift

And here is the crucial part. Spelling began to settle in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, at precisely the moment when the pronunciation of English was going through the most dramatic change in its history.

Between about 1400 and 1700, the long vowels of English moved. All of them. In a chain reaction that no one has ever fully explained, each long vowel shifted to a higher position in the mouth, and the highest ones, which had nowhere higher to go, became diphthongs: two vowels glided together. The Danish linguist Otto Jespersen gave it its name: the **Great Vowel Shift**.

Here is roughly what happened, with approximate Middle English pronunciations:

- *bite* was pronounced "beet-uh", and became "bite";
- *meet* was pronounced like modern "mate", and became "meet";
- *name* was pronounced "nah-muh", and became "name";
- *house* was pronounced "hoose", and became "house";
- *boot* was pronounced like modern "boat", and became "boot".

Nobody knows exactly why it happened. Theories include the social mobility that followed the Black Death, as people from different regions with different accents crowded into London; a desire among the up-and-coming middle classes to distance their speech from the French-tinged speech of the old elite, or alternatively to imitate it; or simply the internal dynamics of a sound system, where one vowel's move pushes another out of its place like a game of musical chairs. It did not affect all dialects equally. In parts of Scotland and the north of England, "house" is still pronounced something like "hoose", which is closer to the original.

The consequences for spelling were enormous. Printers fixed the spellings of many words while their vowels were still on the move, or even before they had begun, so English spelling largely records how words were pronounced in the late Middle Ages, not how they are pronounced now. That is why English vowel letters have such strange values compared with almost every other European language. An Italian, Spaniard, German or Norwegian reading "name" would naturally say "nah-meh", which is close to how Chaucer said it. It is not they who are wrong. It is English that moved.

Combine the Great Vowel Shift with the Norman scribes' habits, the loss of sounds such as the "k" in *knight* and the "gh" in *night*, and the Flemish printers' tinkering, and you begin to see why "though", "through", "tough" and "cough" look alike but sound so different. They used to sound much more alike. Then English pronunciation kept changing, while the spelling stood still.

## The Renaissance: Inkhorns and Silent Letters

### A flood of Latin and Greek

In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the Renaissance reached England, and with it a passion for the classical languages. English scholars wanted to write about science, philosophy, medicine and theology in their own language, but they felt English lacked the words. So they borrowed them, by the thousand, from Latin and Greek.

Many of these borrowings are now so ordinary we cannot imagine English without them: *atmosphere, capsule, education, encyclopedia, exist, explain, expensive, fact, habitual, harass, industrial, meditate, skeleton, system, thermometer*. Others sank without trace. The age produced *anacephalize* (to sum up), *deruncinate* (to weed) and *obstupefact* (stupefied), which are now known only to people who enjoy reading old dictionaries.

Not everyone approved. Critics called the new words "inkhorn terms", after the horn pots that scholars kept their ink in, and mocked writers who larded their prose with Latin. The scholar Thomas Wilson, in *The Arte of Rhetorique* (1553), complained that some people wrote such fancy English that their own mothers could not tell what they said. Others tried to revive old English words, or coin new ones from English roots, instead. The argument between plain English and fancy English has never really ended; it continues in every office where someone has written "utilise" instead of "use".

### The silent letters

Renaissance scholars also did something odder: they changed the spelling of words that English had already borrowed from French, to make them look more like their Latin ancestors. This is the source of many of our silent letters.

The word *dette*, borrowed from French, acquired a "b" to make it look like Latin *debitum*, and became **debt**. *Doute* became **doubt**, after Latin *dubitare*. *Receite* became **receipt**, after *recepta*. *Samon* became **salmon**, after *salmo*. Nobody ever pronounced these new letters; they were there purely to show off the word's classical pedigree.

Sometimes the scholars got the pedigree wrong. The Old English word for an island was *iegland*, which became *iland* in Middle English. It had nothing to do with Latin. But scholars assumed it was related to the French *isle*, from Latin *insula*, and stuck in an "s". So **island** has a silent "s" that was never there in the first place, thanks to a mistake about its family tree. Similarly, *scissors* was *sisoures* in Middle English, from French; the "c" was added by scholars who wrongly linked it to Latin *scindere*, to cut.

## The Book and the Bard

### Tyndale's Bible

If one person deserves credit for the sound of modern English prose, it may be a man few people have heard of: William Tyndale. A brilliant scholar from Gloucestershire, Tyndale was determined to translate the Bible into English directly from the original Hebrew and Greek, so that, as he reportedly told a clergyman, a boy who drove the plough would know more of the Scriptures than the clergyman did. This was illegal in England, so he worked in exile in Germany and the Low Countries, and his New Testament, printed in Worms in 1526, had to be smuggled into England in bales of cloth. Copies were seized and burnt.

![The first page of the Gospel of St John in William Tyndale's English New Testament of 1526, printed in Worms by Peter Schöffer.](/assets/essays/how-english-got-so-strange/images/tyndale.jpg)
*The opening of the Gospel of John in Tyndale's New Testament (Worms, 1526). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Tyndale was betrayed, arrested near Antwerp, and in 1536 strangled and burnt at the stake for heresy. But his words lived on. He wrote in a plain, rhythmic English that was meant to be read aloud, and many of his phrases are now part of the language: "let there be light", "the powers that be", "the salt of the earth", "the signs of the times", "eat, drink and be merry", "fight the good fight". He is credited with coining the words *scapegoat* and *Passover*.

The reason his phrases became so familiar is that, less than a century after his death, the scholars who produced the most influential Bible translation in the English language borrowed heavily from him. The **King James Bible**, or Authorised Version, was commissioned by James I and published in 1611, the work of some fifty scholars working in six committees at Westminster, Oxford and Cambridge. Estimates suggest that more than three-quarters of its New Testament is Tyndale's wording. For three and a half centuries it was read aloud in churches across the English-speaking world, every Sunday, and its rhythms sank into the language. When someone says "a labour of love", "the skin of my teeth", "how the mighty are fallen" or "set thine house in order", they are quoting the 1611 Bible (with Tyndale often hiding behind it), usually without knowing it.

![The title page of the first edition of the King James Bible, 1611.](/assets/essays/how-english-got-so-strange/images/kjv.jpg)
*Title page of the King James Bible, first edition (1611). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

### Shakespeare's word-hoard

The other great shaper of early modern English is William Shakespeare. His plays are full of words and phrases that are now everyday English: "wild-goose chase", "break the ice", "a laughing stock", "in a pickle", "vanish into thin air", "the be-all and the end-all", "green-eyed monster", "a sorry sight", "fair play" and "neither here nor there".

Shakespeare is often credited with inventing well over a thousand words. The truth is more modest. The early editors of the *Oxford English Dictionary* read Shakespeare far more thoroughly than they read his less famous contemporaries, so his plays are often the first place they found a word, even when it was already in use. As more early texts have been digitised and searched, many supposed Shakespeare coinages have turned out to be older. But he really does provide the earliest known use of a good number of words, including *eyeball*, *lacklustre*, *swagger*, and *lonely*. More importantly, he showed what English could do: its range, its flexibility, its talent for turning nouns into verbs ("he words me", says Cleopatra) and for playing with every one of its layers of vocabulary, Saxon and French and Latin, sometimes in a single line.

His plays were collected after his death by two of his fellow actors, John Heminges and Henry Condell, and published in 1623 in a large volume known as the **First Folio**. About half of his plays, including *Macbeth*, *The Tempest* and *Twelfth Night*, had never been printed before, and without the First Folio they would almost certainly have been lost.

![The title page of the First Folio of Shakespeare's plays, published in 1623, with the engraved portrait by Martin Droeshout.](/assets/essays/how-english-got-so-strange/images/firstfolio.jpg)
*Title page of Mr. William Shakespeares Comedies, Histories, & Tragedies, the First Folio (1623), British Library. CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

### Thou and you

The English of Shakespeare and the King James Bible still had two words for "you". **Thou** (with *thee*, *thy* and *thine*) was singular and familiar: used to children, close friends, servants, and to God. **You** (with *ye*) was plural, or a polite form for a single person of higher rank, like French *vous* or German *Sie*, or the old Norwegian *De*. Using "thou" to a stranger could be a calculated insult. In 1603, at the trial of Sir Walter Raleigh, the prosecutor, Sir Edward Coke, shouted at him: "I thou thee, thou traitor!"

Over the seventeenth century, "you" took over in most of England, and "thou" retreated to prayer, poetry and some regional dialects, where it survives in parts of Yorkshire and Lancashire. The Quakers kept using "thou" to everyone on principle, as a statement of human equality, and were often beaten or abused for it. The ironic result is that modern English, which once had a special word for intimate address, now has none, and the old intimate form sounds formal, solemn and religious to modern ears, because we mostly hear it in prayers.

## Taming the Language: Dictionaries and Rules

### Dr Johnson's dictionary

By the eighteenth century, many educated people worried that English was chaotic and decaying, and envied the French, who had an Academy, founded in 1635, to regulate their language. Writers including Jonathan Swift proposed an English Academy. It never happened. Instead, the English got a dictionary, made almost single-handedly by a large, untidy, brilliant man from Lichfield.

Samuel Johnson signed the contract in 1746 with a group of London booksellers, for a fee of £1,575. He boasted that he would finish in three years; the forty members of the French Academy had taken forty years over their dictionary, and, he reasoned, three to sixteen hundred was the proportion of an Englishman to a Frenchman. It took him nearly nine. *A Dictionary of the English Language* was published in 1755, in two enormous volumes, with about 42,000 entries illustrated by more than 100,000 quotations from English writers.

![Samuel Johnson, painted by Sir Joshua Reynolds around 1772: the man who wrote the most famous dictionary in English, largely on his own.](/assets/essays/how-english-got-so-strange/images/johnson.jpg)
*Sir Joshua Reynolds, Samuel Johnson (c. 1772), Tate. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Johnson's dictionary was not the first English dictionary. That honour usually goes to Robert Cawdrey's *A Table Alphabeticall* of 1604, which explained around 2,500 "hard words". But Johnson's was the first great one, and it was also a work of literature, full of his personality. Some of his definitions are famous for their wit:

> **Lexicographer.** A writer of dictionaries; a harmless drudge, that busies himself in tracing the original, and detailing the signification of words.

> **Oats.** A grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people.

Others are famous for being impenetrable. Here is how he defined a "network":

> **Network.** Any thing reticulated or decussated, at equal distances, with interstices between the intersections.

Let us imagine a conversation between Johnson and his friend and biographer James Boswell, who was, of course, a Scot. The exchange is invented, but the opinions are entirely in character.

> **Boswell:** Sir, I have been reading your definition of oats. I must protest on behalf of my country.
>
> **Johnson:** Protest away, Bozzy. It is nevertheless true.
>
> **Boswell:** Very true, sir. And where else will you find such fine horses as in England, or such fine men as in Scotland?
>
> **Johnson:** Sir, the noblest prospect which a Scotchman ever sees is the high road that leads him to England.

That last line is genuinely Johnson's; he said it, according to Boswell, at a dinner in 1763. (Boswell's riposte about horses and men is traditionally attributed to Lord Elibank, not to Boswell, but it was too good to leave out of an imagined conversation.)

![The title page of Johnson's Dictionary of the English Language, from the second edition.](/assets/essays/how-english-got-so-strange/images/johnsondict.png)
*Title page of Samuel Johnson's A Dictionary of the English Language, second edition. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Johnson's dictionary did more than any other single work to fix English spelling. He did not invent most of his spellings; he generally followed what printers were already doing, with an eye to etymology. But once a great dictionary said a word was spelt a particular way, people began to think of other spellings as wrong. Johnson himself was rather humble about what a dictionary could achieve. In his preface he wrote that it was vain for lexicographers to imagine they could change "sublunary nature" and stop a living language from changing. Languages, he noted, like the people who speak them, are always in motion.

### The rule-makers

Not everyone shared Johnson's humility. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, grammarians produced a string of books laying down the law about "correct" English, and some of the rules they invented, often modelled on Latin grammar, which works quite differently, still haunt schoolchildren and pedants today.

The idea that you should never end a sentence with a preposition can be traced back to the poet John Dryden, who in 1672 criticised Ben Jonson for doing it. It became a rule in later grammar books. There is a famous story that Winston Churchill, rebuked for ending a sentence with a preposition, replied: "This is the sort of English up with which I will not put." He almost certainly never said it, but the joke makes the point. The ban on **split infinitives** ("to boldly go") became widely taught in the nineteenth century, partly on the grounds that in Latin an infinitive is a single word and cannot be split. English is not Latin. Most modern style guides now accept the split infinitive when it reads better.

The grammarians also took against the use of **singular "they"**, as in "someone has left their umbrella", and insisted on "he" instead. But singular "they" has been used in English since at least the fourteenth century, and appears in the works of Chaucer's contemporaries, Shakespeare and Jane Austen. Its recent revival is, from a historian's point of view, a return to an old habit rather than a new invention.

## Across the Ocean: American English

### Noah Webster's revolution

English crossed the Atlantic with the first permanent English settlement at Jamestown, Virginia, in 1607, and the *Mayflower* in 1620. American English took in words from Native American languages (*raccoon, moose, skunk, squash, moccasin*), from Dutch settlers in New York (*cookie, boss, Santa Claus, Yankee* probably) and from Spanish in the south-west (*canyon, ranch, rodeo, lasso*).

![Noah Webster, painted by Samuel F. B. Morse, who was a portrait painter before he invented Morse code. Webster's American Dictionary of 1828 gave the United States its own spellings.](/assets/essays/how-english-got-so-strange/images/webster.jpg)
*Samuel F. B. Morse, Noah Webster (undated). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

After independence, a Connecticut schoolteacher and fierce patriot called Noah Webster set out to give the new nation a language of its own. His *American Spelling Book*, the famous "blue-backed speller", first published in 1783, sold tens of millions of copies over the following century. His *American Dictionary of the English Language* of 1828 introduced or popularised most of the spelling differences that now separate American from British English: *color* and *honor* for *colour* and *honour*; *center* and *theater* for *centre* and *theatre*; *defense* for *defence*; *traveler* for *traveller*; and *plow* for *plough*. Some of his more radical proposals, such as *tung* for *tongue*, never caught on. He also had a political point: a nation that had thrown off British rule should throw off British spelling.

Many supposedly "American" usages are actually old British ones that Britain later abandoned. Americans still say "fall" for autumn, as many British people once did, and "gotten", which was common in British English in Shakespeare's day. And the "-ize" spelling in words like "organize", which many British people now think of as American, is also the traditional house style of the Oxford University Press, which prefers it because the ending comes from the Greek "-izein".

## The Biggest Dictionary in the World

### A shed full of slips

In 1857 the Philological Society of London decided that existing English dictionaries were inadequate and proposed a new one that would record every word in English from about 1150 onwards, with its full history, illustrated by quotations showing how its meaning had changed over time. They expected it to take ten years. It took seventy.

The great editor of what became the **Oxford English Dictionary** was James Murray, a largely self-taught Scottish schoolmaster and bank clerk with a huge beard, who took charge in 1879. He worked in a corrugated-iron shed he called the Scriptorium, first in the garden of his house at Mill Hill School in north London and then in his garden in Oxford, surrounded by pigeonholes holding millions of slips of paper. The slips were sent in by an army of volunteer readers all over the English-speaking world, each slip bearing a quotation that illustrated a word in use.

One of the most prolific volunteers was an American former army surgeon called William Chester Minor, who sent in tens of thousands of quotations from his book-lined rooms. Only after years of correspondence did Murray discover that Minor was writing from Broadmoor, the asylum for the criminally insane, where he had been confined since murdering a man in London in a fit of paranoid delusion in 1872. Their friendship is the subject of Simon Winchester's book *The Surgeon of Crowthorne* (published in America as *The Professor and the Madman*). Among the dictionary's later staff was a young J. R. R. Tolkien, who worked on words beginning with "w", including *walrus*, *wasp* and *waggle*, in 1919 and 1920.

The first instalment, covering *A* to *Ant*, appeared in 1884. Murray died in 1915, having reached the letter "T". The final part was published in 1928, more than seventy years after the project began. It contained more than 400,000 words and phrases. The dictionary has never stopped growing, and it is now updated online every few months, adding new words such as *selfie*, *podcast* and *doomscrolling*, and revising old ones.

## English Goes Global

### Words from everywhere

As Britain built a worldwide empire, and as English-speaking traders, soldiers, sailors and settlers travelled the globe, English borrowed words from almost every language it met. It has always been a magpie language, and this was the magpie at its most acquisitive.

From India came *bungalow, shampoo, pyjamas, jungle, thug, loot, cot, juggernaut, verandah, dinghy* and *bandana*. From Malay came *amok* and *orangutan*, and from a southern Chinese dialect, probably by way of Malay, came *ketchup*. From Australian Aboriginal languages came *kangaroo* and *boomerang*. From Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs, via Spanish, came *tomato, chocolate, avocado* and *chilli*. From the Taíno people of the Caribbean came *canoe, hammock, hurricane* and *barbecue*. From Arabic, often via other languages, came *algebra, alcohol, sugar, coffee* and *zero*. From Czech came *robot*, coined for a 1920 play by Karel Čapek. From German came *kindergarten, angst* and *hamster*.

Even Norway, so generous to English in the Viking Age, kept contributing. Modern English took *ski* and *slalom* from Norwegian, along with *fjord* and *lemming*, and in 1940 the name of the Norwegian Nazi collaborator Vidkun Quisling became the common noun *quisling*, meaning a traitor who collaborates with an occupying enemy. It was the twentieth century's grim little gift to the dictionary.

The result is a language with an unusually enormous vocabulary. Estimates vary wildly depending on how you count, but English almost certainly has more words than most languages, largely because it never throws anything away and is always ready to steal something new.

### A language without owners

Today English is spoken by roughly 1.5 billion people, but only around 400 million of them are native speakers. The rest have learnt it as a second or foreign language. That means most conversations in English, around the world, now take place between people for whom English is not their mother tongue: a Brazilian engineer and a Korean client, an Indian programmer and a German manager, a Nigerian doctor and a Norwegian researcher. English has become the world's main language of science, aviation, business, diplomacy and the internet.

The linguist Braj Kachru described it as three circles: an "inner circle" of countries where English is the main native language (Britain, Ireland, the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand); an "outer circle" where it is an official or important second language, often a legacy of empire (India, Nigeria, Singapore, the Philippines, Kenya); and an "expanding circle" where it is learnt as a foreign language (China, Japan, Brazil, most of Europe, including Norway, whose citizens often speak it better than the British).

The outer circle has developed its own thriving varieties, with their own words and grammar: Indian English has *prepone*, meaning to bring forward, the logical opposite of *postpone*, and nobody can deny it is useful. Singapore's Singlish adds Malay, Hokkien and Tamil influences and sentence-final particles such as "lah". Nigerian Pidgin, an English-based language spoken by tens of millions of people, has become a powerful language of music and media. Native speakers in Britain and America no longer get to decide alone what English is.

## Stubborn Survivors: Mice, Feet and Went

If English threw away most of its old grammar, why does it still have irregular plurals and irregular verbs that torment every learner? The answer is that the commonest words are the hardest to change. Children learn them so early, and adults use them so often, that they resist the tidying-up that happens to rarer words. The irregularities of English are mostly fossils of the old Germanic system, preserved in the words people used every day.

Take **mouse** and **mice**, **foot** and **feet**, **goose** and **geese**, **tooth** and **teeth**. In the prehistoric ancestor of English, these plurals were formed with an ending containing an "i" sound, which pulled the vowel of the word towards the front of the mouth, a process linguists call *umlaut* (the same thing that gives German its *Maus* and *Mäuse*). Then the ending disappeared, leaving only the changed vowel to mark the plural. In Old English, *mus* had the plural *mys*, and *fot* had *fet*. These survived because everybody needed to talk about feet and mice. Rarer words with the same pattern were regularised long ago. "Book" once had a plural something like "beech".

Then there are the plurals in **-en**. Old English had several ways of making plurals, and one of them used an "-an" ending, which became "-en". In Middle English, plenty of words took it: *shoon* for shoes, *eyen* for eyes, *housen* for houses, which survived in some dialects for centuries. Today the standard language keeps only **oxen**, the rather biblical **brethren**, and **children**. "Children" is a double plural: the Old English plural was *cildru*, and medieval speakers, no longer hearing it as plural, added an "-en" on top for good measure, like someone wearing a belt and braces.

The verbs are even stranger. Old English had two main kinds of verb. "Weak" verbs made their past tense with an ending in "-d" or "-t", like *walk/walked*. "Strong" verbs changed their vowel instead, like *sing/sang/sung*, *ride/rode/ridden*, *drive/drove/driven*. Over the centuries, most strong verbs have gone over to the weak pattern: people once said *holp* for helped, *clomb* for climbed, and *crope* for crept. A few have gone the other way, at least in American English, where "dove" competes with "dived" and the cheeky newcomer "snuck" with "sneaked".

And then there is **went**. The Old English past tense of *gan*, to go, was *eode*, a word unrelated to "go" at all. By the fifteenth century it had been elbowed aside by *went*, which was originally the past tense of a different verb, *wend*, meaning to turn or make one's way (as in "wending your way home"). So "go" stole its past tense from "wend", and "wend" had to make a new one, "wended". Linguists call this kind of patchwork *suppletion*. English has a few other examples: *be*, *am*, *is*, *are*, *was* and *were* come from three different ancient verbs glued together, and *good*, *better*, *best* have nothing to do with each other either.

### The mysterious "do"

One of the most distinctive features of English grammar is something learners find baffling and native speakers never notice: the little helper verb "do". In English we say "Do you know him?", "I don't know him" and "Where did you go?" Almost no other European language does this. Shakespeare's characters could still say "Know you not?" and "I know not", as a German or a Norwegian would, but the modern pattern was spreading fast in his day, and by around 1700 it had taken over.

Where did it come from? Nobody is quite sure. Some linguists have suggested that it was influenced by the Celtic languages, Welsh and Cornish, which use a similar construction with their verb for "do", and which were spoken alongside English in the west of Britain for centuries. Others think it developed entirely within English. The debate continues, but it is a reminder that even the Britons whom the Anglo-Saxons called foreigners may have left a deeper mark on English than a handful of river names.

## Accents: Whose English Is Proper?

For most of its history, English had no "correct" accent. Kings, courtiers and peasants all spoke with the accents of the places they came from. Sir Walter Raleigh is said to have kept his broad Devon accent at the court of Elizabeth I. It was only in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, as education spread and the middle classes grew anxious about their status, that a particular way of speaking, associated with the upper classes, the great public schools and the universities of Oxford and Cambridge, came to be seen as superior.

In 1869 the phonetician Alexander Ellis described it as "received pronunciation", in the old sense of "received" meaning accepted in polite society. In the 1920s the newly founded BBC adopted it for its announcers, and for decades it was often called "BBC English". The phonetician Daniel Jones was the scholar who made it the standard model of British pronunciation for foreign learners, and George Bernard Shaw satirised the whole business of accent and class in *Pygmalion* (1913), later the musical *My Fair Lady*, in which a phonetics professor bets that he can pass off a Cockney flower girl as a duchess by teaching her to speak properly.

One of the most noticeable features of received pronunciation is that it does not pronounce the "r" in words like "car" and "hard" unless a vowel follows. This "r-dropping" spread in southern England in the eighteenth century. Most of Scotland, Ireland, the West Country and most of North America kept the "r". That is why, in this respect at least, many Americans sound more like Shakespeare than the King does.

Today received pronunciation, in its traditional form, is spoken by only a small minority of the British population, usually estimated at a few per cent, and it has itself changed considerably: recordings of BBC announcers from the 1950s sound startlingly clipped today. Regional accents, once a barrier to many careers, are now heard everywhere in broadcasting. Scouse, Geordie, Brummie, Glaswegian, Cockney and dozens of others remain gloriously alive, and new varieties such as "Multicultural London English", shaped by the speech of the capital's many communities, are emerging among the young.

There is one more British variety worth mentioning, because it brings our story full circle. In Orkney and Shetland, which were ruled by Norway until the fifteenth century, people spoke a Scandinavian language called **Norn** until the eighteenth century, when it finally gave way to Scots and English. It left behind hundreds of words in the local dialects, many of them for weather, the sea, fish and the land, and the islands' place names are still overwhelmingly Norse. The Vikings, it seems, never entirely left.

## Why Not Fix the Spelling?

If English spelling is so chaotic, why not simply reform it? People have tried. The American Simplified Spelling Board was founded in 1906 with money from Andrew Carnegie, and President Theodore Roosevelt ordered the federal Government Printing Office to adopt 300 of its simplified spellings, including *thru* for *through*. Congress rebelled, ridicule followed, and Roosevelt backed down within months. In Britain, the Simplified Spelling Society was founded in 1908. George Bernard Shaw left part of his estate to fund a new phonetic alphabet for English, and after a competition the "Shavian" alphabet was used to publish an edition of his play *Androcles and the Lion* in 1962. Hardly anyone has used it since.

The reasons reform fails are worth understanding. The first is practical: hundreds of millions of people have already learnt the current system, and the whole printed record of the language uses it. The second is the problem of accents. A spelling system based on pronunciation has to choose *whose* pronunciation. Should "bath" be spelt to match the vowel of London or of Manchester? Should "car" have an "r", which Americans and Scots pronounce and most English people don't? English spelling, precisely because it reflects no modern accent, is equally unfair to everyone, which is a sort of fairness.

The third reason is more subtle. English spelling preserves connections between words that pronunciation hides. *Sign* and *signature*, *bomb* and *bombard*, *nation* and *national*, *electric* and *electricity*, *muscle* and *muscular*: the spelling shows you the family relationship even though the sounds have drifted apart. A reader who knows "sign" can guess what "signature" means. For a fluent reader, English spelling is less a guide to pronunciation than a map of meaning and history.

## Hwæt! The Story Continues

English never stops changing. Every year brings new words (*selfie*, *podcast*, *ghosting*, *rizz*), new meanings for old ones (a "mouse", a "cloud", a "tweet", a "stream"), and the slow death of others. "Whom" is fading. "Literally" now means "figuratively" as often as not, much to the fury of people who have not noticed that "very" once meant "truly" and "awful" once meant "inspiring awe". The internet, texting and emoji have created new forms of written informality. Artificial intelligence is now producing a substantial share of the English text in the world, which raises entirely new questions about how the language will evolve.

Some people fear that English will fracture, as Latin did after the fall of the Roman Empire, into mutually unintelligible daughter languages. It is possible in the very long run, but global communication pulls the other way, constantly bringing the varieties back into contact. Others fear that global English will crush smaller languages, and that fear is better founded: many of the world's roughly 7,000 languages are endangered, and English is one of the big languages pressing on them.

But the history of English suggests something hopeful too. It is not a pure language and never has been. It was made by Angles and Saxons, Danes and Norwegians, Norman knights and Flemish printers, Renaissance scholars, Bible translators, playwrights, dictionary-makers, and millions of people across the world who took it and made it their own. Its spelling is a museum. Its vocabulary is a bazaar. Its grammar is a ruin that people have lived in so long they have made it comfortable.

The next time you stumble over "though", "through" and "tough", or wonder why "colonel" is pronounced "kernel" (it came into English in two rival forms, *coronel* and *colonel*, and English kept the pronunciation of one and the spelling of the other), remember that you are not looking at a mistake. You are looking at history: fifteen centuries of invasions, marriages, markets, monasteries, printing presses and arguments, written down in a single strange alphabet. *Hwæt*, as the Beowulf poet would say. Listen.
