# The War That Made America

### The American Civil War: what it was about, who fought it, and why it is still arguing with us

*A long read in the spirit of an educator who has had one coffee too many and is not sorry.*

---

## 1. A bombardment in which nobody died

At 4.30 on the morning of 12 April 1861, a mortar shell rose over Charleston Harbour, South Carolina, and burst above Fort Sumter, a pentagonal island fortress of brick and masonry that had been under construction for more than thirty years and was still not quite finished. It was the signal for Confederate batteries ringed around the harbour to open fire. The garrison inside, some 80-odd soldiers commanded by Major Robert Anderson, a Kentuckian who owned slaves himself and had no wish to fight his neighbours, waited for daylight before replying.

![Bombardment of Fort Sumter, Charleston Harbor, 12th and 13th of April 1861](https://www.worldhistory.org/uploads/images/21516.jpeg)
*The bombardment of Fort Sumter, 12–13 April 1861. Lithograph by Currier & Ives, 1861. Library of Congress. Public domain.*

For thirty-four hours the shells fell. The fort's wooden barracks caught fire; ammunition ran low; the smoke grew thick enough that the defenders lay on the floor with wet cloths over their faces. On the afternoon of 13 April, Anderson agreed to surrender. And here is the strange and rather wonderful fact with which any honest account of the American Civil War must begin: **nobody was killed in the bombardment**. The first death associated with the fort came the next day, during the surrender ceremony, when a cannon being fired in a 100-gun salute to the American flag exploded prematurely and killed a Union private named Daniel Hough. A second man later died of his injuries.

The war that began with one accidental death went on to kill, by the most careful modern estimates, somewhere between 650,000 and 850,000 people. The traditional figure, 620,000, was calculated in the 1880s and 1890s from muster rolls and pension records; a 2011 re-analysis of census data by the demographer J. David Hacker pushed the likely total to about 750,000. Whichever number you prefer, it is roughly as many as the United States has lost in all its other wars *combined*, and it came out of a population of about 31 million. Imagine a modern war that killed several million Americans and you begin to feel the scale.

How does a country get from a harmless bombardment to that? What exactly were these people fighting about? Who were they, beyond the famous generals in their famous hats? What came of it, and what is still lying around from it, like unexploded ordnance, in the politics and geography of the present day?

That is the task. Let us begin, as one should with any good argument, with the furniture.

## 2. A country built on a crack

### The founding bargain

The United States was born in 1776 declaring that "all men are created equal," and was at that moment home to roughly half a million enslaved people. Many of the signatories owned human beings, including the author of that sentence, Thomas Jefferson. Historians have spent two and a half centuries pointing out the tension, and the founders themselves knew it. The question was what to do about it.

Their answer, in the Constitution of 1787, was to dodge. The document never uses the words "slave" or "slavery". It speaks, delicately, of "other persons" and of persons "held to service or labour". But the dodging had teeth:

- **The three-fifths clause** counted each enslaved person as three-fifths of a free person when allocating seats in the House of Representatives and votes in the Electoral College. The enslaved could not vote, of course, but their presence swelled the political power of the men who held them.
- **The fugitive slave clause** required that people escaping slavery into free states be returned.
- **A ban on restricting the international slave trade until 1808** was written into the text. Congress duly outlawed the importation of enslaved Africans from 1 January 1808, though the internal trade, selling people from Virginia to the cotton lands of the Deep South, boomed instead.

Meanwhile the northern states, one by one, began to abolish slavery or adopt gradual emancipation: Pennsylvania in 1780, Massachusetts by court ruling in the 1780s, New York gradually from 1799, with the last enslaved people there freed in 1827. By the early nineteenth century the country was visibly dividing into "free states" and "slave states". Each side carried on as if this was a temporary arrangement. It was not.

### Cotton, and a gin

In 1793 a Massachusetts-born inventor named Eli Whitney, working on a Georgia plantation, patented a machine that separated the sticky green seeds of short-staple cotton from its fibre far faster than human hands could. The cotton gin made a previously unprofitable crop spectacularly profitable. Combined with the Industrial Revolution in Britain, whose mills in Lancashire were hungry for raw cotton, it made the American South the supplier of the world's most important industrial raw material.

The effect was the exact opposite of what some founders had hoped. Rather than slavery withering away as uneconomic, it expanded westward in a great arc from the Carolinas to Texas. By 1860 cotton accounted for well over half of all American exports by value, and the United States produced the great majority of the cotton Britain consumed. Southern politicians were fond of saying "cotton is king", and they meant it as a threat.

By 1860 the census counted just under four million enslaved people, about one in eight Americans and about one in three residents of the slave states. By some estimates the market value of those enslaved people, about three billion dollars at the time, exceeded the combined value of all the nation's railways and factories. That is a ghastly sentence to write. It is also essential to understanding the war: slavery was not a quaint peripheral institution that the South hung on to out of habit. It was the single largest asset class in the American economy, and the legal, social and political order of the South was built around it. Roughly a quarter of white southern families owned slaves, and many more aspired to, or depended on, those who did.

### Two societies, one flag

This did not mean that the North was a land of pure virtue. Northern banks financed the cotton trade, Northern mills wove the cotton, Northern shipping carried it, and Northern racism was real and deep; in 1860 most free states barred Black people from voting, and several barred them from entering at all. Illinois, Lincoln's own state, tried to bar Black settlers by law. But the North was developing a different economy and a different ideology. It had factories, canals, railways, and an immigrant working class, and it had an ideology, "free labour", which held that a man should be able to rise by his own work and sell it on his own terms. Slavery, said the new Republican Party, was an affront to that idea and a threat to it, because it would drag the nation toward a "Slave Power" that would monopolise land and political power.

The South, for its part, increasingly stopped calling slavery a "necessary evil," the phrase of earlier generations, and started calling it a "positive good". Senator John C. Calhoun of South Carolina gave the argument its famous form in 1837, and by the 1850s southern pulpits, newspapers and universities were full of it.

## 3. So what was it about?

If you ask the question in an American classroom, you may hear any of four answers: slavery; states' rights; economics; or "it's complicated". Let us take them in turn, because this is the part of the story that has been most argued over, most distorted, and where being clear matters most.

### The primary source test

The best way to find out why the seceding states seceded is to ask them. Several of them were kind enough to write it down. Four states issued formal declarations explaining their reasons: South Carolina, Mississippi, Georgia and Texas. They are not subtle.

Mississippi's, of January 1861, opens with a sentence that has the virtue of being clear: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery, the greatest material interest of the world." South Carolina complained that Northern states had failed to enforce the Fugitive Slave Act and that a party had been elected president "whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery." Texas declared that the "servitude of the African race" was justified by "the revealed will of the Almighty Creator" and defended white supremacy as the foundation of its government.

Then there is the Confederacy's own vice-president, Alexander Stephens of Georgia, who gave a speech in Savannah on 21 March 1861 in which he said the new government's "cornerstone" rested on the "great truth" that the Black man was not equal to the white man and that slavery was his natural and normal condition. It became known as the **Cornerstone Speech**, and later generations of Confederate sympathisers have been embarrassed by it ever since.

And the Confederate Constitution, adopted in March 1861, copied the US one almost word for word, with a telling exception: it forbade any law "denying or impairing the right of property in negro slaves" and guaranteed slavery in any new territories. It was a constitution for a country founded to preserve slavery, and it said so.

### "States' rights", for what?

Now, the states' rights answer. It is not quite nonsense, because southern leaders really did talk constantly about states' rights and the dangers of an overbearing federal government. But ask: *which rights?* The right to own human beings, to take them into new territories, and, crucially, to have the federal government help recapture people who escaped.

South Carolina's own declaration of causes complained about **Northern** states exercising *their* states' rights by passing "personal liberty laws" to protect Black residents from kidnap. The same South Carolinians who loved states' rights in the abstract had demanded in 1850 a stronger federal Fugitive Slave Act that overrode the states' decisions. As the historian Eric Foner has remarked, in effect, southern leaders were states' rights men when it helped slavery and nationalists when it did not.

### The economic argument, and why it is not a rival

There is an economic explanation, but it is not a rival to the slavery explanation; it *is* the slavery explanation. Slavery was the dominant economic interest in the South. The fight over whether slavery could expand into the western territories was, among other things, a fight over land, political power and the future shape of the American economy. To say the war was "about economics" is rather like saying a murder was "about a death".

### The Northern side

For the North, the war began as a war to preserve the Union, not to end slavery, and Lincoln was explicit about that. In a famous letter of August 1862 to the newspaper editor Horace Greeley he wrote that his "paramount object" was to save the Union, and that if he could do so without freeing any slave he would, if freeing all of them, he would, or some and not others, he would do that too. But the *reason* the Union was in danger was slavery, and the northern public, with the aid of events, gradually came to see that it could not defeat the Confederacy without confronting it. By 1865 it was a war to preserve the Union *and* destroy slavery, and Lincoln said so, in his Second Inaugural Address, in language of almost Biblical severity.

### The Lost Cause

Why does any of this need re-stating? Because after the war the defeated white South embarked on one of history's most successful public-relations campaigns, now known as **the Lost Cause**. It said the war had been a noble fight for constitutional principle; that slavery had been benign and not the cause; that the Confederate soldier was a gallant defender of home; that Reconstruction was a tragic abomination imposed on a prostrate South. It was taught in textbooks, carved into statues and filmed in *The Birth of a Nation* (1915) and, in softer form, *Gone with the Wind* (1939). Several generations of Americans, Northern as well as Southern, grew up on a version of history that the participants themselves would not have recognised. Professional historians have been taking it apart for about a century, beginning with the Black scholar W. E. B. Du Bois, whose *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935) was largely ignored by the white profession of his day.

So: **the Civil War was about slavery**, in the sense that without slavery there would have been no secession and no war. This does not make every Union soldier an abolitionist (most were not, at the start) or every Confederate soldier a committed defender of slavery (many owned no slaves and fought because their state, their neighbours, or their pride called). Motives of individuals are always more tangled than the causes of nations. But the cause of the *war* is not really in doubt.

## 4. The long fuse: 1820 to 1860

Wars like this do not start on a single morning. They are the end of a long chain of failed compromises. Let us go through them at a trot.

**1820: The Missouri Compromise.** When Missouri applied to join as a slave state, northerners objected that it would upset the balance of free and slave states in the Senate. The solution: admit Missouri as a slave state, Maine as a free one, and draw a line (36°30′ north) across the rest of the Louisiana Purchase, north of which slavery would be forbidden. Thomas Jefferson called the controversy "a fire bell in the night".

**1846–48: The Mexican–American War.** The United States seized from Mexico a vast territory, including present-day California, Nevada, Utah and most of Arizona and New Mexico. A Pennsylvania congressman, David Wilmot, proposed that slavery be banned in any land taken. The "Wilmot Proviso" passed the House repeatedly and died in the Senate, but it made the central question unavoidable: *would the West be slave or free?* A young Illinois congressman named Abraham Lincoln voted for it many times.

**1850: The Compromise.** California was admitted as a free state; the slave trade (but not slavery) was abolished in Washington, DC; and a much tougher Fugitive Slave Act forced Northerners to help recapture escapees, denying accused people a jury trial. The Act radicalised a generation of northerners who had never previously thought about slavery, as slave catchers appeared in their own towns.

**1852: *Uncle Tom's Cabin*.** Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel sold some 300,000 copies in the United States in its first year and was a sensation in Britain. It brought the human reality of slavery into drawing rooms, and made it very difficult for the North to see it as an abstraction. (A story that Lincoln greeted Stowe by calling her "the little woman who wrote the book that started this great war" is almost certainly apocryphal, but it is a good story, and the novel's impact was real.)

**1854: Kansas–Nebraska.** Senator Stephen Douglas of Illinois, wishing to build a railway across the plains, proposed to open the territories of Kansas and Nebraska to settlement under "popular sovereignty", letting settlers decide for themselves whether to allow slavery. This repealed the Missouri Compromise line. Northerners were furious. The Republican Party, formed in 1854 from the wreckage of the Whigs and from anti-slavery Democrats and Free Soilers, sprang up in a matter of months.

**1854–56: "Bleeding Kansas".** Pro- and anti-slavery settlers poured into Kansas, each trying to outvote the other, and sometimes shooting. In 1856 the abolitionist John Brown led a party that murdered five pro-slavery settlers at Pottawatomie Creek. In the same year, in the US Senate chamber, a South Carolina congressman named Preston Brooks beat the Massachusetts senator Charles Sumner nearly to death with a cane, after Sumner delivered a speech denouncing slavery and insulting one of Brooks's relatives. Sumner took three years to return to his seat. Southerners sent Brooks new canes in tribute.

**1857: *Dred Scott v. Sandford*.** The Supreme Court, in an opinion by Chief Justice Roger Taney, held that Black Americans, whether enslaved or free, could not be citizens of the United States, and that Congress had no power to ban slavery in the territories. At a stroke, it seemed to declare unconstitutional the entire platform of the Republican Party. It also made compromise much harder: how can you negotiate with a court that has said you have no case?

**1858: Lincoln and Douglas.** In the contest for an Illinois Senate seat, the two men held seven debates before large crowds. Lincoln, accepting the nomination, had said "a house divided against itself cannot stand," and that he did not expect the Union to be dissolved, but he did expect it to cease to be divided: it would become all one thing, or all the other. He lost the Senate race, but the debates made him a national figure.

**1859: Harpers Ferry.** John Brown, now convinced that only violence could end slavery, led about twenty men, including several Black men, in a raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, hoping to seize weapons and spark a slave uprising. It failed within about thirty-six hours; a detachment of US Marines, under the command of an army colonel named Robert E. Lee, stormed the engine house where Brown had taken refuge. Brown was tried and hanged on 2 December 1859, behaving with a dignity that made him a martyr in the North, and a terrifying symbol in the South of what Northern "fanatics" might unleash. A song celebrating him, *John Brown's Body*, became the tune to which Julia Ward Howe wrote, in 1861, "The Battle Hymn of the Republic".

Southerners had been told for years that the abolitionists wanted blood. Now it seemed they did.

### The election of 1860

In November 1860 the Democratic Party split in two, Northern Democrats backing Douglas and Southern Democrats backing the sitting vice-president, John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky, while a fourth party, the Constitutional Union ticket of John Bell, tried to appeal to moderates by essentially saying "let us not talk about it". Against this fractured field, Abraham Lincoln, the Republican, won with only about 39.8 per cent of the popular vote, but with a clear majority of electoral votes: 180 out of 303. He was not even on the ballot in ten southern states. He had won without a single southern electoral vote. For the first time a presidential candidate whose party was opposed to the expansion of slavery had won the White House, and the South could do nothing about it.

Lincoln made it clear that he would not interfere with slavery where it already existed. This was not enough. The Republicans' principle that slavery was wrong, and should be put on a path to extinction by being contained, was, to southern leaders, an existential threat. On 20 December 1860, South Carolina's convention voted to secede. Mississippi followed on 9 January, then Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana and, on 1 February, Texas. In February, delegates met at Montgomery, Alabama, and formed the **Confederate States of America**, choosing Jefferson Davis of Mississippi, a former US senator, secretary of war and West Point graduate, as their provisional president.

The outgoing president, James Buchanan, held that secession was illegal but that he had no power to stop it, a combination of views that satisfied nobody, and left the problem to Lincoln, who was inaugurated on 4 March 1861. In his inaugural address he pleaded with the South, ending with the line about "the better angels of our nature". Five weeks later, the shells fell on Sumter.

## 5. The cast: parties, plural

The question asked for "parties" in the broader sense, and quite right, because the Civil War was not merely a contest between two armies. It involved a whole cast, some obvious, others often ignored. Let us go through them.

### The Union

By 1861 the Union consisted of 23 states (including the border slave states that stayed loyal) with a population of about 22 million. It held roughly 90 per cent of American industrial capacity, the great majority of the railways (something like 35,000 km of track, compared with about 14,000 km in the South), and virtually the whole of the navy, the banking system and the machinery to make rifles. Its strategic aim was to restore the Union by force; its political aim, by the end, was to make the Union free.

**The Republican Party.** Lincoln's party was a coalition: former Whigs, anti-slavery Democrats, nativists, Free Soilers, abolitionists and businessmen who wanted tariffs, railways and free land in the West. It contained a *Radical* wing, men like Thaddeus Stevens and Charles Sumner who pushed for emancipation and equal rights early, and a *conservative* wing that wanted to move more cautiously. Lincoln spent the entire war managing the tension between them, usually calmly, sometimes with a story from his Illinois days to defuse the room.

**Abraham Lincoln himself.** Born in a Kentucky log cabin in 1809 with about a year of formal schooling in total, a lawyer and one-term congressman, he emerges from the record as one of those rare politicians who grew in office. He began by insisting the war was only about the Union; he ended by shepherding the Thirteenth Amendment through Congress. He also made serious mistakes: he spent the first years of the war with a series of timid generals, he suspended habeas corpus, and until at least 1862 he flirted with the idea of resettling freed Black people abroad, an idea he later dropped.

![Abraham Lincoln, 8 November 1863](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Abraham_Lincoln_November_1863.jpg)
*Abraham Lincoln, photographed by Alexander Gardner in Washington, 8 November 1863, eleven days before the Gettysburg Address. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

**The Democrats of the North.** Not all Northerners supported the war. "War Democrats" backed the Union effort; "Peace Democrats", called "Copperheads" by their enemies, wanted a negotiated settlement. Their most prominent figure, the Ohio congressman Clement Vallandigham, was arrested in 1863 for criticising the war and sent, by a bemused Lincoln, into exile within the Confederacy. Democratic opposition grew whenever the war went badly, and peaked in 1864.

**Abolitionists and free Black Northerners.** Frederick Douglass, who had escaped slavery in 1838 and become the most famous Black orator in the country; William Lloyd Garrison, whose newspaper *The Liberator* had been demanding immediate emancipation since 1831; Sojourner Truth; and thousands of lesser-known free Black Northerners who organised, petitioned, and eventually enlisted. They pushed the war toward emancipation more than any single politician.

![Frederick Douglass, c. 1879](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Frederick_Douglass_(circa_1879).jpg)
*Frederick Douglass, c. 1879. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

**Immigrants.** About a quarter of the Union Army was foreign-born. Germans, many of them refugees of the failed 1848 revolutions and passionately anti-slavery, and Irish, many of them fleeing the famine, filled whole regiments. The Irish Brigade fought with extraordinary courage at Antietam and Fredericksburg, though Irish workers in New York would also riot in 1863 against the draft, and Black New Yorkers paid the price.

### The Confederacy

The Confederate States of America ultimately comprised eleven states, with about 9 million people, of whom some 3.5 million were enslaved. It was a peculiar creation: a nation founded on the principle of state sovereignty that nonetheless had to build a powerful central government to fight a war. It created a national draft in April 1862, a year before the Union did, and imposed taxes, impressed supplies and, at times, suspended habeas corpus. Its own governors, notably Joseph Brown of Georgia and Zebulon Vance of North Carolina, often resisted Richmond's demands in the name of the very principle the Confederacy was founded on.

**Jefferson Davis.** Davis was able, hard-working, dutiful, proud, and not much loved. A West Point graduate who had been a distinguished secretary of war, he took a hands-on approach to military strategy that irritated his generals. He lacked Lincoln's political gifts, his humour, and his ability to bring critics into the tent. Whether that mattered to the outcome is a favourite parlour game among historians.

**The generals.** Robert E. Lee, Thomas "Stonewall" Jackson, James Longstreet, J. E. B. Stuart, Nathan Bedford Forrest, who would become the first Grand Wizard of the Ku Klux Klan, and others. The Confederacy had a remarkable early advantage in military leadership: many of the best US Army officers were southerners, and a good number resigned to follow their states. Lee, who had been offered command of the Union Army, declined, famously saying that he could not raise his hand against his native Virginia. It is a moving sentence; it is also a decision to fight for a government whose cornerstone was slavery.

![Robert E. Lee](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Robert_Edward_Lee.jpg)
*General Robert E. Lee, photograph, 1860s. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

**Poor whites, and the "rich man's war".** Roughly three-quarters of white southern families did not own slaves. Many of them fought bravely and loyally for the Confederacy. Many others resented a conflict in which, as the saying went, it was "a rich man's war and a poor man's fight". The Confederate draft law exempted anyone who oversaw twenty or more slaves, which did nothing to calm the feeling. Inflation, hunger and bread riots hit the South badly; in Richmond in April 1863 a crowd of women, demanding bread, ransacked shops. In mountain regions of Appalachia, in parts of Texas, Mississippi and Alabama, Unionism ran deep; tens of thousands of white southerners joined the Union Army.

### The people at the heart of it: the enslaved

Nearly four million people were held in slavery in 1860. They were not mere bystanders, and the idea that Lincoln or the Union Army "gave" them freedom is, as historians have shown, a distortion. As Union armies advanced into the South, enslaved people walked away from the plantations by the tens of thousands, heading for Union lines. Early in the war, General Benjamin Butler at Fort Monroe, Virginia, declined to return three escaped men to a Confederate owner, reasoning that if the Confederacy claimed to be a foreign country, he need not obey the Fugitive Slave Act, and that the men were "contraband of war". The term stuck: the escaped were called "contrabands". Their flight deprived the Confederacy of labour, handed the Union information and manpower, and forced the issue. The scholar W. E. B. Du Bois would later call it a "general strike", and the phrase captures how the collective act of walking away helped to unmake slavery from beneath.

![Harriet Tubman, c. 1885](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Harriet_Tubman_by_Squyer,_NPG,_c1885.jpg)
*Harriet Tubman, c. 1885. Photograph by H. Seymour Squyer, National Portrait Gallery, Smithsonian Institution. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Among them were extraordinary individuals. **Harriet Tubman**, who had escaped slavery in Maryland in 1849 and returned repeatedly to guide others to freedom, served the Union Army as a nurse, scout and spy, and in June 1863 helped to plan and lead the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, which liberated more than 700 enslaved people. **Robert Smalls**, an enslaved ship's pilot in Charleston, in May 1862 took command of a Confederate steamer, the *Planter*, while her white officers were ashore, steered her out of the harbour past five Confederate forts with his family aboard, and surrendered her to the Union blockade. He became a celebrated figure and later served in Congress.

### Women

Women were not on the battlefield in official capacity, but they were everywhere else. They ran farms and businesses while the men were away; staffed the Sanitary Commission, which raised money and supplies for Union soldiers; and nursed in hospitals: Clara Barton, later founder of the American Red Cross; Dorothea Dix, appointed Superintendent of Army Nurses; Sally Tompkins, a Richmond woman commissioned a captain in the Confederate cavalry so that her private hospital could keep running legally. Spies such as the Richmond Unionist Elizabeth Van Lew ran networks inside the Confederate capital. And several hundred women, by modern estimates, disguised themselves as men and served as soldiers.

### Native nations

Perhaps 20,000 Native Americans served in the war, on both sides, and the conflict spilled into Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma), where the "Five Tribes", Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek and Seminole, were split. Some of their leaders were slaveholders themselves. A Cherokee officer, Stand Watie, rose to brigadier general in the Confederate Army and became the last Confederate general to surrender, on 23 June 1865. Elsewhere the war gave cover for appalling violence: in Minnesota in 1862, after the Dakota uprising, 38 Dakota men were hanged in a single mass execution on 26 December, the largest in American history, and in 1864 Colorado militia massacred some 150 or more mostly Cheyenne and Arapaho people, many of them women and children, at Sand Creek.

### The border states

Four slave states, Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky and Missouri, stayed in the Union, and Lincoln treated them with enormous care. (He is said to have remarked that he hoped to have God on his side but must have Kentucky; the line is probably apocryphal but the sentiment is accurate.) Maryland, which surrounded Washington on three sides, mattered most: had it seceded, the capital would have been marooned in enemy territory. When Baltimore mobs attacked Massachusetts troops passing through in April 1861, Lincoln moved quickly to secure the state, suspending habeas corpus to do it. In 1863 the western counties of Virginia, which had opposed secession, were admitted to the Union as **West Virginia**.

### The foreign powers

No account of this war is complete without Britain, so let us give her her due.

The British government, led by Lord Palmerston, declared neutrality in May 1861, and in doing so acknowledged the Confederacy as a "belligerent", a legal status that angered Washington. British opinion was divided: the aristocracy and much of the press leaned toward the South, which seemed to them gentlemanly and the North vulgar; but Britain had abolished slavery in 1833 and a powerful anti-slavery movement stood against recognising a slaveholding republic. The Confederacy had hoped that "King Cotton" would force the issue: that if the supply of cotton were cut off, British mills would close and the government would be compelled to intervene.

And mills *did* close. The "Lancashire Cotton Famine" of 1861–65 threw hundreds of thousands of workers out of work, and yet, remarkably, the cotton workers of the north of England mostly refused to demand intervention on the side of the South. On 31 December 1862 a meeting of Manchester workers at the Free Trade Hall sent a message to Lincoln in support of the Union and of emancipation, and Lincoln replied with a famous letter praising their "sublime Christian heroism". A statue of Lincoln stands today in Manchester's Lincoln Square.

Two crises nearly brought war between Britain and the United States. In November 1861, in the **Trent Affair**, a US Navy captain stopped the British mail steamer *RMS Trent* on the high seas and seized two Confederate envoys, James Mason and John Slidell. Britain was outraged, sent several thousand troops to Canada, and prepared to fight. Prince Albert, in one of the last acts of his life (he died that December), helped soften the wording of the British ultimatum. Lincoln, who remarked that he could fight only one war at a time, released the men.

Then there was the **CSS *Alabama***. Built secretly at the Laird shipyard in Birkenhead on the Mersey and slipped out in July 1862 before the British government could stop her, she went on to capture or sink more than 60 Union merchant ships before the USS *Kearsarge* sank her off Cherbourg in June 1864. The US demanded compensation, and after years of acrimony an international tribunal in Geneva in 1872 ordered Britain to pay the United States $15.5 million. The "Alabama Claims" were a landmark in international arbitration. And the very last Confederate ship to surrender was another British-built raider, the CSS *Shenandoah*, which sailed into Liverpool on 6 November 1865, months after Appomattox, and hauled down her flag there.

France, under Napoleon III, took advantage of the American distraction to install an Austrian archduke, Maximilian, as emperor of Mexico in 1864; once the Union had won, the US pressured France to withdraw, and Maximilian was shot by a Mexican firing squad in 1867. Russia, which had its own reasons for wanting to show the Royal Navy a friendly face, sent fleets to New York and San Francisco in 1863. Neither Britain nor France ever formally recognised the Confederacy, and the Union's victories at Antietam and Gettysburg in 1862 and 1863 did much to hold them back.

## 6. The war itself

### 1861: Two armies of amateurs

On both sides, the first year was a comedy of enthusiasm. Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers on 15 April 1861; Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee and North Carolina responded by joining the Confederacy, which moved its capital to Richmond, a mere 160 km from Washington. Neither army was prepared. Most soldiers had never fired a shot in anger; regiments elected their own officers; uniforms were a riot of improvisation, so that at the first big battle many soldiers in blue and grey could not tell friend from foe.

That battle came on 21 July 1861, near a stream called Bull Run, close to Manassas Junction, Virginia. Washington society, treating it as a day out, drove down in carriages with picnic baskets to watch the Union army crush the rebellion. They watched instead as the green Union troops, after a promising start, broke and fled, becoming tangled with the civilian carriages on the road back. It was here that Brigadier General Thomas Jackson, standing firm with his Virginia brigade, was said to have "stood like a stone wall", and acquired his nickname.

The shock concentrated minds. The Union brought in a young, immensely self-assured general named **George B. McClellan** to build the Army of the Potomac, which he did superbly, drilling it into shape and winning its love. He then spent much of the next year not using it. Lincoln's frustration with McClellan, who was forever convinced the enemy outnumbered him, is among the great sagas of the war. ("If General McClellan does not want to use the army, I would like to borrow it for a while," Lincoln is reported to have said, which sounds like him, even if the exact wording is uncertain.)

Meanwhile the veteran General Winfield Scott, a hero of 1812 too old and too fat to ride a horse, proposed a plan to strangle the South: blockade its ports and take the Mississippi River, splitting the Confederacy. Newspapers mocked it as the "Anaconda Plan". It turned out to be roughly what happened.

### 1862: The war gets serious

The war's first great Union successes came in the West. In February 1862 an obscure brigadier general named **Ulysses S. Grant**, a failed farmer and shopkeeper who had been drummed out of the peacetime army for drinking, captured Forts Henry and Donelson in Tennessee, and when the Confederate commander asked for terms, replied that "no terms except unconditional and immediate surrender can be accepted." Newspapers decided his initials stood for "Unconditional Surrender" Grant. In April, at **Shiloh**, Tennessee, Grant's army was surprised and nearly destroyed, yet held on; the two-day battle produced about 23,000 casualties, more than in all of America's previous wars combined, and it taught both sides that this would not be short. Also that month, the Union navy under Captain David Farragut ran past the forts below New Orleans and captured the Confederacy's largest city and greatest port.

At sea, a different revolution was under way. In March 1862, in Hampton Roads, Virginia, the Confederate ironclad CSS *Virginia*, built on the hull of a captured Union frigate, destroyed two wooden Union warships in a single afternoon; the next day she met the Union's strange new vessel, USS *Monitor*, which looked, as one observer said, like "a cheesebox on a raft". They battled for hours to a draw. The world's navies took note: wooden warships were obsolete. The Royal Navy, among others, began to build ironclads in earnest.

In Virginia, McClellan finally moved on Richmond by sea, in the Peninsula Campaign, and came within sight of the city's church spires. But on 1 June, after the Confederate commander Joseph E. Johnston was wounded, Jefferson Davis gave command to his military adviser, **Robert E. Lee**. Lee, nicknamed "the King of Spades" for his enthusiasm for fortifications, turned out to be an aggressive, audacious general. In the Seven Days battles he threw McClellan back, and in August he smashed another Union army at Second Bull Run. In September he crossed the Potomac, taking the war into Maryland.

On 17 September 1862, outside the small town of Sharpsburg, along a creek called Antietam, the two armies met in what remains **the bloodiest single day in American military history**: about 22,700 dead, wounded or missing. The fighting swirled around a cornfield, a sunken lane and a stone bridge, and the corn was, as one witness described it, cut as with a knife. Tactically it was a draw; Lee withdrew, and McClellan, who had been given a captured copy of Lee's orders, let him go. But it was enough of a Union victory to give Lincoln the opening he had been waiting for. Five days later, he issued the **preliminary Emancipation Proclamation**. It also ended Britain's flirtation with mediation: Gladstone, then Chancellor of the Exchequer, had said in October that Jefferson Davis had "made a nation", but after Antietam and the proclamation the British Cabinet drew back.

### 1863: The turning point

On **1 January 1863**, Lincoln signed the final **Emancipation Proclamation**, declaring free all enslaved people in areas still in rebellion, and authorising the enlistment of Black men into the Union armed forces. We will return to this.

Lee, meanwhile, continued to humiliate a series of Union commanders: at Fredericksburg in December 1862, and at Chancellorsville in May 1863, where, outnumbered more than two to one, he divided his army three times and sent Stonewall Jackson on a daring flank march that crushed the Union right. It was Lee's greatest victory, and it cost him his best lieutenant. Returning from a reconnaissance in the dark, Jackson was shot by his own men, who mistook his party for Union cavalry. He died on 10 May. Lee is said to have remarked that Jackson had lost his left arm but that he, Lee, had lost his right.

Emboldened, Lee invaded the North a second time, this time heading into Pennsylvania. On 1 July his army collided with the Union Army of the Potomac, now commanded by George Meade, around the small crossroads town of **Gettysburg**. Over three days the two sides fought through wheatfields, orchards, boulder-strewn hills and cemetery ridges. On the second day Union troops, including the 20th Maine under Joshua Chamberlain, a professor of rhetoric turned colonel, held the extreme left of the line at Little Round Top. On the third, Lee ordered an assault of some 12,500 men across about a kilometre of open ground against the centre of the Union line. **Pickett's Charge**, as it has been known, was cut to pieces. Lee, riding out to meet the survivors, said (as the story goes) "It is all my fault." The battle left some 46,000 to 51,000 casualties in total. Lee's army withdrew, and never again invaded the North.

![The Battle of Gettysburg, Currier & Ives](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Battle_of_Gettysburg,_by_Currier_and_Ives.png)
*The Battle of Gettysburg, lithograph by Currier & Ives, 1863–1890s. Library of Congress. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

On the very next day, **4 July**, Vicksburg, Mississippi, surrendered to Grant. The "Gibraltar of the Confederacy" had been besieged for 47 days; its civilians lived in caves and ate mule meat. With Vicksburg gone, the Union controlled the Mississippi from source to mouth and cut the Confederacy in two. Lincoln wrote, with justified relief: "The Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea."

Four months later, on 19 November 1863, at the dedication of the soldiers' cemetery at Gettysburg, the main speaker, the celebrated orator Edward Everett, spoke for about two hours. The President followed with some 270 words in about two minutes. Everett wrote to him afterwards that he wished he could have come as near to the central idea of the occasion in two hours as Lincoln had in two minutes. The **Gettysburg Address** redefined the war, in a single paragraph, as a test of whether a nation "conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" could survive, and promised "a new birth of freedom". Note the echo of 1776. Lincoln was, quietly, rewriting the founding to include everyone.

Not all was glory in the North that summer. In July, New York City erupted in the **Draft Riots**. The new conscription law allowed wealthy men to buy their way out for $300, an unaffordable sum for most workers; poor, mostly Irish, crowds attacked draft offices, burned buildings, and then turned on Black New Yorkers, lynching several and burning down an orphanage for Black children (the children escaped). More than a hundred people died over four days and troops had to be rushed from Gettysburg to restore order.

### 1864: Grant, Sherman, and "hard war"

In March 1864 Lincoln, after years of searching for a general who would fight, promoted Grant to lieutenant general and put him in command of all Union armies. Grant devised a coordinated plan: every army would advance at once, so that the Confederacy, with fewer men, could not move reserves between fronts. He himself would go after Lee. His friend and subordinate **William Tecumseh Sherman** would take Atlanta.

![Ulysses S. Grant](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Ulysses_S._Grant_1870-1880.jpg)
*Ulysses S. Grant, 1870–1880. Brady-Handy Photograph Collection, Library of Congress. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The Overland Campaign in Virginia, from May to June, was a savage grind: the Wilderness, Spotsylvania, Cold Harbor. The two armies fought almost daily, and Union casualties amounted to tens of thousands in six weeks, a toll that appalled Northern opinion. Yet unlike previous commanders, when Grant was beaten he did not retreat. He sidestepped south and attacked again, until Lee's army was pinned in the trenches around Petersburg, the railway hub that supplied Richmond, for a siege that would last nine months and look rather like the First World War half a century early.

For Lincoln the summer was dire. The casualties, the stalemate, and a growing demand for peace left him convinced, in August, that he would lose the November election. He wrote a private memorandum, which he asked his cabinet to sign unread, pledging to cooperate with his successor to save the Union before inauguration. Then, on 2 September, came a telegram from Sherman: "Atlanta is ours, and fairly won." Farragut had already won Mobile Bay in August (the legend: "Damn the torpedoes, full speed ahead", torpedoes in those days meaning mines). Lincoln was re-elected on 8 November with 55 per cent of the popular vote and 212 electoral votes to 21 for his old general, George McClellan, now the Democratic candidate. Soldiers voted overwhelmingly for him.

Sherman then took the decision that has made him loved, hated and debated ever since. Leaving Atlanta in ashes, he set out in November with about 60,000 men for the coast at Savannah, some 480 km away, cutting loose from his supply lines and living off the land. His columns tore up railways, burned warehouses and mills, and seized food and livestock across a swathe some 50 to 100 km wide. The "March to the Sea" did less physical killing than its legend suggests, but a great deal of economic destruction. Sherman did not mince words: the aim was to make the South feel the war, and "war is cruelty," as he told the mayor of Atlanta. Historians still argue over whether it makes him the first modern practitioner of "total war", or a harder-edged version of what armies had always done. What is not in dispute is that it demonstrated to the South and to the world that the Confederacy could no longer protect its own heartland. He presented Savannah to Lincoln as a Christmas gift.

### 1865: Endings

In the cold early months of 1865, the Confederacy was starving. Lee's soldiers were deserting in thousands. In Petersburg the trenches were thinning. On 2 April, Lee told Davis that Richmond could not be held; the government fled by train, and the city, set alight by retreating Confederates, fell to Union troops, among them regiments of Black cavalry. Lincoln, in a gesture that was both reckless and moving, walked through the burned streets of the Confederate capital on 4 April, surrounded by freed people.

Lee tried to escape west with what remained of his army, but Grant's forces got ahead of him. On **9 April 1865, Palm Sunday**, at the little village of Appomattox Court House, Lee, immaculate in dress uniform, met Grant, mud-spattered in a private's coat with a general's shoulder straps, in the parlour of a house owned by a man named Wilmer McLean. McLean, in a remarkable twist, had been living near Manassas in 1861 when the first major battle of the war raged around his farm, and had moved his family south to escape the fighting. He is said to have remarked that the war began in his front yard and ended in his front parlour.

The terms were generous. Grant paroled Lee's tens of thousands of soldiers, rather than imprisoning them; gave them rations from his own army's stores; and, when Lee mentioned that his cavalry and artillery soldiers owned their horses, allowed them to take the animals home, "to work their little farms". When Union soldiers began to cheer, Grant ordered them to stop: "The war is over; the rebels are our countrymen again."

It was not over everywhere at once. Joseph Johnston surrendered to Sherman in North Carolina on 26 April; Kirby Smith's Trans-Mississippi command capitulated on 2 June; Stand Watie's Cherokee on 23 June. On 19 June, in Galveston, Texas, Union General Gordon Granger read General Order No. 3, informing the enslaved people of Texas that they were free. The anniversary is **Juneteenth**, a federal holiday since 2021. And there was the odd tail of the CSS *Shenandoah*, whose captain, ignorant of the Confederate defeat, went on capturing American whalers in the Bering Sea in June 1865, before finally surrendering at Liverpool in November.

### Five days

Lincoln lived for five days after Appomattox. On 11 April he gave his last public speech, in which he floated the idea of limited Black suffrage, "the very intelligent" and those who had served as soldiers. In the crowd, the actor **John Wilkes Booth**, an ardent Confederate sympathiser, heard it, and according to those who were with him, vowed that it would be the last speech Lincoln ever made. On the evening of 14 April, Good Friday, Lincoln went to Ford's Theatre in Washington to see a comedy, *Our American Cousin*. Booth entered the presidential box and shot him in the back of the head. Lincoln died early the next morning, 15 April. A coordinated attack on Secretary of State William Seward the same night left him wounded but alive. Booth was hunted down and killed on 26 April. Andrew Johnson, a Tennessee Unionist and former tailor, became the seventeenth president.

### Why did the North win? And could it have gone differently?

The usual answer is numbers and industry, and it is a good one: more men, more factories, more railways, more ships, and a blockade that choked the South. But it is only partly satisfying, since rebellions against bigger powers have often succeeded (the American Revolution being the obvious case). The Confederacy did not have to conquer the North; it had to survive long enough for the North to tire. At the low points, in summer 1862 and summer 1864, it very nearly did.

Several things stand out. **Leadership**: Lincoln, after trial and error, found Grant, Sherman and Sheridan, and, importantly, never lost the political thread. **Strategy**: the Union learned to use its advantages together, applying pressure on every front at once. **Politics**: the North had functioning elections even during war, so that popular consent could be renewed. And **emancipation**: the war to preserve the Union became a war that drew on the energy of freed people, who provided labour, intelligence and, finally, soldiers. The Confederacy's refusal to contemplate freeing any enslaved person, even to save itself, deprived it of manpower to the last. In January 1864 one of its best generals, Patrick Cleburne, proposed arming and freeing slaves to fight. His superiors suppressed the memo. Only in March 1865, with the Confederacy collapsing, did its Congress pass a hesitant bill to recruit Black soldiers; almost none were enrolled before the end.

### The technology of a new kind of war

It is sometimes called the first modern war. It was not quite, since the Crimean War had preceded it, but it certainly foreshadowed the machine-age wars to come. Rifled muskets, accurate at several hundred metres, made frontal assaults murderous while commanders were still trained to order them. The railways moved armies and the telegraph connected them to capitals (Lincoln spent hours in the War Department's telegraph office). Photography, in the hands of Mathew Brady and Alexander Gardner, brought battlefield dead to a public that had never seen them: when Brady exhibited images of Antietam in New York in October 1862, the *New York Times* wrote that if he had not brought bodies and laid them in our dooryards and along the streets, he had done something very like it. The Confederate submarine *H. L. Hunley* sank the USS *Housatonic* off Charleston on 17 February 1864, the first time a submarine sank an enemy warship, though she sank too, and her crew with her. Medicine advanced because it had to: Jonathan Letterman's ambulance corps and evacuation system, set up in the Army of the Potomac, became the basis for military medical care for the next century.

And yet disease killed far more than bullets. Roughly two-thirds of all deaths were from illness: typhoid, dysentery, pneumonia, measles, malaria. Camp sanitation was dismal. Prison camps were worse; at Andersonville, Georgia, about 13,000 of some 45,000 Union prisoners died in just over a year, and its commandant, Henry Wirz, was hanged after the war.

## 7. The war within the war: emancipation

It would be easy to tell the story of the Civil War as a series of battles and miss what mattered most. The destruction of slavery was the most radical thing to happen in American history, bar none. It took the property rights of some four million people, valued at billions of dollars, and abolished them without compensation.

It did not happen in one stroke. It happened by stages, driven by events, the actions of enslaved people, and a gradual shift in Northern opinion:

- **May 1861**: General Butler's "contraband" decision at Fort Monroe.
- **August 1861 and July 1862**: Confiscation Acts, allowing the seizure of slaves used for the Confederate war effort and later freeing those of rebels.
- **April 1862**: Slavery abolished in Washington, DC, with compensation to owners, the only compensated emancipation in US history, and one that, tellingly, is hardly known.
- **July 1862**: The Militia Act allowed Black men to be employed in the army.
- **1 January 1863**: The Emancipation Proclamation.
- **January 1865**: The Thirteenth Amendment passed Congress (31 January) and was ratified, after the war, on **6 December 1865**.

Let us be precise about the Emancipation Proclamation, since it is often misdescribed. It did not free *every* enslaved person: it applied only to areas "in rebellion", exempting the loyal border states and parts of the Confederacy under Union occupation. Lincoln justified it as a war measure, an exercise of his powers as commander-in-chief, since he had no constitutional authority to abolish slavery in peacetime. That is why he, and many others, wanted a constitutional amendment: to nail the thing down. But the Proclamation changed the nature of the war. It made it impossible for the Union to make peace without ending slavery in the South. It gave every Union advance a liberating character. And it opened the door to Black enlistment.

### Black soldiers

About 180,000 Black men served in the Union Army, roughly a tenth of its total strength, in the United States Colored Troops, with another 19,000 in the navy. Many were former slaves from the South; others were free Black men from the North. They were initially paid less than white soldiers ($10 a month, minus $3 for clothing, against $13 for whites), and some regiments refused all pay in protest until the discrepancy was ended in June 1864. They were commanded, for the most part, by white officers, and they faced the extra risk that Confederates would not treat them as prisoners of war: in April 1864, at Fort Pillow, Tennessee, Confederate forces under Nathan Bedford Forrest killed large numbers of Black and white Union soldiers after the fort had been overrun, in what Northern opinion immediately called a massacre.

![Storming Fort Wagner, 54th Massachusetts](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Storming_Fort_Wagner.jpg)
*The 54th Massachusetts Infantry storming Fort Wagner, South Carolina, 18 July 1863. Lithograph by Kurz & Allison, 1890. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

They fought. At Fort Wagner, outside Charleston, in July 1863, the 54th Massachusetts, a regiment of free Black Northerners (including two of Frederick Douglass's sons) led a doomed assault in which their colonel, Robert Gould Shaw, was killed; the regiment's courage did much to change Northern attitudes, and the 1989 film *Glory* told their story. Tens of thousands of Black soldiers died, the great majority of disease.

### Imagined: Douglass at the White House

*(Imagined dialogue. Frederick Douglass really did meet Lincoln at the White House on 10 August 1863 and pressed him about unequal pay, retaliation for the killing of Black prisoners, and promotions for Black soldiers; what they actually said is paraphrased from Douglass's own accounts. What follows is not a transcript.)*

> **Douglass:** Mr President, the Black soldier is paid less, fed worse, and if captured, murdered. And you ask him to bleed for the same flag.
>
> **Lincoln:** I know it, Mr Douglass. I am a man of limited power in these matters. I must carry public opinion with me, or the whole enterprise falls to pieces.
>
> **Douglass:** And if public opinion will not move?
>
> **Lincoln:** Then I must move it. Slowly, I fear. I am like a man who has to cross a river on a rope, and must not rush.
>

>
> **Lincoln:** (after a moment) That is a hard sentence. I'll turn it over.

Douglass came away from that meeting, by his own account, impressed by Lincoln's lack of condescension: he had, he said, never been treated as an equal so naturally by a white man. They met twice more. At the reception after Lincoln's Second Inaugural in March 1865, the doorkeepers tried to turn Douglass away; Lincoln heard of it, called to him across the crowded room, and asked what he thought of the speech. It was, said Douglass, "a sacred effort".

## 8. The immediate aftermath: ruin, grief, and a revolution unfinished

### The arithmetic of grief

Add up the numbers. Somewhere between 620,000 and 750,000 dead. About 2 million men served in the Union forces and somewhere between 800,000 and a million in the Confederate. A third or more of white southern men of military age were killed or wounded; in 1866, Mississippi is said to have spent about a fifth of its state budget on artificial limbs. Every town, North and South, was dotted with widows, orphans and amputees. In response, the government created the first national cemeteries. **Decoration Day**, the precursor of Memorial Day, grew out of the custom of decorating soldiers' graves. And the **pensions** system that took shape afterwards was so long-lived that the last person receiving a Civil War pension, Irene Triplett, the daughter of a Civil War veteran, died in 2020, aged 90, still collecting about $73 a month.

The South was physically wrecked. Richmond, Atlanta, Columbia and much of the Shenandoah Valley lay in ruins; railways were torn up, bridges blown, livestock gone, and the Confederate currency, which had suffered runaway inflation, was worthless. The wealth that had been invested in enslaved people, the greatest part of southern capital, had vanished. The South, which in 1860 had been among the richest regions on earth per head of free population, fell to being the poorest region of the United States, and stayed there for roughly a century.

### What the Union gained, and became

The North emerged transformed. To pay for the war, the federal government had imposed the first income tax, issued paper currency, the "greenbacks", that were not backed by gold, and created a national banking system in 1863. Congress, freed of southern votes, passed in 1862 a wave of legislation long blocked: the **Homestead Act**, giving 160 acres of western land free to settlers; the **Morrill Act**, funding land-grant colleges; the **Pacific Railway Act**, launching the transcontinental railway; and the Department of Agriculture. The federal government, which in 1860 had scarcely touched ordinary Americans' lives except through the post office, was now a massive presence.

There was a subtler change too. Before the war, people used to say "the United States *are*", a plural; afterwards, increasingly, "the United States *is*". The historian Shelby Foote famously made much of the grammatical shift, and while linguists find the real change more gradual, the point stands: the war settled, in blood, the question whether the Union was a voluntary compact of states or one nation. The Supreme Court wrote this into law in *Texas v. White* (1869): secession had been legally impossible.

### Reconstruction: the great unfinished business

What to do with the defeated South, and with four million newly freed people, was the central question of the next twelve years, **Reconstruction**.

Lincoln had proposed a lenient plan: when 10 per cent of a state's 1860 voters swore loyalty, the state could form a new government. After his death, President Andrew Johnson took a similar line, pardoning thousands of ex-Confederates and allowing southern states to elect new governments, largely dominated by the old elite. These governments promptly passed **Black Codes**: laws restricting freed people's movement, requiring annual labour contracts, banning them from carrying guns or renting land, and in places allowing them to be arrested for "vagrancy" and leased out as labour. It looked, to many Northerners, like slavery by another name. 

Congress, controlled by Republicans, and sceptical of Johnson, struck back. It extended the Freedmen's Bureau (set up in March 1865 to provide food, schools, medical care and legal help), passed the **Civil Rights Act of 1866**, and then, over Johnson's veto, the Reconstruction Acts of 1867, which divided the South into military districts and required southern states to write new constitutions with Black male suffrage. Johnson was impeached in 1868 (the first president to be), and survived in the Senate by a single vote. Congress also proposed two constitutional amendments, which were, together with the Thirteenth, a second founding of the United States:

- The **Fourteenth Amendment** (1868) defined citizenship to include all persons born in the country, overturning *Dred Scott*; guaranteed "equal protection of the laws" and "due process"; and applied these to the states.
- The **Fifteenth Amendment** (1870) prohibited denial of the vote on grounds of "race, colour, or previous condition of servitude".

For a few remarkable years, there was something that had never existed anywhere: a multiracial democracy in a former slave society. Black men voted, served on juries, and held office. Between 1870 and 1877, some 1,500 Black men held public office in the South, from local posts to Congress; sixteen were elected to the US Congress during Reconstruction. **Hiram Revels**, a Black minister from Mississippi, took in 1870 the Senate seat that Jefferson Davis had vacated nine years earlier. Southern states created their first public school systems, mostly open to Black and white children (albeit separately); freed people built churches and colleges, and flocked to learn to read. They also tried, mostly in vain, to acquire land. General Sherman's Field Order 15, issuing "forty acres" along the coast, was reversed by Johnson, and the "forty acres and a mule" was never delivered. Without land, most freed people ended up in **sharecropping**, working another man's land for a share of the crop, in debt to the landlord's store, in an arrangement that kept many bound to the soil for generations.

### The backlash

The reaction was ferocious. White supremacist paramilitaries, among them the **Ku Klux Klan**, founded in Pulaski, Tennessee, by Confederate veterans in 1865 or 1866, and later groups like the White League and Red Shirts, murdered Black and white Republican officials and terrorised voters. In Colfax, Louisiana, in April 1873, white militiamen killed somewhere between 60 and 150 Black men, many of them after they had surrendered. President Grant, to his credit, used the new Department of Justice and the Enforcement Acts of 1870–71 to crush the Klan in South Carolina, and for a time it worked. But Northern will was fading. The country slid into an economic depression in 1873; scandals tainted Grant's administration; Northern voters tired of the "Southern question".

The end came with the disputed presidential election of 1876, in which the Republican Rutherford B. Hayes lost the popular vote but claimed the White House after an electoral commission awarded him the disputed southern states. In what is known as the **Compromise of 1877**, Hayes withdrew the remaining federal troops from the South, and the southern "Redeemer" Democrats took control. Reconstruction was over.

## 9. The long consequences

### Jim Crow

What followed was not a return to slavery, but a new system of racial subordination. Over the next three decades southern states stripped Black men of the vote by means of poll taxes, literacy tests and "grandfather clauses" (Mississippi led in 1890), and legally mandated segregation in schools, trains, parks, hospitals and cemeteries. In *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896), the Supreme Court held that "separate but equal" facilities were constitutional, and for the next half-century "separate" was strictly enforced and "equal" was optional. Convict leasing allowed states to rent out prisoners, overwhelmingly Black, to mines and plantations, often in conditions as lethal as slavery. And thousands of Black men, women and children were lynched, often in public and with an audience.

At the same time, the **Lost Cause** took hold. Confederate veterans' organisations, and in particular the United Daughters of the Confederacy, raised statues, shaped school textbooks and promoted a mythologised memory of the war. Most of the monuments that still stand in the South were not erected in the immediate aftermath of the war, but decades later: a first great wave from about 1900 to the 1920s, a second in the 1950s and 1960s, as the civil rights movement began to challenge segregation. They were less about mourning than about asserting who held power.

North and South also agreed, in the late nineteenth century, to reconcile: veterans of both armies held joint reunions at Gettysburg (in 1913, for the 50th anniversary, and Woodrow Wilson, the first southern-born president since Andrew Johnson, spoke), and the price of national reconciliation was too often the sacrifice of the Black Americans whose freedom had been the war's greatest achievement.

### The Great Migration

Faced with violence, poverty and exclusion, Black southerners voted with their feet. Between about 1916 and 1970, some six million moved north and west, to Chicago, Detroit, New York, Los Angeles. This **Great Migration** changed the demographic, cultural and political map of the country: it gave the world Chicago blues and Harlem Renaissance literature, and also faced Black migrants with new forms of discrimination, in housing, in schools, in jobs.

### The second Reconstruction

The unfinished business of 1865 to 1877 was taken up again after the Second World War by a generation of Black Americans, and white allies, who demanded that the promises of the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments be honoured. *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) struck down school segregation; the Montgomery bus boycott, the sit-ins, the Freedom Rides and the March on Washington followed. In August 1963, Martin Luther King Jr stood on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial and began his speech with the words "Five score years ago", referring back to the Emancipation Proclamation, and calling the nation to make good on a "promissory note". The **Civil Rights Act of 1964** and the **Voting Rights Act of 1965** finally enforced, nearly a century late, what the Reconstruction amendments had promised. Historians call the civil rights era the "Second Reconstruction", and the phrase is apt.

### Compared with the rest of the world

It is worth pausing to put America's experience in context. Most slave societies ended slavery without a war. Britain did it by Act of Parliament in 1833, paying about £20 million in compensation, not to the enslaved but to the slaveholders, a sum so large that the loan raised to pay it was not fully repaid by British taxpayers until 2015. Brazil, the last in the Americas, abolished slavery in 1888 by law. Haiti made it by revolution. The United States, uniquely among large nations, did it by civil war, and without compensation. This tells us something about how entrenched slavery was in America, how large it had become as an economic interest, and how fiercely it had been defended.

## 10. What is still with us?

Here we come to the question that makes history matter. The Civil War is not "over" in the sense that it has left no traces. Let us survey some of them.

**The Constitution itself.** Three amendments, the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth, were direct products of the war. The Fourteenth, in particular, is arguably the most litigated part of the Constitution: its "equal protection" and "due process" clauses underpin a great many modern cases, from school desegregation to marriage equality to disputes over citizenship. When Americans argue today about birthright citizenship, they are arguing about a sentence written in 1866 to overturn *Dred Scott*.

**The relationship between the states and the federal government.** The question "who decides?", the heart of the antebellum fight, has not gone away. Debates about federal power over education, policing, voting, health, and the environment retain a strong flavour of the older arguments, even when the stakes are different. But the *right* to leave the Union has been closed. Secession is no longer a live constitutional option.

**Politics and geography.** Political scientists have found startling correlations between the distribution of slavery in 1860 and political attitudes today. A well-known 2016 study (Acharya, Blackwell and Sen, "The Political Legacy of American Slavery") found that white residents of southern counties that had a higher share of enslaved people in 1860 are today, on average, more conservative on race-related issues and more likely to identify as Republican, and argued that this reflects the persistence of the economic and political structures of slavery, handed down through generations, rather than something innate. The old "Solid South" voted Democratic for almost a century after the war, in reaction to Reconstruction and the Republican party of Lincoln; the political realignment after the civil rights era, which largely turned it Republican, is one of the great shifts in American politics. Whichever party you prefer, the point is that the map of American politics has deep roots.

**Wealth and inequality.** Slavery generated enormous wealth for owners, and it took the labour of millions without pay. After emancipation, most freed people received no land, and for the following century, Black Americans were excluded, by law and custom, from many of the pathways that built white family wealth: homeownership, GI Bill benefits, good schools, union jobs. The result is that the median white American household today has several times the wealth of the median Black household, a ratio that in recent Federal Reserve surveys has been around six to one. The debate over what, if anything, should be done about it, including discussion of reparations, is one of the most contentious in American life, and it is a debate that, in a direct line, traces back to 1865 and the unfulfilled promise of "forty acres".

**Memory, monuments and symbols.** In June 2015 a white supremacist murdered nine Black worshippers at the Emanuel AME Church in Charleston, a few kilometres from Fort Sumter. The killer had posed with the Confederate battle flag. In the aftermath, South Carolina took the flag down from the Statehouse grounds. In August 2017, a rally in Charlottesville, Virginia, ostensibly to protest the removal of a statue of Robert E. Lee, brought neo-Nazis and white nationalists onto the streets; a counter-protester, Heather Heyer, was killed. After the murder of George Floyd in 2020, many statues were removed, among them, in September 2021, the huge statue of Lee on Richmond's Monument Avenue. Debates continue over what to do with Confederate memorials, over the naming of military bases and schools, and over how history is taught. One may take any side of these debates; what cannot be said is that they are about the *past*. They are about who America thinks it is.

**Juneteenth and commemoration.** On the other side of the ledger, **Juneteenth** (19 June) became a federal holiday in 2021, the first new one since Martin Luther King Jr Day in 1983. Battlefields such as Gettysburg, Antietam and Vicksburg are among the best-preserved historic sites in the world; in a typical summer, tens of thousands of re-enactors, and millions of visitors, tread them.

**Policing, incarceration, and voting.** Historians have traced lines, contested but serious, from the Black Codes and convict leasing to aspects of modern criminal justice. And the question of who gets to vote, and how easy it is, the central issue of Reconstruction, remains a live and bitter fight, with each party accusing the other of undermining democracy.

**The American self-image.** Perhaps the deepest legacy is moral and civic. The Civil War forced America to confront the contradiction in its founding, a nation dedicated to liberty built partly on bondage, and led to a "new birth of freedom" that is still being worked out. It left a standing argument about whether the country's story is one of progress, redemption, or hypocrisy. All three, a good historian would say, are true at once.

### Imagined: a conversation at Gettysburg

*(Imagined. The guide and the visitor are invented.)*

> **Visitor:** It's so peaceful. It's hard to believe all that happened here.
>
> **Guide:** It's the peace that gets people. On the morning of the third of July, about this spot, there was an artillery barrage so loud they say people heard it in Pittsburgh, more than 200 km away.
>
> **Visitor:** Who won?
>
> **Guide:** The Union won the battle, in the sense that Lee left. But ask a different question: who won the *argument*? That's still going on. We keep the field exactly as it was so that you can stand here and ask it.
>
> **Visitor:** And the answer?
>
> **Guide:** I'm a guide, not a prophet. But I'd point you to the 272 words engraved on that wall, and say: ask whether we've yet become the nation they describe.

## 11. Epilogue: why the war still matters

What should we take from all this?

First, that the American Civil War was, at its heart, a war about slavery, and the attempts to disguise that fact were themselves part of the war's long aftermath. Honest history begins by looking at what people said at the time.

Second, that the war was not a simple morality play. Many good people were on the Union side for bad reasons, and many bad outcomes flowed from good intentions. Lincoln, the Great Emancipator, started out wanting to save the Union and tolerated slavery where it stood. Lee, a decent man in private life, fought for a cause whose foundation was human bondage. Grant, a failure at everything but war, turned out to be the Union's most important general and, later, an energetic defender of Black civil rights. Understanding a historical event does not require choosing saints and villains. But it does require recognising where the evil lay.

Third, that the people who suffered most, and who did the most to win the war's moral victory, were those who are least likely to be remembered in the grand narratives: the enslaved, who ran, worked, scouted, fought and testified; the Black soldiers; the women who nursed; the poor who died. Historians have spent the past half-century rediscovering them, and the result is a richer, stranger, more human story than the one our grandparents were taught.

And fourth, that the "unfinished work" Lincoln spoke of at Gettysburg is still under way. The war settled that the Union would last and that slavery would end. It did not settle what equality means, or how to secure it, or who gets to decide. Those are the questions the Civil War left to us, and they are, in a very real way, the questions of American life.

If you ever visit, go to Gettysburg early in the morning, when the fog lies in the hollows and the stone walls run off into the trees. Stand on Cemetery Ridge and look across at Seminary Ridge, about a kilometre away, where Pickett's men began their march. It is a gentle walk. It is also, in its way, the most eloquent argument in the history of democracy about what it costs when a free country refuses to look at what it is.

## Further reading

For those who wish to read more, in a range of lengths and tones:

- **James M. McPherson**, *Battle Cry of Freedom* (1988), the standard single-volume history, Pulitzer Prize-winning and wonderfully readable.
- **Eric Foner**, *The Fiery Trial: Abraham Lincoln and American Slavery* (2010), on Lincoln's evolving views; and *Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution, 1863–1877* (1988).
- **Ta-Nehisi Coates**, *We Were Eight Years in Power* (2017), essays that include a long argument about the legacy of the era.
- **Drew Gilpin Faust**, *This Republic of Suffering: Death and the American Civil War* (2008), on how the nation coped with mass death.
- **Ulysses S. Grant**, *Personal Memoirs* (1885), unfussy and, by consensus, one of the finest military memoirs ever written.
- **Frederick Douglass**, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave* (1845) and *Life and Times of Frederick Douglass* (1881).
- **David W. Blight**, *Race and Reunion: The Civil War in American Memory* (2001), on the Lost Cause and the politics of remembering.
- **Amanda Foreman**, *A World on Fire: Britain's Crucial Role in the American Civil War* (2010), for the British angle, from the Trent Affair to the *Alabama*.
