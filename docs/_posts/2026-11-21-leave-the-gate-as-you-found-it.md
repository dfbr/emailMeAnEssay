## Before We Set Off

There is a moment, familiar to everyone who has moved from a city to the countryside, when you realise that you have been speaking English all your life and yet cannot follow a conversation in a farmyard. Someone says the tups went in on Bonfire Night, that the theaves scanned well, that they've got a few cades on the bottle, that the stores were dear at the mart and that the cob's gone footy again because he got on the spring grass. Every word is English. None of it makes sense.

This essay is a story about crossing that gap. It follows Alex Hartley, a remote-working IT man who has lived his whole life in Manchester, through his first year on a nine-acre smallholding on the Welsh border in south-west Shropshire. Alex, his partner Jo, their neighbour Gwen Pryce and everyone else in it are invented, as is the hamlet of Upper Hope where they live. Everything else is meant to be true: the farming practices, the animals, the laws, the seasons and, above all, the words. The towns, the breeds and the agricultural show are real.

Words in **bold** are terms worth learning. Most are explained as they appear; all of them are gathered into a phrasebook at the end, so that you can check what a **hogg** is without hunting back through twelve months of mud. Measurements are metric, except where the countryside itself stubbornly refuses to be: British land is still bought and sold in **acres** (one acre is about 0.4 hectares), and horses are still measured in **hands**.

Pull on your wellies. We are going to start in October, because that is when Alex arrived, and because, as Gwen would point out, the farming year doesn't begin in January anyway.

## October: Completion Day

The removal lorry could not get up the lane.

This was Alex's first lesson, and it arrived before he had even unlocked the front door. The lane to Brook Cottage was what the estate agent had called "a quiet country road", which turned out to mean a single strip of tarmac about the width of a Transit van, with grass growing up the middle, high hedges on both sides and, every few hundred metres, a little bulge in the verge called a **passing place**, where one vehicle pulls in to let another by. (The etiquette: whoever is nearer the passing place reverses to it, and everyone raises a finger from the steering wheel in thanks.) The removal men, who were from Stockport and had not been warned, took one look at the overhanging oak at the bottom and declared that the lorry was going no further. The next three hours were spent ferrying a life's worth of furniture up the hill in a borrowed pick-up.

"You'll want to get that tree cut back," said a voice from the other side of the hedge. "But not till September. Birds."

The voice belonged to a small woman in her seventies wearing a waxed jacket held together with **baler twine**, the orange or blue plastic string that comes off bales of hay and straw and that, Alex would discover, holds most of rural Britain together. She introduced herself as Gwen Pryce from Cefn Farm, the next holding along. She had come, she said, to see who had bought old Mr Davies's place, and whether they were going to put up a trampoline.

They were not. Alex said so, a little too quickly, and Gwen seemed satisfied.

### The things nobody tells you about the house

Brook Cottage was a stone house, about two hundred years old, with walls nearly a metre thick and windows that let in light reluctantly. It was not connected to the gas main, because there was no gas main for kilometres. Heating came from an **oil-fired boiler**, fed from a green plastic tank of **kerosene** (heating oil, often called "28-second" after an old measure of its viscosity) at the end of the garden. Nobody delivers kerosene daily. You order a few thousand litres at a time, watch the price as anxiously as a stock trader, and learn that many villages run an **oil-buying club** that bulk-orders for everyone to win a discount. Run out in a January cold snap and you will wait days for a lorry that, like the removal van, may not fit up the lane.

The kitchen held a **Rayburn**, a cast-iron range cooker that also heated the water: the slightly humbler cousin of the **Aga**, that famous symbol of the comfortable country kitchen. In the living room was a **wood burner**, which was not decoration but infrastructure. When the power went, which it did that first winter for eleven hours after a storm brought a tree down on an overhead line, the wood burner was the only source of heat, and the stack of **logs** in the lean-to was suddenly worth more than the car. Logs, Alex learnt, come **seasoned** (air-dried for a year or two) or **kiln-dried**; burning **green**, or unseasoned, wood produces smoke, tar and very little heat.

The mobile signal, Alex found, existed only in the upstairs bathroom, standing on the edge of the bath. Then there was the drainage. Like roughly one in twenty households in England, Brook Cottage was not connected to the mains sewer. Its waste went into a **septic tank** buried in the garden, where solids settle and bacteria digest them, with the liquid soaking away through a **drainage field** of perforated pipes. The tank needs **desludging** by a tanker every year or two. You quickly learn not to pour bleach down the sink, because it kills the bacteria that do the work. The surveyor had been very interested in whether the tank discharged directly into the brook, because since 2020 the **general binding rules** in England have required tanks that do so to be upgraded or replaced, usually with a small **sewage treatment plant**. To Alex's relief, it didn't.

### Nine acres, and what to call them

The land was the reason they had come. Nine acres, a little over 3.6 hectares, split into three fields by **hedges** of hawthorn, blackthorn and hazel, some of them clearly centuries old. Alex had been calling them "the fields". Gwen, walking him round on the second day, called them by name.

"That's the Cae Mawr, the big field, though it's not very big. That's Brook Meadow, wet at the bottom. And this is the Banky Piece. Mr Davies always called it the Banky Piece. You can call it what you like, but the post'll still come to Brook Cottage."

Field names are one of the great hidden treasures of the English countryside. Almost every field on old farms has one, recorded on nineteenth-century **tithe maps**, and they describe what the field was like or used for: Ten Acre, Long Meadow, Starve Acre, Hungry Hill, Kiln Field, Gallows Piece. Here on the Welsh border, many are part-Welsh. *Cae* means field; *mawr* means big.

Gwen also corrected his vocabulary for the fields themselves. A **paddock** is a small enclosed field, usually near the house, used for horses or a few animals. **Pasture** is grassland grazed by livestock. A **meadow**, strictly, is grassland cut for hay rather than grazed, though people use the word loosely. A **ley** is a field sown with grass (and often clover) for a few years as part of a rotation, as opposed to **permanent pasture**, which has been grass as long as anyone can remember. Brook Meadow, with its knapweed, yellow rattle and buttercups, was permanent pasture of a kind that is now rare, and Gwen advised him not to plough it or throw fertiliser on it if he wanted the wildflowers to survive.

They walked the boundaries, and Gwen named things as they went. The **gateway**, the muddy patch inside a field gate, was **poached**: churned to a quagmire by hooves. The fence along the lane was **stock-proof**: tight enough and high enough to keep animals in, which is a much higher bar than it sounds. The fence along the brook was not: it was a single strand of rusting **barbed wire** that a determined sheep would treat as a mild suggestion. A **sheep netting** fence (woven wire mesh, about 80 cm high, usually with a strand of barbed or plain wire above) would need to go up before any sheep went in. The **water trough** in the corner was filled from a pipe controlled by a **ballcock**, like the one in a lavatory cistern, and it was cracked.

"You've got a lot to do," said Gwen, cheerfully. "What are you going to keep?"

Alex had a list. Jo wanted a horse. They both wanted chickens. Alex had a romantic notion of a few sheep. Cattle, he admitted, frightened him.

"Good," said Gwen. "They should."

## November: Learning to Speak Sheep

![Clun Forest sheep, with the dark brown faces typical of the breed, which takes its name from the market town of Clun in the south-west corner of Shropshire.](/assets/essays/leave-the-gate-as-you-found-it/images/cluns.jpg)
*Clun Forest sheep. Photo: Azuschlag, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

By November, Gwen had decided that the newcomers were worth educating. The method was simple: Alex was invited to help, given a job slightly beyond his competence, and corrected loudly.

The first job was at Cefn Farm, where Gwen and her son Rhys kept about four hundred **ewes** (adult female sheep that have had lambs) and a herd of thirty **suckler cows**, which we will come to. The sheep were mostly **Clun Forests**, the local breed: dark brown faces, alert ears, a woolly topknot, and a reputation for being hardy, long-lived and good mothers. The breed takes its name from the little market town of Clun a few kilometres away, and has been recorded in this corner of Shropshire since at least the early nineteenth century; its breeders' society was formed in 1925. Shropshire, it turned out, punches above its weight in sheep. The county's other breed, the **Shropshire**, developed in the 1840s, and its breeders founded what is generally credited as the world's first flock book society for sheep in 1882.

### Tupping time

The job that day was **tupping**. Up here, a male sheep kept for breeding is a **tup**; in much of southern England he would be a **ram**. A castrated male is a **wether**. In early November, the tups were going in with the ewes, and Alex's job was to hold each tup while Rhys buckled on a **raddle harness**, a leather strap across the chest that holds a block of coloured wax crayon against the tup's **brisket** (the lower part of the chest).

"Every ewe he serves gets a coloured backside," said Rhys. "Then we know who's been tupped, and when."

The colours were the clever part. A ewe comes into season, or **on heat**, roughly every seventeen days. Rhys would start with yellow, switch to orange after about sixteen days, then red, then blue. At a glance, walking through the flock in December, you could tell which ewes had been served in the first cycle (and would lamb first), which in the second, and which had not **held** to the tup and come back on heat. Ewes with a stack of colours were **repeating**: a warning sign. Some farmers use the same trick with colour sprays on the ewes rather than crayons on the tup. The marks also show if a tup is lazy, lame or infertile: if all the ewes are coming back on heat, the problem is probably him.

The pregnancy of a ewe lasts about five months (roughly 147 days). Gwen's rule of thumb was that if the tups went in on Bonfire Night, 5 November, lambing would start around April Fool's Day. Lowland flocks with indoor lambing sheds often tup earlier and lamb in January or February, to have lambs ready for the lucrative Easter market; hill flocks lamb later, outdoors, when there is some grass for the ewes to eat.

### A sheep's life, in words

Over tea afterwards, which in a farmhouse kitchen means a mug of strong tea and a slab of something home-made at about eleven in the morning, Gwen gave Alex the vocabulary of a sheep's life. He wrote it on the back of an envelope, which is the traditional way.

A newborn is a **lamb**, obviously. A **ewe lamb** is female, a **tup lamb** or **ram lamb** male. Once weaned and until its first shearing, a young sheep is a **hogg** or **hogget** (spellings vary). Lamb meat from a hogget, eaten in its first winter and spring, is also called hogget, and many chefs prefer its flavour. After the first shearing it becomes a **shearling**; up north a young female might be a **gimmer**. A young ewe that has had her first lamb is a **first-time lamber**, and an older ewe that is sold off a hill farm, where the conditions are hard, to a lowland farm where she can have a few more years of breeding, is a **draft ewe**. A ewe that has lost teeth is **broken-mouthed**, which, since sheep have no top front teeth and graze by gripping grass between their bottom teeth and a hard **dental pad**, matters a great deal for her ability to eat. Farmers age sheep by their teeth: a two-tooth, a four-tooth, a six-tooth and then **full-mouthed**.

A group of ewes in a field is a **flock**. A **mob** is a group moved together. A sheep that has rolled onto its back and can't get up, which happens to heavy, woolly or pregnant ewes surprisingly often, is **cast** (or **rigged**, **riggwelted** or **kessen** depending on where you are), and she can die within hours as gases build up in her stomach. If you see one while out walking, gently rolling her back onto her feet is a genuine kindness.

"Sheep," Gwen said, "spend their whole lives looking for new ways to die. That's not a joke. It's husbandry."

Up on the open hills of Wales and the north, she explained, flocks are **hefted**: each group of ewes knows its own patch of unfenced mountain, its **heft**, and stays on it, and the knowledge passes from ewe to lamb down the generations. A hefted flock is worth far more than its individual sheep, because if it is wiped out by disease, the knowledge of the hill dies with it. That was one of the quieter tragedies of the 2001 foot-and-mouth epidemic.

### Alex buys some sheep

By the end of the morning Alex had agreed to buy six of Gwen's draft ewes, older Cluns that were past their best for a commercial flock but had a few seasons left in them. They would go in with one of Gwen's tups for a month and come home in December.

But first there was paperwork, because in Britain every farm animal is traceable, a system strengthened enormously after the disasters of BSE in the 1990s and foot-and-mouth in 2001. Before anyone keeps sheep, goats, pigs or cattle in England, even one pet lamb, the land must have a **county parish holding number**, or **CPH**, issued by the **Rural Payments Agency** (RPA). It looks like 12/345/6789: county, parish, holding. The keeper also needs a **flock mark** from the **Animal and Plant Health Agency** (APHA), which goes on the animals' **ear tags**. Sheep in England must be identified with ear tags (most now carry an electronic chip, known as **EID**), and every movement on or off a holding must be recorded in the keeper's **holding register**, accompanied by a **movement document**, and reported online through the **Livestock Information Service**.

And every time sheep, cattle or goats arrive on a holding, the other animals there go into a six-day **standstill**: none may leave for six days, so that if a disease arrives with the newcomers it has a chance to show itself before it spreads. Pigs trigger a longer one. To a newcomer it feels like bureaucracy. To a farmer who remembers the burning pyres of 2001, it is common sense.

### Grass keep and winter tack

There was one more arrangement. Alex had more grass than six ewes could eat, and Gwen had more sheep than grass. So for the winter, Gwen's ewe lambs would graze the Cae Mawr. On the Welsh border, winter grazing like this is called **tack**: the sheep go "on tack". More generally, renting out grass by the season is **grass keep** or a **grazing licence**. Alex was advised, by a land agent in Bishop's Castle, to keep the arrangement formal, written and for less than a year, because a long informal arrangement can accidentally create an **agricultural tenancy** with legal protections that are very hard to undo.

"Is that a thing people do?" he asked. "Fall out over grass?"

The land agent smiled the smile of a man who had built a career on people falling out over grass.

## December: Footpaths, Dogs and Other People

The first walkers appeared on a Sunday morning in December, coming over the stile from Gwen's land, crossing the Banky Piece diagonally and heading for the gate onto the lane. Alex watched them from the kitchen window with the indignation of a man who has recently paid a great deal of money for some fields.

Jo, who had read the conveyancing documents more carefully than he had, explained. A **public footpath** crossed their land. It was marked on the **definitive map** held by Shropshire Council, and the walkers had every right to be there.

### Rights of way, in four colours

England and Wales have more than 200,000 kilometres of **public rights of way**, a network built up over centuries of people walking to church, market and work, and now one of the great free treasures of British life. They come in four types, and the **waymarks** (little arrows on posts and gates) are colour-coded:

- A **public footpath** (yellow arrow) is for walkers only.
- A **bridleway** (blue arrow) is for walkers, horse riders and cyclists.
- A **restricted byway** (plum or purple arrow) adds horse-drawn carriages.
- A **byway open to all traffic**, or **BOAT** (red arrow), allows motor vehicles too.

Landowners own the land beneath the path, but must keep the route open and must not obstruct it, put up misleading signs or lock gates across it. A farmer may plough a cross-field path, but must restore it within a short set time. A **stile** is a step or ladder for climbing over a fence or wall; a **kissing gate** is a small gate swinging inside a U- or V-shaped enclosure, which lets one person through at a time but stops livestock.

In open country there is a wider right too. Under the **Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000**, usually just called **CRoW**, walkers can roam freely across mapped **access land**: mountain, moor, heath, down and registered common land. Nearby, the heather-topped ridge of the Long Mynd and the jagged quartzite tors of the Stiperstones are both access land.

### The Countryside Code

What the walkers owe in return is set out in the **Countryside Code**, first published in the 1950s and revised many times since. Its spirit is "respect, protect, enjoy", and its most famous line is the one every child in the country once learnt: **leave gates and property as you find them**. Note: not "close all gates". A farmer may have left a gate open deliberately, so that animals can reach water in the next field. Closing it can be as harmful as leaving a shut gate open.

Gwen had her own, shorter version. "Shut what you opened, open what you shut, don't feed my animals, take your rubbish home and keep that dog on a lead."

### Dogs and livestock

The dog rule is not a matter of manners. In the spring that Alex arrived, a law that had stood almost unchanged since 1953 was strengthened. The **Dogs (Protection of Livestock) (Amendment) Act 2025** came into force in March 2026 in England and Wales. It replaced the old maximum fine of £1,000 with an unlimited fine, gave the police new powers to seize dogs and take samples from them, extended the offence to attacks on roads and paths, and brought llamas and alpacas into the list of protected animals alongside sheep, cattle, goats and poultry.

The offence is **livestock worrying**, and "worrying" is one of those countryside words that means something much more serious than it sounds. It covers attacking animals, but also chasing them in a way that could cause injury, suffering or loss. A pregnant ewe chased by a playful spaniel may **abort** her lambs days later, or die of stress. Sheep will crowd into a corner and smother each other, or bolt through a fence onto a road. Farmers in England and Wales may, in limited circumstances, lawfully shoot a dog that is worrying their livestock, and every year some do, an outcome that is devastating for everyone involved.

Alex and Jo, who had a nervous rescue lurcher called Biscuit, bought a longer lead.

## January: A Horse Called Bramble

![Horses grazing at the edge of a field. A horse kept out at grass all or most of the time is "living out"; putting it out in the morning is "turning out".](/assets/essays/leave-the-gate-as-you-found-it/images/horses.jpg)
*Field with horses. Photo: Mx. Granger, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Jo had ridden as a teenager, at a riding school on the edge of Manchester, and had wanted her own horse for twenty-five years. Gwen, who regarded horses as expensive animals that eat grass better spent on sheep, sent her to Sandra.

Sandra ran a **livery yard** three kilometres away: a place where people who don't have their own land keep their horses, for a fee. Liveries come in grades. On **DIY livery** you rent a stable and some grazing and do all the work yourself. On **part livery** the yard does some jobs, such as turning the horse out in the morning and bringing it in at night. On **full livery** the yard does everything, and you turn up to ride, at a cost that would make a city parking space look cheap. There is also **grass livery**, where the horse simply lives in a field.

"Before you buy anything," Sandra said, "come and muck out for a month. If you still want a horse after that, you'll do."

### Mucking out

**Mucking out** is the daily job of cleaning a stable: removing the droppings and the wet, soiled bedding, and putting down fresh. Bedding might be **straw**, **wood shavings**, **wood pellets** or rubber **stable mats** with a thin layer on top. Some people use **deep litter**, where clean bedding is added on top of the old and the whole lot is dug out every few weeks; others do a full muck-out every day. **Skipping out** is the quick version: picking up the droppings with a fork into a **skip** (a plastic basket) several times a day. Everything goes onto the **muck heap**, which steams gently on cold mornings and, once rotted, becomes the best compost a gardener could want.

Jo learnt to fill a **haynet** (a string bag of hay, hung up so that the horse eats it slowly and without trampling it), to scrub water buckets, to **poo-pick** the paddocks (removing droppings from the field, partly for tidiness and partly to break the life cycle of worms), and to tell the difference between hay and straw. This last was surprisingly important.

**Hay** is grass that has been cut, dried in the field and baled. It is food. **Straw** is the dried, hollow stalks left over after a cereal crop such as wheat or barley has been harvested for its grain. It is mostly bedding, though some animals eat it as roughage. They look similar to a townie. They are not interchangeable. "If you ever tell a farmer you've bought straw for your horse's dinner," Sandra said, "he'll sell you as much as you want and laugh about you in the pub."

### Buying Bramble

At the end of the month Jo still wanted a horse, and Sandra helped her find one: Bramble, a twelve-year-old bay **cob** gelding, 14.2 **hands**.

Each of those words is worth unpacking. Horses are measured from the ground to the **withers**, the ridge at the base of the neck between the shoulder blades, in hands. One hand is four inches, about 10.2 centimetres, and the figure after the point is extra inches, not a decimal: 14.2 hands, written **14.2hh** (hands high), is 14 × 4 + 2 = 58 inches, or about 147 centimetres. That is the traditional upper limit for a **pony**; anything taller is a horse, though breed matters too.

A **cob** is not a breed but a type: a stocky, short-legged, sensible, strong-boned animal, good at carrying adults and famous for being able to live on fresh air (and for getting fat on anything more). A **bay** horse has a brown body with a black mane, tail and lower legs. A **chestnut** is reddish-gold all over; a **grey** is born dark and lightens with age; **piebald** is black and white, **skewbald** brown and white.

As for the sex of horses: a **foal** is a baby; a **colt** is a young male, a **filly** a young female; a **stallion** is an uncastrated adult male, a **mare** an adult female, and a **gelding** a castrated male. Most riding horses in Britain are mares or geldings.

Before handing over money, Jo paid a vet to carry out a **pre-purchase examination**, which is the horse world's equivalent of a house survey: heart, lungs, eyes, and a careful look at whether the horse is **sound** (walking and trotting evenly) or **lame**. Bramble came with his **passport**, a document every horse, pony and donkey in Britain must have, recording its identity, and with a **microchip** in his neck. Since 2021, every equine in England has had to be microchipped, and the details sit on a national database. The passport also records whether the horse may ever enter the human food chain, which determines which medicines a vet may give it.

### Tack, rugs and the art of being outside

Jo then discovered that buying the horse was the cheap bit. The equipment for riding is called **tack**, and the **tack room** is where it lives: a **saddle**, a **bridle** (the headgear with the **bit** that goes in the horse's mouth and the **reins** attached to it), a **girth** (the strap holding the saddle on), a **numnah** or **saddle pad** underneath it, and a **headcollar** and **lead rope** for leading and tying up. A horse is **tacked up** before riding and **untacked** afterwards.

Then there were **rugs**: horse blankets, in an astonishing variety. A **turnout rug** is waterproof, for wearing in the field. A **stable rug** is for indoors. A **fleece** or **cooler** dries off a sweaty horse. Rugs come in weights, measured by the grams of filling, from **no-fill** to **heavyweight**, and a great deal of the conversation on any livery yard in winter is about who has rugged their horse too heavily and who too lightly. Bramble, being a cob with a thick winter coat, needed less than most; overheating a horse in a heavy rug is a common newcomer's mistake.

Bramble lived out on Brook Meadow during the day and came into a small **field shelter**, a three-sided timber building, at night, with a patch of **hardstanding** (concrete or compacted stone) in front so the gateway didn't turn into soup. Even so, by late January the gateway was **poached** knee-deep, and Bramble developed **mud fever**, a bacterial skin infection of the lower legs caused by constantly wet, muddy skin, which leaves scabs and sore heels. Jo learnt to keep his legs clean and dry, and that **feathers**, the long hair round the lower legs of heavy horses and some cobs, can both protect the skin and hide the problem.

### The farrier

Every six to eight weeks, the **farrier** came. A horse's hoof grows continuously, like a fingernail, and needs trimming. A horse that is ridden on roads may wear **shoes**, which are made of steel, shaped to the foot, and nailed into the insensitive outer wall of the hoof. Horses that don't need them go **barefoot**, though they still need trimming.

![A farrier at work at the West Show in Jersey, shaping a shoe against the hoof. In Britain, only farriers registered under the Farriers (Registration) Act 1975 may shoe a horse.](/assets/essays/leave-the-gate-as-you-found-it/images/farrier.jpg)
*Farrier at the West Show, Jersey, 2010. Photo: Man vyi, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The farrier, a cheerful man called Dai with forearms like hams and a mobile forge in the back of his van, explained what he was doing as he worked. He took off the old shoes, trimmed the overgrown hoof with **nippers** and a **rasp**, heated a new shoe until it glowed, and pressed it briefly against the hoof, which produced a cloud of acrid white smoke and a smell that Alex never forgot. This **hot shoeing** shows the farrier exactly where the shoe touches, so he can shape it to fit. The hoof wall has no nerves, so the horse feels no pain. Dai then nailed the shoe on, twisted off the protruding nail ends and bent them over to form **clenches**, and finished the hoof with the rasp.

Alex learnt the parts of the foot: the hard outer **wall**; the **sole** underneath; and the rubbery, V-shaped **frog** in the middle, which helps absorb shock and pump blood. He learnt the old saying "no foot, no horse". And he learnt, from Dai, that in Britain shoeing a horse is a regulated craft: under the **Farriers (Registration) Act 1975**, only a registered farrier may do it, after a long apprenticeship. Anyone may trim a hoof; only a farrier may shoe one.

"What's the worst thing that happens to feet?" Alex asked.

"Laminitis," said Dai, without hesitation. "Watch him in the spring."

He meant the spring grass. **Laminitis** is inflammation of the **laminae**, the delicate tissues that bond the hoof wall to the bone inside. It is excruciating and can be crippling, and one of its most common triggers is a sudden flush of sugar-rich spring or autumn grass, especially in fat, hardy types like cobs and native ponies. A laminitic horse stands rocked back on its heels to take weight off its toes and is said to be **footy** or **foot-sore**. Prevention means **strip-grazing** with electric fencing, a **grazing muzzle**, or keeping the horse on a **track system** or **dry lot** with little grass. Jo bought the muzzle in February, just in case.

### Feed, worms and the other things that matter

A good-doer like Bramble needed little more than hay and grass. Horses in harder work get **hard feed** or **concentrates** (mixes, cubes or chaff with oil and cereal), but the golden rules of horse feeding are old and simple: plenty of **forage** (hay, haylage or grass), little and often, plenty of fresh water, and no sudden changes. The horse's gut is long, delicate and designed for grazing all day. **Colic**, which simply means abdominal pain, can range from mild discomfort to a twisted gut needing emergency surgery, and is the thing horse owners fear most. Jo learnt the signs: a horse pawing the ground, looking at its flanks, lying down and getting up repeatedly, or rolling violently.

She also learnt that blanket **worming**, giving every horse a dose of wormer every few months, has given way to a smarter approach because worms have developed resistance to the drugs. Instead, a sample of droppings is sent off for a **faecal egg count**, and the horse is treated only when it needs it.

Finally, Sandra gave her a lesson in **ragwort**: a tall, yellow-flowered weed common on verges and poor pasture. It contains toxins that damage the liver, cumulatively and irreversibly. Horses usually avoid it growing, because it tastes bitter, but dried in hay it loses its bitterness while keeping its poison. Hence the sight, every summer, of horse owners pulling it out by the roots with gloves and a special fork. Ragwort is also a vital food plant for dozens of insects, notably the black-and-red caterpillars of the cinnabar moth, so the aim is control near horses and hay fields, not extermination everywhere.

## February: The Hens Arrive

![A hen with her chicks. A hen that wants to sit on eggs and hatch them is "broody"; without a cockerel, she may sit faithfully on eggs that will never hatch.](/assets/essays/leave-the-gate-as-you-found-it/images/hen.jpg)
*A free-range hen with chicks. Photo: safaritravelplus, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Chickens, everyone told them, were the gateway drug of smallholding. Get a few hens and within a year you will have ducks, then geese, then a pig.

Alex did the research the way he did everything, with a spreadsheet, and Gwen laughed at him, and then sent him to a poultry breeder near Craven Arms who sold **point-of-lay** hybrids.

### The language of the henhouse

A female chicken under a year old is a **pullet**; an adult is a **hen**. A young male is a **cockerel**, and an adult male is a **cock** or **rooster** (Americans prefer "rooster"; British keepers mostly say "cockerel" whatever his age). **Point-of-lay**, abbreviated **POL**, means a pullet of about sixteen to twenty-two weeks old that is just about to start laying. A **hybrid** is a commercial cross bred for egg production; the best can lay around three hundred eggs in their first year, but they wear out sooner. **Pure breeds**, such as the Light Sussex, the Marans with its dark chocolate eggs, or the fluffy, stubborn Orpington, lay fewer eggs but are often hardier, longer-lived and more beautiful. **Bantams** are miniature chickens. **Layers** are hens kept for eggs; **broilers** or **table birds** are kept for meat.

Hens do not need a cockerel to lay eggs. This came as news to Alex, and also to a surprising number of his colleagues. A cockerel is only necessary if you want **fertile** eggs to hatch chicks. He also crows, at dawn and frequently at other times, and in a hamlet as quiet as Upper Hope, Gwen observed, a cockerel was a good way to find out what your neighbours really thought of you.

They bought six hybrid pullets: two black, two brown, two speckled. Alex named them after Linux distributions. Jo did not speak to him for an hour.

### Housing

The hens lived in a **hen house** (the word **coop** is also used, though some keepers reserve it for a small cage for a broody hen and her chicks). A good house needs **perches** for roosting, since chickens sleep perched off the ground, set higher than the **nest boxes**, where they lay; otherwise they sleep in the nest boxes and foul them. It needs ventilation without draughts. And it needs a **pop-hole**, the small door the hens use to go in and out, which many keepers now fit with an **automatic door** that opens at dawn and closes at dusk.

The run was protected by **electric poultry netting**, because of the fox. Every chicken keeper in Britain has a fox story, and most of them end badly. A fox will take hens in broad daylight, will dig under fences, will return to a run night after night to test it, and, notoriously, may kill every bird in a henhouse rather than just one. The usual explanation is that the panicked flapping of trapped hens keeps triggering the fox's instinct to kill. "Shut them in every night," said Gwen. "Every night. The one night you forget is the night he comes."

### The paperwork, again

Since 1 October 2024, everyone in England and Wales who keeps poultry, even a single hen in a back garden, has been legally required to register with the Animal and Plant Health Agency, within a month of getting the birds. The reason is **avian influenza**, or **bird flu**. When outbreaks strike, the government declares **protection zones** and **surveillance zones** around infected premises, and needs to know where every flock is in order to warn keepers and control the spread. In recent winters it has also, more than once, ordered poultry across all or part of England to be kept indoors, a **housing measure**, to keep them away from infected wild birds.

The hens had barely arrived when the advice came round to tighten **biosecurity**: keep feed and water under cover so it doesn't attract wild birds, especially ducks and geese; use a disinfectant **foot dip** at the run's gate; don't let visitors wander in and out. It sounded excessive for six hens. Then Alex read how quickly the virus can wipe out a flock, and bought the foot dip.

### Life with hens

The hens ate **layers' pellets**, a complete feed with the right protein and calcium for egg production. They had a separate pot of **grit**, partly soluble **oyster shell** for calcium, and partly insoluble **flint grit**, which they store in the **gizzard**, a muscular part of the stomach, to grind their food, since chickens have no teeth. **Mixed corn** was a treat, scattered in the afternoon, which the hens greeted with the joy of a pub at last orders. Kitchen scraps, Alex was surprised to learn, are illegal to feed to farmed animals in Britain, including backyard hens, because of the risk of spreading disease. That rule exists largely because of foot-and-mouth.

He learnt the **pecking order**, which is a real thing: every flock has a strict hierarchy, and a newcomer has to fight her way into it. He learnt that hens lay far fewer eggs in winter, because laying is triggered by day length, and that once a year, usually in autumn, they go through a **moult**, losing and regrowing their feathers, during which they look dreadful and stop laying altogether. He learnt that egg-laying takes roughly a day per egg, so a good hen lays most days but not all.

He learnt about **red mite**: tiny blood-sucking mites that hide in crevices in the hen house by day and crawl out at night to feed on the sleeping birds. Badly infested hens become pale and anaemic and stop laying. The test is to wipe a white tissue along the underside of a perch at night: if it comes away streaked with red, you have a problem.

And in May, he learnt about **broodiness**. One of the brown hens, a hybrid called Mint, refused to leave the nest box. She fluffed herself up to twice her size, growled when approached, and pecked Alex's hand hard enough to draw blood. She was **broody**: hormonally determined to sit on eggs until they hatched. Without a cockerel, there was nothing to hatch. Gwen's advice was either to slip some fertile eggs under her from a neighbour with a cockerel, or to "break" the broodiness by putting her in a wire-floored cage for a few days to cool her down. They chose the eggs. Three weeks later (a chicken egg takes about 21 days to hatch), Mint walked out of the nest box with four chicks, and the gateway drug had done its work.

By summer there was a table at the end of the lane with a box of eggs and a jar for coins: an **honesty box**, one of the minor miracles of rural Britain, which works far more often than it should.

## March and April: Lambing

![A ewe and her lambs. Getting a ewe to accept and feed her lambs is called "mothering up".](/assets/essays/leave-the-gate-as-you-found-it/images/lambs.jpg)
*Ewe with lambs. Photo: Gerda Arendt, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

In January the ewes had been **scanned**. A contractor arrived with an ultrasound machine in a portable crate, and Gwen's four hundred ewes, plus Alex's six, were run through it one at a time, at remarkable speed. Each ewe was sprayed with a mark on her back: one colour for a **single**, another for **twins**, another for **triplets**, and none for those that were **empty** or **barren** (not in lamb). Combined with the raddle colours, this told Gwen roughly when each ewe would lamb and how many lambs to expect, so the ewes could be split into groups and fed accordingly. Ewes carrying twins and triplets need more feed in the last six weeks of pregnancy, when most of the lambs' growth happens. Ewes carrying singles need less, or the lamb grows too big to be born easily.

Gwen's **scanning percentage**, the number of lambs expected per hundred ewes put to the tup, was 185%. Alex's six old ewes scanned at nine lambs between them, which Gwen declared respectable "for pensioners".

### The shed at three in the morning

Gwen lambed indoors in a big open-sided shed, divided with hurdles. Alex volunteered for night shifts, partly to learn and partly because he was, as Gwen pointed out, the only person she knew who was used to staying up all night looking at screens.

The work went like this. A ewe close to lambing becomes restless, separates herself, paws at the straw, gets up and lies down. Then the **water bag** appears, followed, in a normal birth, by two front feet and a nose, "like a diver", as Gwen put it. Most ewes lamb on their own and should be left to get on with it. The shepherd intervenes only when something is wrong: a lamb presented backwards, a head back, a leg back, twins tangled together, or a ewe straining for too long without progress. **Assisting** a ewe means washing up, using plenty of lubricant, and gently sorting out the jigsaw of legs inside before helping the lamb out with the ewe's contractions. Alex watched Gwen do it a dozen times before she let him try. His first lamb came out backwards, blue and limp. Gwen cleared the mucus from its nose and mouth with her fingers, rubbed it hard with a handful of straw and poked a stalk up its nostril until it sneezed, coughed and shook its head, which is the moment every shepherd waits for.

Once a ewe had lambed, she and her lambs went into a **lambing pen** or **individual pen** (sometimes called a **jug**) for a day or two, so that she would bond with them. This is **mothering up**, and it does not always work. A ewe recognises her lambs mainly by smell. If she rejects one, the shepherd has tricks: rubbing the lamb with the ewe's own birth fluids, or, in the oldest trick of all, skinning a lamb that has died and tying its skin onto an orphan as a jacket, so that the bereaved mother smells her own lamb and accepts the stranger. This is **setting on** or **skinning on** a lamb. It sounds grim, and works remarkably well.

### The first few hours

The rules for a newborn lamb, Gwen said, were three: **colostrum**, warmth, and iodine.

Colostrum is the thick, yellow first milk that a ewe produces in the hours after birth. It is packed with energy and, above all, with antibodies, because a lamb is born with almost no immunity of its own and can only absorb these antibodies through its gut for a short window after birth. A lamb that doesn't get enough colostrum early on is in grave danger. If a lamb was too weak to suck, Gwen fed it with a **stomach tube**, a soft plastic tube slid down the throat into the stomach, attached to a syringe of colostrum. It looked terrifying and took her about thirty seconds. Alex practised on a dead lamb before he was allowed near a live one.

Warmth: a newborn lamb loses heat fast, and **hypothermia**, together with the starvation that so often goes with it, kills more newborn lambs than anything else. A chilled lamb was warmed in a **warming box**, a wooden box with a heater, but only after being given energy, because warming a lamb with no reserves can kill it.

Iodine: the lamb's **navel** was dipped in strong iodine solution to dry it and stop infection, which can otherwise travel up the umbilical cord and cause **joint ill** (infected, swollen joints) or **navel ill**.

### Cades, rings and tags

A lamb with no mother to feed it, whether orphaned, rejected, or the third of triplets from a ewe with only two teats, becomes a **cade lamb**, also called a **pet lamb** or **bottle lamb** (or **sock lamb** in some areas). Cades are fed on **lamb milk replacer** from a bottle or, on bigger farms, from an automatic feeder. They are adorable, and they follow you everywhere, and every farmer's child has loved one and wept over it. By April, Jo was bottle-feeding three of Gwen's cades in the kitchen of Brook Cottage, and Biscuit the lurcher had adopted them.

Within the first few days, most male lambs that would not be kept for breeding had a tight rubber ring put round the **scrotum** with a tool called an **elastrator**, a method of castration in which the tissue below the ring loses its blood supply and drops off after a few weeks. Many lambs also had their tails **docked** the same way, partly to reduce the risk of fly strike (more on which shortly). In England, the rubber ring may only be used without anaesthetic in the lamb's first week of life, and only enough tail must be left to cover the **vulva** of a ewe lamb or the **anus** of a ram lamb. These are not pleasant procedures, and welfare science has been steadily pushing practice towards pain relief and less docking. But they are part of the life of nearly every sheep in Britain, and a newcomer should know they happen.

Lambs are identified with ear tags before they leave the holding. Gwen's were electronic, and each one could be read with a wand at the market or the abattoir, linking the animal back to Cefn Farm.

Alex's ewes produced eight live lambs from nine. The ninth was born dead, a big single from the oldest ewe. Gwen took it away without comment and used its skin, a day later, to give a cade one of her own a mother. Alex thought about that for a long time afterwards.

### Turnout

By mid-April the ewes and lambs had gone out to grass, and on a bright morning Alex watched Gwen let her cattle out of the sheds where they had spent the winter. This is **turnout**, and if you have never seen cows turned out to spring grass after five months indoors, you have missed one of the great comic spectacles of rural Britain. Thirty half-tonne animals galloped, bucked, skidded and kicked their heels in the air like children let out of school. Some farms now open the day to the public. Gwen did not, on the grounds that she was busy.

## May and June: Grass, Fleece and Flies

![Wrapped round bales of silage in a cut field. The plastic seals out air so that the grass ferments rather than rots.](/assets/essays/leave-the-gate-as-you-found-it/images/silage.jpg)
*Field with silage bales. Photo: W.carter, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The countryside does not have a quiet season, but it has a loud one, and it begins in May when the grass starts to grow faster than anything can eat it.

Every livestock farmer in Britain spends the summer trying to save as much of that grass as possible for the winter, when nothing grows. There are three ways to do it, and Alex had to learn the difference before he could understand a single conversation in the pub.

**Hay** is grass cut and left to dry in the field for several days, turned to help it dry, and baled when it is dry enough not to go mouldy or, at worst, catch fire. Damp hay can genuinely heat up enough in a barn to burst into flames, which is why old barns sometimes burnt down in summer. Hay needs a run of dry, sunny days, which is why the expression "make hay while the sun shines" exists, and why British farmers watch weather forecasts with a fervour that city people reserve for football scores.

**Silage** is grass cut and gathered while still green and moist, then sealed away from air so that it **ferments**, a bit like sauerkraut, and keeps for months. It is stored either in a **clamp**, a huge heap in a walled concrete bunker, rolled flat by tractors and covered with black plastic sheets weighed down with old tyres, or in round **bales** wrapped in plastic film, the green, black or white marshmallows that dot British fields in summer. Silage smells powerfully of vinegar and tobacco, and cattle adore it. It doesn't need a long dry spell, which in a British summer is a decisive advantage. Most cattle farms now rely on it.

**Haylage** sits in between: grass wilted until it is drier than silage but wetter than hay, then baled and wrapped. Horse owners like it because it is less dusty than hay.

### Cutting silage

Gwen's first cut of silage happened in late May, when the grass was at its most nutritious, just before it went to seed. Like most small farmers, she used a **contractor**, a local business with the expensive machinery, who arrived with a convoy of enormous tractors and worked late into the night to beat the rain. The sequence went: **mowing** (cutting with a mower that leaves the grass in strips), **tedding** (spreading it out with a machine of spinning forks, a **tedder**, so that it wilts evenly), **rowing up** or **raking** (gathering it into long lines called **windrows** or **rows**), and then either **baling** and **wrapping**, or **picking up** with a forage harvester, which chops it and blows it into trailers that ferry it to the clamp. The whole lot might be done in a day. The lanes filled with tractors and trailers, the hedges grew a fringe of grass, and Alex learnt to pull into a gateway and wait.

A second cut followed in July, and on some farms a third in late summer.

Meanwhile, the hedges were left alone. Since 2024, under the **Management of Hedgerows (England) Regulations**, hedges on or next to farmland in England may not be cut or trimmed between 1 March and 31 August, with a few exceptions, to protect nesting birds. Which is why rural England has scruffy, overgrown hedges full of birdsong all summer, and why, in September, the lanes fill with tractors pulling **flails**: rotating arms of whirling blades that smash the hedge back into shape and scatter thorns across the road. Every September, rural tyre shops do excellent trade in punctures.

### Shearing

In June, the sheep were **shorn**. Sheep in Britain are shorn once a year in early summer, both for their own comfort (a sheep in full fleece in July is a miserable sheep) and to reduce the risk of fly strike. The work is done by **shearers**, highly skilled contractors who travel from farm to farm with their own gear, many of them moving around the world with the seasons, from New Zealand to Britain and back. A good shearer can shear a sheep in a minute or two, and the best competition shearers can do several hundred in a single day. The ewe is set on her rump and turned through a precise sequence of positions as the electric handpiece takes the fleece off in one piece.

Each **fleece** was then rolled, skin side out, and packed into a huge **wool sheet**, a sack that is sent to **British Wool**, the organisation that collects, grades and sells the wool of British sheep. The price of wool is a long-running source of bitter humour among farmers. In several recent years a fleece has fetched less than the cost of shearing it, partly because synthetic fibres took over much of the market.

Alex's six ewes were shorn in about ten minutes by a young woman called Ffion, who asked him whether he wanted them **dagged** first. **Dagging** or **crutching** means clipping away the dirty, dung-clotted wool, the **dags**, from around the tail. This was the moment Alex was introduced to the great horror of summer sheep-keeping.

### Fly strike

**Fly strike**, or **myiasis**, happens when blowflies, mainly the greenbottle, lay their eggs in a sheep's fleece, attracted by dirt, dung, sweat or wounds. The eggs hatch into maggots, which eat the sheep's flesh, alive. A struck sheep stands apart from the flock, nibbles at its back end, wags its tail, and has a damp, discoloured patch of wool that moves when you part it. Left untreated, a sheep can die within days. It is the single most important welfare problem of a British summer, and the reason sheep farmers spend June to September constantly looking at their animals' backsides.

Prevention means shearing, dagging, controlling worms (which cause the diarrhoea that leads to **dirty back ends**), and applying a **pour-on** insecticide along the back. Treatment means clipping away the wool, removing every maggot and treating the wound, a job that tests the stomach of every newcomer. Alex found his first case in July: one of his lambs, behind the hedge in the Cae Mawr. He rang Gwen in a panic. She talked him through it on the phone, and he did it himself, and afterwards he sat in the field for a while.

The other enemies of the sheep, he learnt over the summer, were legion. **Foot rot** and **scald** are bacterial foot infections that make sheep limp or graze on their knees. **Liver fluke** is a parasite carried by a tiny mud snail that thrives on wet ground, which meant Brook Meadow, with its boggy bottom, was dangerous grazing in a wet autumn. **Orf** is a viral disease causing scabby lips, which, unusually, can also infect humans, producing an ugly blister on the hands. **Worms** (internal parasites, especially in lambs) were controlled by **drenching**, squirting a dose of liquid wormer into the back of the mouth with a **drenching gun**, ideally guided by faecal egg counts, because sheep worms, like horse worms, have become resistant to many of the drugs.

"And then there's the ones that just die," said Gwen. "For no reason at all. Because it's a Tuesday."

## July: Cattle

![A Hereford bull at a show in Dunblane, Scotland. The breed, from the neighbouring county of Herefordshire, is one of the most widespread beef breeds in the world.](/assets/essays/leave-the-gate-as-you-found-it/images/hereford.jpg)
*Hereford bull at a show in Dunblane. Photo: Xabier Cid, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Alex had been avoiding the cattle.

This was not entirely irrational. Cattle are large, curious, and occasionally dangerous. Cows with young calves can be fiercely protective, and every year in Britain walkers are injured, and occasionally killed, by cattle, most often when they have a dog with them. The advice from the farming unions and walking charities is consistent: don't walk between a cow and her calf; move calmly and quietly round the edge of the herd; and if cattle threaten you while you have a dog, let the dog go. It can outrun them, you can't, and the cattle are after the dog.

By July, though, Gwen needed help, and Alex had run out of excuses.

### What's what

The first task, as always, was vocabulary. Collectively, cattle are **stock** or, to old farmers, **beasts**. A **cow**, strictly, is an adult female that has had at least one calf; a young female that has not yet calved is a **heifer**. An uncastrated male kept for breeding is a **bull**; a castrated male raised for beef is a **bullock** or **steer**. Very young animals are **calves**. Young cattle between calf and adult are **young stock**, **yearlings**, or in the north and Scotland **stirks**; heifers being reared to join the herd are **followers** or **replacements**.

A **cow and calf** together are described as having the calf **at foot**. A cow that is pregnant is **in calf**; one that isn't is **empty** or **barren**; one that is on heat is **bulling**, because she will often mount other cows, or be mounted by them. Cattle pregnancy lasts about nine months, around 283 days, so a cow has at most one calf a year.

Then there are the two great divisions of cattle farming. A **dairy herd** is kept for milk. The cows, usually black-and-white **Holstein Friesians**, are milked twice or three times a day, every day of the year, in a **parlour** or by a **robotic milking** machine, which makes dairy farming one of the most relentless jobs in the country. Dairy cows must calve to produce milk; their calves are taken off them early and reared separately. A **beef herd**, by contrast, raises cattle for meat. Gwen's was a **suckler herd**, meaning the cows raise their own calves, which **suckle** them in the field until **weaning** at six to ten months old.

Gwen's cows were mostly **Hereford crosses**: red-bodied with white faces, from the breed developed in the neighbouring county of Herefordshire, and now farmed everywhere from Argentina to Australia. Her bull was a **Limousin**, a muscular, golden-brown French breed used to sire calves with plenty of meat on them. Other breeds Alex learnt to recognise: the black, hornless **Aberdeen Angus**; the huge, cream-coloured **Charolais**; the shaggy, long-horned **Highland**; the bulky, double-muscled **Belgian Blue**; and the delicate brown **Jersey** and **Guernsey**, whose milk is famous for its cream. Cattle with no horns, either naturally or because they were removed as calves (**disbudding**), are **polled**.

Beef cattle are sold at different stages. **Store cattle** (**stores**) are animals sold to another farmer to be grown and fattened. **Finished** or **fat** cattle are ready for slaughter, sold either at auction or **deadweight**, direct to an abattoir, where the farmer is paid on the weight of the carcase and on its grading for shape and fat cover.

### Paperwork, cattle edition

Every bovine animal in Britain has a **cattle passport**. Every calf must be identified with a pair of official ear tags soon after birth and registered, and every movement, birth and death reported. For years, in England, all this has gone through the **British Cattle Movement Service** and its online **Cattle Tracing System**. A new system, the **Livestock Information Service**, has been rolling out to replace it, to be followed by compulsory **electronic ear tags** for cattle. As with many government IT projects, the timetable has slipped more than once, a fact Alex, as an IT man, found oddly comforting.

### The bull on the footpath

Gwen's bull lived in a field crossed by a public footpath, which made Alex nervous until Gwen explained the law. Under the **Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981**, it is an offence to keep a bull in a field crossed by a public right of way unless he is under ten months old, or he is not of a recognised dairy breed and is running with cows or heifers. Dairy bulls are banned outright from such fields because they are, as a rule, far more dangerous than beef bulls. A beef bull running with his cows is generally interested in the cows, not you. "Generally," Gwen added.

### TB test day

The job Gwen needed help with was the **TB test**. **Bovine tuberculosis**, a bacterial disease of cattle that can also infect badgers, deer and other animals, is one of the most serious and contentious issues in British farming. Like much of western England, this border country has a high incidence. Cattle herds are tested regularly by law, every year or more often in high-risk areas, and the test takes two visits.

On day one, the vet clipped two small patches of hair on each animal's neck, measured the thickness of the skin with callipers, and injected two types of **tuberculin**, one made from bovine TB bacteria and one from bird TB bacteria. Seventy-two hours later, the vet came back and measured the skin again. A lump at the bovine site that is much larger than any at the avian site suggests the animal's immune system has met bovine TB.

This meant handling every animal twice, which meant Alex learnt about cattle handling equipment. The cattle were gathered into the yard, then pushed up a **race**, a narrow fenced passage, one behind another, and into a **crush**, a strong metal cage with a **yoke** that closes gently round the animal's neck to hold its head still while the vet works. Alex's job was to stand behind the last animal in the race with a board and move them forward, which involved more shouting and less heroism than he had imagined, and to keep well out of range of the back legs. Cattle kick forwards and sideways as well as backwards, a move farmers call **cow-kicking**.

The result, three days later, was **clear**. Gwen let out a breath Alex hadn't realised she was holding. A **reactor**, an animal that tests positive, must be slaughtered, with compensation, and the whole herd is put under **restriction**, unable to sell animals except to slaughter, until it passes a series of tests. Farmers call this being **shut down**, and it can last for months or years. An **inconclusive** result means a retest in sixty days and an anxious wait. Some farmers have been under restriction for a decade. Few subjects stir stronger feelings in the countryside, and the debate over whether badgers should be culled to control the disease has divided farmers, scientists, conservationists and politicians for decades. Alex, a newcomer, learnt quickly that it was not a subject to raise lightly in the pub.

"You did all right," Gwen said, which from her was a medal.

## August: The Show and the Harvest

On a Thursday in early August, Gwen took them to the **Burwarton Show**, held on a showground in the parkland of the Burwarton estate near Bridgnorth, on the other side of the county. Shropshire has a show almost every summer weekend, from little village affairs to the big **county show**, but Burwarton is one of the largest one-day agricultural shows in the country, drawing crowds of nearly twenty thousand.

An **agricultural show** is the countryside on parade. It is part competition, part trade fair, part family day out and part annual reunion for people who see each other otherwise only at the mart. Alex had expected a fête with cows. What he found was a small temporary town.

There were the **livestock classes**, the heart of any show. Farmers who had spent weeks preparing their animals, washing, clipping, trimming and **halter-training** them, led them round the **show ring** in front of a **judge**, who walked round each one, felt its back and loins, looked at how it stood and moved, and then lined them up in order. Winners got **rosettes**, red for first, blue for second, yellow for third (in British shows; the colours are different in America), and the best animals went forward to compete for **breed champion**, then **interbreed champion**, the grand prize of the day. Gwen had entered a pair of Clun ewe lambs, and Rhys, who had been dressed in a white **stock coat** by his mother, came third.

There were **young handlers' classes**, in which children not much taller than their sheep showed them with grave professionalism. There was a **sheep shearing competition**, in which shearers raced against the clock and each other while a commentator explained what they were doing. There were horse classes: **in-hand** classes, where horses are led and judged on their **conformation** (build and proportions), and ridden classes, **show jumping**, and the beloved **heavy horses**, Shires and Clydesdales and Suffolk Punches, plaited and ribboned and polished till they shone. There was a **dog show** and **terrier racing**. There was a **produce tent**, run with the help of the **Women's Institute** (the **WI**), where the best jam, chutney, sponge cake, onions and arrangement of five dahlias were judged with a severity that made the cattle ring look relaxed.

Gwen introduced Alex to so many people that he gave up trying to remember their names, and started instead to understand something about the countryside that no book had told him. In a city, you can live for years without knowing your neighbours. Here, everyone knew everyone, everyone had known everyone's parents, and the show was where the web of relationships that held the place together was renewed for another year. It is also the great event of the **Young Farmers' Clubs** (**YFC**), the rural youth organisation whose members compete, organise, raise money and socialise with a vigour that has been the start of many a farming marriage.

### Harvest

August is also harvest. Gwen's land was mostly grass, but the big **arable** farms on the flatter land to the east, those growing crops rather than raising livestock, were in full swing. The **winter barley** had come off in July and the **oilseed rape**, whose brilliant yellow fields lit up the countryside in May, soon after. Now it was the turn of the **winter wheat**: wheat sown the previous autumn, which sits through the winter as a short green carpet and grows away in spring. (**Spring** crops are sown in spring and harvested a little later.)

The **combine harvester** is so called because it combines three jobs that once took a small army of workers: cutting the crop, **threshing** it to knock the grain out of the ears, and separating the grain from the **chaff** (the husks) and the straw. The grain is stored in a tank on top of the machine and unloaded on the move, through a long pipe called the **unloading auger**, into a **grain trailer** driven alongside. The straw drops out of the back in long lines called **swaths**, to be baled later for bedding. What is left in the field is **stubble**, the short, stiff stalks that crunch underfoot.

Combining runs day and night when the weather is right, because grain must be harvested at the right **moisture content**; too wet and it goes mouldy in store, so it has to be put through a **grain dryer**, at a cost. All through August, the lanes rumbled with tractors and trailers late into the evening, and the air was full of dust and the smell of cut straw. An oddly satisfying piece of jargon Alex picked up was the **headland**: the strip round the edge of a field where machinery turns at the end of each run. Another was **tramlines**: the regular, parallel unsown strips you see running through growing crops, left so that the tractor can drive through to spray and fertilise without crushing the crop.

Within days of harvest, the stubble was being worked. Some fields were **ploughed**, the soil turned over by a plough with a row of curved steel **mouldboards**. Others were **cultivated** (broken up more shallowly) or sown directly into the stubble, which is called **direct drilling** and has become more popular because it disturbs the soil less. Sowing is called **drilling** because the seed goes in with a **seed drill**, which places it in neat rows at a set depth. By late September, the first fields of next year's wheat were already showing as faint green lines.

## September and October: The Mart, and the Wheel Turns

![A wartime sale of ponies, sheep and cattle at Bampton Fair in Devon, October 1943. The traditional livestock fair is the ancestor of today's market.](/assets/essays/leave-the-gate-as-you-found-it/images/bampton.jpg)
*Bampton Fair, Devon, October 1943. Photo: Ministry of Information Photo Division, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

In September, Alex sold his lambs.

He had kept two of the ewe lambs to join his flock, to replace the oldest ewes. The other five went to the **livestock market** at Ludlow, which everyone called the **mart**. Lambs are sold either as **stores**, to another farmer who will fatten them on his own grass or **root crops** (turnips, swedes and the like), or as **finished** lambs, also called **prime** or **fat** lambs, ready for slaughter. Alex's were not quite fat enough, Gwen judged, running her hand along each lamb's backbone and across its **loin**, feeling for the cover of flesh over the bones. This **handling** is how farmers assess condition, and the **condition score** of a ewe, on a scale of one to five, is felt the same way.

He took them in Gwen's **livestock trailer**, with their movement document filled in, and unloaded them into a numbered **pen** in a cavernous shed full of noise: the bleating of thousands of sheep, the clang of gates, the shouts of the **drovers** moving animals between pens with sticks and boards. Then he went to stand at the **ringside**.

A livestock auction is a performance. The animals are run into the **sale ring**, a small sawdust-floored arena, and the **auctioneer** stands above it in a raised box called the **rostrum**, rattling out bids in a chant so fast that Alex could not, at first, tell which numbers were prices and which were filler. The buyers, a row of men and a few women leaning on the rail, bid with almost invisible gestures: a twitch of a catalogue, a raised finger, a nod, the flick of an eyebrow. The auctioneer knew each of them and missed none of them. Each lot was sold in seconds. Finished lambs and cattle are usually priced in **pence per kilo liveweight**, weighed on scales as they enter the ring; store lambs are often sold **per head**.

Alex's five store lambs made a fair price. Afterwards, at the office, he was told about **luck money**, a small sum that by long tradition a seller hands back to the buyer "for luck", and about the importance, in a community where everyone does business with everyone for decades, of being known as someone who sells honestly and pays promptly. The mart, he understood, was not just a place to buy and sell. It had a café that served an excellent breakfast. It was where a farmer who might otherwise see nobody all week caught up with the news, heard who was ill and who was struggling, and found out the price of everything. Many of Britain's small livestock markets have closed in recent decades, and the loss is felt as much socially as commercially.

### Deadstock

In October one of Alex's old ewes died. There was no obvious reason. She was simply found dead one morning by the hedge, which is the end, sooner or later, of every animal on every farm. The phrase is old and blunt: "Where there's livestock, there's deadstock."

What surprised him was what came next. In England, you may not bury a dead farm animal on your own land, as people did for centuries. That has been banned since 2003, under the rules on **animal by-products**, because of the risk of spreading disease and polluting water. Instead, **fallen stock**, as dead farm animals are called, must be collected and taken to an approved knacker's yard, hunt kennels or incinerator. The person who does this is still known as the **knacker man**, or simply **the knacker**, a trade name that goes back centuries. Most farmers belong to a scheme run by the **National Fallen Stock Company** to arrange collections. He arrived the next day with a truck and a winch, was kind and matter-of-fact, and was gone in five minutes.

### Incomers

By the end of October, Alex and Jo had lived at Brook Cottage for a year.

They had learnt that they were **incomers**, which is a description, not an insult, though it can be used as both. Every village in rural Britain has a population of people who were born there, and a population who moved in. The relations between them are usually perfectly friendly, and occasionally tense, especially where incomers push up house prices, complain about the smell of muck spreading or the noise of the cockerel, or object to the planning applications of the families who have farmed the land for generations. The quickest way to be accepted, Alex found, was to turn up: to help, to ask, to listen more than talk, to buy eggs from the honesty box of the people down the lane, to drink in the pub, to support the **village hall**, and to go to the meetings of the **parish council**, the most local tier of government in England, where the issues of the day included potholes, the bus, a planning application for a new barn and a long-running dispute about a bench.

They had learnt the rhythms of the year, which in the country are set by animals, grass and weather rather than by school terms and holidays. They had learnt that a farmer's day starts at five or six, that you never phone a dairy farmer at milking time, and that the polite way to ask a farmer whether he can help you is to ask in March and not in silage week. They had learnt to say thank you with a raised finger from the steering wheel to every car that waited for them at a passing place, and to wait patiently behind tractors without overtaking on blind bends. They had learnt that **muck spreading**, the spreading of manure on fields as fertiliser, smells exactly as bad as people say, and that **slurry**, liquid manure from cattle sheds, smells worse. They had learnt that the countryside is not quiet: it is full of bleating, lowing, crowing, birdsong, tractors at midnight, gunfire from the shoot in the next valley in winter, and the occasional unearthly scream of a vixen.

And they had learnt that it is a working landscape. The fields, the hedges, the drystone walls and the woods that people drive out from the cities to admire on Sunday afternoons are, almost all of them, the product of centuries of farming and still being farmed, by people whose living depends on them and who are, on the whole, glad to share them, as long as you leave the gate as you found it.

In early November, Gwen phoned.

"Tups are going in Thursday," she said. "Bonfire Night. You coming?"

Alex was already pulling on his wellies.

## A Countryside Phrasebook

Here, gathered in one place, are the words that Alex learnt in his first year. Meanings vary a little from region to region, and farmers enjoy arguing about them, which is part of the fun.

### Land, farming and the farm year

- **Access land**: open country (mountain, moor, heath, down, registered common) where walkers have a right to roam under the CRoW Act 2000.
- **Acre**: the traditional unit of land area, still used in Britain; about 0.4 hectares (one hectare is about 2.5 acres).
- **Arable**: land used for growing crops, as opposed to grazing livestock.
- **Baler twine**: plastic string used to tie bales, and much else.
- **Bridleway**: right of way for walkers, horse riders and cyclists; blue waymark.
- **Clamp**: a large heap of silage, rolled flat and sealed under plastic sheeting.
- **Combine harvester**: machine that cuts, threshes and separates a cereal crop.
- **Contractor**: a business hired to do farm work with its own machinery.
- **CPH (county parish holding) number**: the registration number of land where livestock are kept.
- **Drilling**: sowing seed in rows with a seed drill.
- **Fallen stock**: dead farm animals, which must be collected rather than buried.
- **Flail**: hedge-cutting machine with rotating blades.
- **Grass keep / grazing licence / tack**: renting out grass for grazing for a season.
- **Hay**: grass cut and dried for feed.
- **Haylage**: grass wilted, then baled and wrapped; between hay and silage.
- **Headland**: strip at a field's edge where machinery turns.
- **Ley**: field sown with grass for a few years as part of a rotation.
- **Livestock market / mart**: auction where animals are bought and sold.
- **Meadow**: grassland mown for hay (often used loosely for any grassy field).
- **Muck / muck spreading**: manure / spreading it on fields as fertiliser.
- **Paddock**: small enclosed field near the house.
- **Pasture**: grassland grazed by livestock. **Permanent pasture** has never (or not for a long time) been ploughed.
- **Poached**: ground churned to mud by hooves.
- **Public footpath**: right of way for walkers only; yellow waymark.
- **Silage**: green grass preserved by fermentation, in a clamp or wrapped bales.
- **Slurry**: liquid manure, especially from cattle.
- **Standstill**: the six-day ban on moving animals off a holding after new livestock arrive.
- **Stile / kissing gate**: ways for walkers to cross a field boundary without letting stock out.
- **Stock-proof**: describes a fence or hedge that keeps animals in.
- **Straw**: dried stalks of cereal crops, mostly used as bedding.
- **Stubble**: short stalks left in a field after harvest.
- **Tedding**: spreading cut grass to help it dry or wilt evenly.
- **Tramlines**: unsown wheel tracks through a growing crop.
- **Windrow / row**: a line of cut grass or straw raked together for baling or picking up.

### Horses

- **Barefoot**: unshod.
- **Bay / chestnut / grey / piebald / skewbald**: common horse colours.
- **Cob**: a stocky, strong, sensible type of horse.
- **Colic**: abdominal pain; potentially life-threatening.
- **Colt / filly / foal**: young male / young female / baby.
- **DIY, part and full livery**: grades of service at a yard where you keep a horse for a fee.
- **Farrier**: registered professional who trims and shoes hooves.
- **Frog**: rubbery V-shaped structure on the underside of the hoof.
- **Gelding / mare / stallion**: castrated male / adult female / uncastrated adult male.
- **Hand (hh)**: unit of height, four inches (about 10.2 cm), measured to the withers. 14.2hh is the traditional upper limit for a pony.
- **Haynet**: string bag for hay.
- **Laminitis**: painful inflammation inside the hoof, often triggered by rich grass.
- **Mucking out / skipping out**: cleaning a stable fully / picking out droppings.
- **Mud fever**: skin infection of the lower legs caused by wet, muddy conditions.
- **Passport**: compulsory identity document for every horse, pony and donkey.
- **Ragwort**: yellow-flowered weed, poisonous to horses, especially in hay.
- **Rug**: horse blanket; a **turnout rug** is waterproof for the field.
- **Sound / lame**: moving evenly / not.
- **Tack**: riding equipment (saddle, bridle, girth and so on).
- **Turnout / turning out**: putting a horse (or cattle) out to grass.
- **Withers**: ridge between the shoulder blades, where height is measured.

### Chickens

- **Bantam**: miniature chicken.
- **Biosecurity**: measures to keep disease out.
- **Broody**: a hen determined to sit on eggs and hatch them.
- **Cockerel / cock / rooster**: male chicken.
- **Grit**: oyster shell for calcium, flint grit for grinding food in the gizzard.
- **Hybrid / pure breed**: commercial cross bred for laying / traditional breed.
- **Layers' pellets**: complete feed for laying hens.
- **Moult**: annual loss and regrowth of feathers.
- **Pecking order**: the flock's strict social hierarchy.
- **Point-of-lay (POL)**: young hen about to start laying.
- **Pop-hole**: small door to the hen house.
- **Pullet**: female chicken under a year old.
- **Red mite**: blood-sucking mite that hides in the hen house by day.

### Sheep

- **Cade / pet / bottle lamb**: lamb reared by hand.
- **Cast**: stuck on its back and unable to rise.
- **Colostrum**: first milk, vital for a newborn's immunity.
- **Dagging / crutching**: clipping dirty wool from around the tail.
- **Draft ewe**: older ewe sold from a hill farm to a lowland farm.
- **Drenching**: dosing with liquid medicine, usually wormer.
- **Ewe**: adult female sheep.
- **Fly strike**: infestation of a sheep's fleece and flesh by maggots.
- **Foot rot / scald**: bacterial foot infections.
- **Hefted**: a hill flock attached to its own patch of unfenced land.
- **Hogg / hogget**: young sheep from weaning to first shearing (also its meat).
- **Liver fluke**: parasite spread by a mud snail on wet land.
- **Mothering up**: getting a ewe to bond with her lambs.
- **Raddle**: coloured crayon or paint that marks ewes served by a tup.
- **Scanning**: ultrasound pregnancy testing.
- **Shearling**: sheep after its first shearing.
- **Single / twins / triplets / empty**: scanning results.
- **Store lamb / finished (prime, fat) lamb**: one sold for further fattening / one ready for slaughter.
- **Tup / ram**: breeding male. **Tupping**: mating season.
- **Wether**: castrated male.

### Cattle

- **At foot**: of a calf, still with its mother.
- **Beasts / stock**: cattle collectively.
- **Bull / bullock (steer)**: uncastrated breeding male / castrated male.
- **Bulling**: on heat.
- **Cow / heifer**: female that has calved / young female that has not.
- **Crush and race**: handling cage and the narrow passage leading to it.
- **Dairy / beef / suckler herd**: kept for milk / for meat / beef cows raising their own calves.
- **Deadweight / liveweight**: paid on carcase weight from the abattoir / weighed alive at market.
- **Followers / replacements**: young heifers being reared to join the herd.
- **In calf / empty**: pregnant / not pregnant.
- **Polled**: without horns.
- **Reactor**: animal that tests positive for bovine TB.
- **Shut down / under restriction**: a herd barred from selling animals after a TB breakdown.
- **Stirk / yearling / young stock**: young cattle.
- **Stores**: cattle sold to be grown and fattened elsewhere.

### Rural life

- **Aga / Rayburn**: cast-iron range cookers.
- **Honesty box**: unattended stall where you pay by leaving money.
- **Incomer**: someone who has moved into a rural community.
- **Kerosene / heating oil**: fuel for most off-gas-grid boilers.
- **Knacker man**: collector of fallen stock.
- **Parish council**: the most local tier of English local government.
- **Passing place**: widened spot on a single-track lane.
- **Septic tank**: private underground sewage system.
- **WI**: Women's Institute.
- **YFC**: Young Farmers' Clubs.
