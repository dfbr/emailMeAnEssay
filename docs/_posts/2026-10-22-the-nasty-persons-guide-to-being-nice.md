> *"…and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short."*
> Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* (1651)

## A confession at the bus stop

Let us begin with Gerald. Gerald does not exist, which is a pity, because he would make an excellent dinner-party guest if only for the arguments he would start. He is a composite, assembled from a great many real people, some of whom you will recognise, and one or two of whom may be looking back at you from the bathroom mirror.

Gerald is 47. He works in procurement for a firm that makes industrial fastenings. He is good at his job, in the sense that he is accurate, punctual and difficult to fool. He has never knowingly broken a law. He sends back soup. He believes, sincerely, that he is a "straight talker", and that people who dislike him dislike him because the truth is unwelcome. When he writes emails, he removes the greetings, because "time is money". When someone holds a door for him, he walks through it as though the door had been installed for the purpose. When a colleague brings in cakes for her birthday, he considers the matter closed once he has had a slice.

One grey Tuesday, in a bus shelter in a town that does not matter, Gerald is overheard by a friend of his sister's, Priya, who happens also to be a psychologist, though she is off duty and would very much like to stay that way.

"You look thoughtful," says Priya.

"I've had a performance review," says Gerald. "They said I need to work on my 'interpersonal impact'. My *interpersonal impact*. I was told my team find me 'abrasive'. I'm not abrasive. I'm efficient."

"What did they actually say you did?"

"Nothing! That's the thing. Apparently it's what I don't do. Say thank you. Ask how people are. I told them: I'm not here to be liked, I'm here to get the work done."

"And is the work getting done?"

Gerald pauses. This is not, he notes with irritation, an easy question. "Mostly. Two people have left this year. One of them was good. And Dave in logistics has stopped copying me into things, which I assumed was an accident."

"Gerald," says Priya, as the bus finally lurches into view, "do you want to be nice?"

"No," says Gerald, with the clarity of a man who has been asked whether he would like to be audited. "But I'd like to stop being a problem."

This essay is for Gerald. It is also for the people who live with Gerald, work with Gerald, and sit behind Gerald on aeroplanes while he reclines his seat. Above all, it is for anyone who has noticed, perhaps at three in the morning, that they are somewhat harder to be around than they intended, and who would like to do something about it without undergoing a personality transplant.

That last clause matters, because most advice about kindness is aimed at the already kind. It tells you to *radiate warmth*, to *lead with love*, to *be the change*. If you are the sort of person who bristles at phrases like "lead with love", this advice is worse than useless; it is an irritant. You close the tab. You go back to being Gerald.

This guide takes a different line. It assumes you are, at present, not very nice, and that you may not especially wish to become so. It makes no appeal to your better nature, partly out of politeness and partly because we have no evidence you have one. Instead it appeals to your self-interest, your curiosity and your sense that there must be a method. And it turns out that there is. There is, in fact, a surprisingly large body of evidence on how kindness works, why it pays, and how the unwilling can acquire it as a skill, in the way that one acquires a decent backhand or the ability to parallel park. Nobody is born able to parallel park either.

## What we mean by "nice"

First, some definitions, because the English language is slippery here and the slipperiness is often how nasty people excuse themselves.

**Nice** is a social surface. It is the smile, the "lovely to see you", the "no, honestly, it's no trouble". Niceness is cheap, quick and frequently insincere. It is also, in many contexts, entirely appropriate: nobody wants a hairdresser who gives them the unvarnished truth about their life choices. The word has had a curious history. It comes from the Latin *nescius*, "ignorant", and entered English in the thirteenth century meaning foolish or silly. Over the following six centuries it drifted through "fussy", "precise" and "delicate" (as in a *nice* distinction, which survives) before arriving at "agreeable" sometime in the eighteenth century. It has never entirely lost its flavour of the superficial. When someone describes a meal as "nice", you do not rush to book.

**Polite** is niceness's more disciplined sibling: a set of conventions, learned and rule-bound, for avoiding offence. Politeness is the grammar of social life. It can be perfectly observed by someone who does not care about you in the slightest, which is precisely its function. A polite person has agreed to a treaty; they need not be fond of you.

**Considerate** is the quiet one, and it is the word most worth having. To be considerate is to *consider*: to think, before you act, about what your act will cost another person. The considerate person puts the bin out because they noticed it was full, not because they were asked. They keep their voice down on the train because they have registered that other people are on it. Consideration is mostly noticing, and noticing is a skill.

**Kind** is the active form. To be kind is to do something, usually at some cost to yourself (time, comfort, a small amount of pride), for the benefit of another. The word shares a root with "kin": to be kind, originally, was to treat a stranger as though they were family. The kind person lends the lift, covers the shift, sits with you in the hospital corridor.

**Good** is the heavyweight, and we will not need it much. Goodness involves moral commitments that run deeper than any of the above, and it is not the subject here.

Here is the central claim of this essay, and you may want to underline it: **you can be reliably considerate and kind without being nice, and without feeling the warm glow that nice people apparently feel.** You do not need to enjoy it. Many of the most dependably decent people in history have been, by all accounts, rather dry. What you need is a handful of habits, an understanding of why they matter, and a willingness to be slightly wrong about yourself for a few weeks.

The distinction matters in practice because "be nice" is a terrible instruction for a person like Gerald. It sounds like a demand to pretend, and Gerald rightly distrusts pretending. "Be considerate" is a much better one: it asks him to notice things and to adjust accordingly, which is very much his sort of work. Gerald can notice that a bin is full. Gerald can adjust. And, as we shall see, a surprising amount of what makes people feel well treated is no more mysterious than that.

## Part one: are you actually nasty?

Before we embark on any programme of improvement, it is sensible to establish what is being improved. Do you have a *nasty character*, as the Victorians would have put it, or merely a nasty *afternoon*?

### The personality argument

Psychologists have been measuring personality for about a century, and the model that has won most agreement is the "Big Five": openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. The one that concerns us is **agreeableness**, which captures how far a person is trusting, cooperative, modest and sympathetic, as against suspicious, competitive and tough-minded. If you want to know whether you are the kind of person others call "difficult", it is the best single place to look. Low scorers are not necessarily villains: they make good negotiators, sceptical editors and effective auditors, and a society consisting solely of high scorers would be easy prey for anyone with a clipboard and a plausible story. But low agreeableness does tend to predict friction.

Twin studies suggest that the main personality traits are heritable to roughly the tune of 40 to 50 per cent. That is a substantial share, and it means that some people do start the race several lengths behind. It also means that around half of the variation in how pleasant people are is *not* inherited. Experience, culture, habit and choice account for the rest.

There is more good news. Personality is not fixed. A well-known 2006 meta-analysis by Brent Roberts, Kate Walton and Wolfgang Viechtbauer pooled data from 92 longitudinal studies and found that, on average, people become more conscientious, more emotionally stable and, to a lesser degree, more agreeable as they age. Psychologists call this the "maturity principle". Most of us, left alone for long enough, sand ourselves down. The aim of this essay is to speed the process up by a decade or two.

There is also a darker corner of the personality literature that deserves a brief mention, if only so that we can dismiss it. In 2002 Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams described the "Dark Triad" of narcissism, Machiavellianism and psychopathy: traits associated with grandiosity, manipulation and callousness. People who score highly on all three are, to put it mildly, a problem. But two things should reassure you. First, a genuine psychopath, by the usual estimates, makes up around one per cent of the general population; the number of people who are actually *dangerous* is dwarfed by the number who are merely tiresome. Second, the very fact that you are reading a guide to being more considerate suggests that you do not belong in that company. Callousness in the clinical sense does not typically come with a nagging suspicion that one might be a bit much. Gerald, who has such a suspicion, is not a monster. He is a man who has never been taught the mechanics.

### The situation argument

The more interesting finding, and a more useful one, is that *the situation* tends to matter far more than we think. Psychologists have a name for our habit of ignoring this: the "fundamental attribution error", which is a grand name for the tendency to explain other people's behaviour by their character ("he is rude") and our own by our circumstances ("I was rushing"). It is a bias that produces a world of nasty *them* and understandable *us*.

The classic demonstration is a study carried out in 1973 by John Darley and Daniel Batson at Princeton Theological Seminary. It has an irresistible premise. The participants were seminary students: young men and women training for the ministry, who could reasonably be expected to hold views on kindness. They were asked to prepare a short talk, and were then sent to another building to deliver it. Half had been asked to speak on a particular subject: the parable of the Good Samaritan, the story of a traveller who stops to help a beaten man by the road after a priest and a Levite pass by on the other side. On the route, in an alley, the researchers had placed a man slumped in a doorway, coughing and groaning.

Some of the seminarians had been told they were running late. Some were told they had a few minutes. Some were told they had time to spare. The results were striking. Of those in the low-hurry condition, 63 per cent stopped to help. Of those in the highest-hurry condition, only 10 per cent did. Several of the hurried, who were on their way to talk about the Good Samaritan, stepped over the victim. Whether they were due to discuss the parable made no great difference.

The lesson is not that seminarians are hypocrites. It is that nearly all of us, given enough hurry, stop noticing other people; that kindness is crowded out not by malice but by a to-do list. Which means that a good proportion of what looks like nastiness is simply *speed*.

Gerald, it will not surprise you, is always in a hurry. He is not rude to people because he dislikes them. He is rude because they are *in the way of a thing*. (Note, too, that it is rarely his own hurry that he regards as the problem. It is other people's slowness.)

Another factor worth naming is power. The psychologist Dacher Keltner has spent decades studying how holding power changes behaviour, and has found a consistent pattern: power dulls empathy. In a 2006 experiment led by Adam Galinsky, participants were asked to draw the letter E on their own foreheads. People who had been put in a high-power frame of mind were markedly more likely to draw it so that it read correctly to themselves, and backwards to anybody facing them; those made to feel powerless were more likely to draw it so that others could read it. It is a charmingly small test of a large phenomenon: power makes people less likely to ask how the world looks from the other side of the desk. Keltner has also described an experiment in which a group was asked to work together, one member having been randomly put in charge, and a plate of biscuits was left out. The leaders were more likely to take the last biscuit, to chew with their mouths open, and to scatter crumbs.

You do not have to be a chief executive to have a taste of this. A mid-ranking manager, a long-serving parent, a customer with a complaint: all hold a small amount of power over someone, and all are therefore at risk of the "biscuit effect". A great deal of nastiness is not a trait but a *position*.

### What this means for you

The upshot is encouraging. If your nastiness is mostly a matter of haste, power and habit, then it is something you can *work on by changing your habits*, rather than by feeling differently, which is a good deal harder. You need not become a different person. You need to build in a few pauses, develop a few checks, and shift your default settings.

Better still, you do not need to like people to do it. The great irony of kindness is that it is often easier to practise as a technique than as a feeling. The relationship between behaviour and feeling runs in both directions, and the route from behaviour to feeling is the more reliable for people who are, ahem, resistant to feeling. We shall come to that. But first we need to deal with an objection that has been quietly forming at the back of Gerald's mind.

"Fine," says Gerald. "Perhaps I'm a bit abrupt. But the world is full of people who are nice to your face and walk all over you. Nice guys finish last."

He is not alone in thinking so. The phrase is usually credited to Leo Durocher, the combative manager of the Brooklyn Dodgers, who is reported to have said something to that effect about a rival team in 1946. It has stuck in the language for eighty years. It is therefore worth asking whether it is true.

## Part two: do nice guys finish last?

The short answer is: sometimes, in the short run, against opponents who are not paying attention. The long answer is one of the more beautiful results in the social sciences, and it begins with a pair of prisoners.

### The tournament

In the early 1980s the political scientist Robert Axelrod, at the University of Michigan, wanted to know how cooperation could arise among selfish agents who have no central authority to compel them. His tool was the "prisoner's dilemma", a game so famous that it has bored generations of undergraduates. Two players each choose to cooperate or defect. If both cooperate, both do reasonably well. If one defects while the other cooperates, the defector does very well and the cooperator very badly. If both defect, both do poorly. In a single round, defecting is always the rational move, which is the dilemma: two rational players end up worse off than two cooperative ones.

But real life is not a single round. We meet the same people again, at work, at the school gate, in the lift. So Axelrod invited experts in game theory, economics, psychology and mathematics to submit computer programs to play the game against one another repeatedly, and then he ran a round-robin tournament. Fourteen programs entered the first contest, along with one that played at random. Some were cunning. Some were fiendish. One tried to work out its opponent's pattern and exploit it.

The winner was the shortest program of the lot, submitted by the Russian-born mathematical psychologist Anatol Rapoport. It was called **Tit for Tat**, and it consisted of a few lines of code. It cooperated on the first move; thereafter it simply did whatever its opponent had done on the previous move. If you were nice to it, it was nice to you. If you cheated it, it cheated you back, once, and then forgave you the moment you stopped.

Axelrod, intrigued, published the results and ran a second tournament. This time 62 entries arrived from six countries, and everybody knew that Tit for Tat had won the first time. They had every incentive to design something that would beat it. It won again.

Axelrod's analysis of why it won, in his 1984 book *The Evolution of Cooperation*, is a quiet masterpiece, and it reads rather like a manual for Gerald. The successful programs shared four properties:

- **They were nice**: they never defected first. The eight highest-scoring entries were all "nice" in this sense. The vicious programs, which tried to exploit their opponents, did well against the naive but terribly against each other, and finished well down the table.
- **They were retaliatory**: they did not let cheating go unanswered. A program that cooperated no matter what was a doormat, and was eaten alive.
- **They were forgiving**: having retaliated, they returned to cooperation as soon as the other player did. Grudges proved expensive, because they locked both sides into endless cycles of mutual punishment.
- **They were clear**: they were easy for the other player to read. Complex, inscrutable programs made it hard for opponents to see that cooperation was on offer.

Pause on that list, because it dismantles the "nice guys finish last" folk theory more thoroughly than any sermon. The winning strategy is not "be a pushover". It is: *begin generously, respond firmly, forgive quickly, and be predictable.* Nastiness fails not because it is immoral but because it is a poor way of playing a game that repeats.

There is an important refinement. In 1993 Martin Nowak and Karl Sigmund, writing in *Nature*, showed that in a noisy world, where moves are sometimes misread or mistakes are made, a slightly different strategy called "win-stay, lose-shift" can outperform Tit for Tat, and that "generous" variants that occasionally forgive a defection tend to do better still. Real life, in other words, is noisy. People misinterpret, forget, are tired. A policy of forgiving some of the time is better than a policy of strict equivalence. This will be useful later when we discuss how to react to other people's lapses, and indeed your own.

### An imagined dinner

To get a feel for the intellectual argument that has run for centuries over whether people are naturally nasty or naturally nice, let us imagine, purely for entertainment, a dinner. The guests could never have met (they are separated by centuries), but their published positions are on the record, and I have kept to those.

At the head of the table sits **Thomas Hobbes**, born in 1588, a man who famously said his mother had given birth to twins, himself and fear, because his birth was brought on early by news of the Spanish Armada. He has just finished explaining that, absent a strong state, human beings would live in a "war of all against all".

"Without a sovereign to keep us in awe," says Hobbes, "men are driven by competition, diffidence and glory. The life of man is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short."

"With respect," says **Adam Smith**, born 1723, polishing his spectacles, "you leave something out. I begin my *Theory of Moral Sentiments* by observing that, however selfish a man may be supposed, there are evidently principles in his nature that interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it except the pleasure of seeing it. We are not made of iron. We wince when another is hurt."

"And yet you also wrote that it is not from the benevolence of the butcher that we expect our dinner," says Hobbes, with a little smile.

"From his regard to his own interest, yes," says Smith. "Which does not contradict what I said before. Both are true. We are creatures of self-interest *and* of sympathy, and a decent society is one in which the first is channelled through the second. The butcher who cheats his customers does not remain a butcher for long."

**Charles Darwin**, who has been quietly eating his pudding, looks up. "I should add that I made this point in *The Descent of Man*. Communities that included the greatest number of the most sympathetic members would flourish best and rear the greatest number of offspring. People forget that. They remember the struggle for existence and forget that the struggle is often between *groups*, and a group of mutual helpers will generally win."

"Quite right," says **Peter Kropotkin**, the Russian geographer and anarchist, born 1842, who has been waiting for his moment. "I wrote an entire book, *Mutual Aid*, to say so. I had spent years in Siberia and Manchuria observing animals, and what struck me was not war of all against all but how much cooperation there was. Huxley had written a famous essay in 1888 describing nature as a gladiators' show. I was not persuaded. I looked for the gladiators and found the herds."

Hobbes folds his napkin. "You are all very optimistic for people who have never had to share a cave."

It is a good-natured disagreement, and, as with most good-natured disagreements, each of them is partly right. What matters for our purposes is the modern evidence, which has accumulated in every direction and tends to side with Smith, Darwin and Kropotkin on the question of origins, and with Hobbes on the question of what happens when nobody is watching.

Consider the toddlers. In a 2006 study in *Science*, Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello showed that 18-month-old infants will spontaneously help an adult who has accidentally dropped something out of reach: a marker, a clothes peg. They do so without being asked and without any reward. Even the nastiest person you know was once a person who would fetch your peg.

Consider the bats. In 1984 the biologist Gerald Wilkinson showed that vampire bats in Costa Rica regularly share blood meals with roost-mates who have failed to feed, regurgitating blood to keep them alive; a bat can starve in around two or three days without a meal. The donors were more likely to be kin, but not exclusively: they also favoured bats that had shared with them in the past. In other words, vampire bats keep a ledger. They are, by this evidence, better at reciprocity than a number of people in your office.

Cooperation is not a veneer over a nasty nature; it is one of the oldest tools in the box. The question is only whether *you* are using it.

### The cost of the bad apple

All of this is abstract. Gerald, like most practical men, wants to know what it means in the office.

The evidence there is about as clear as organisational research ever gets. In 2006 Will Felps, Terence Mitchell and Eliza Byington reviewed the literature on what they called "bad apples": team members who are negative, rude or withdrawn. They concluded that a single such member can drag a whole group down, out of proportion to their numbers, because the rest of the group begins to spend its energy managing the problem, the mood sours, and other members start copying the behaviour. Felps went on to run experiments in which actors, placed in student teams, played a jerk, a slacker or a depressive; teams with the jerk did markedly worse on the task than those without, in some reports by something like 30 to 40 per cent. The Stanford professor Robert Sutton drew on this and much else in his 2007 book *The No Asshole Rule*, which sold well precisely because many readers had a name in mind.

An even sharper demonstration came from medicine. In a randomised trial published in the journal *Pediatrics* in 2015, Arieh Riskin and colleagues in Israel took 24 teams of neonatal intensive care staff and had them take part in a simulated emergency. Before the simulation, half the teams were exposed to a rude comment from a (fictional) visiting expert from the United States, who remarked that the standard of medicine in Israel was poor and that he would not want his daughter treated there. The other half heard a neutral comment. The teams exposed to rudeness scored significantly worse on both diagnosis and on the procedures they carried out. They had not become less skilled. They had become worse at sharing information and at asking for help. One rude remark, from someone they would never see again, had measurably degraded how well they treated sick babies.

If rudeness can impair a neonatal team, it can certainly impair a department of fastener procurement.

There is also a more cheerful finding on the other side. The organisational psychologist Adam Grant, in his 2013 book *Give and Take*, sorted people into "givers", "takers" and "matchers", and reported that the givers were over-represented at both ends of the success ladder. The worst performers were often givers who gave until they burned out; but so were the best. The difference lay not in how much they gave but in how they gave: the successful ones were selective, set boundaries and were generous without being exploited. It is a finding worth treating with a little caution, as popular management research often needs to be, but it fits neatly with Axelrod: be generous, but not a doormat.

### A word for the ant

There is a final ancient witness to call. In Aesop, as most of us were told at school, the grasshopper spends the summer singing while the ant stores grain, and when winter comes the grasshopper begs for food and is turned away. It is generally taught as a lesson in prudence.

Look at the ant, however. In the seventeenth-century version by Jean de La Fontaine (the first book of his *Fables*, 1668), the ant is not merely careful but spectacularly unkind. Told that the grasshopper had sung all summer, she replies, in effect: *you sang? How delightful. Now dance.* It is the single most quoted piece of cruelty in children's literature, and it has been held up as a model for four centuries. Many of us have been quietly taught that the ant is right. Axelrod would say that she is playing a one-shot game in a repeated world, and that she may find herself, in some other winter, with nobody to share a grain with.

### Kindness is good for the kind

So far the case has been strategic: kindness pays, nastiness costs. But there is another, rather unfashionably direct argument: being kind makes the *giver* feel better.

In a 2008 study published in *Science*, Elizabeth Dunn, Lara Aknin and Michael Norton gave participants an envelope containing either $5 or $20 and told them to spend it by the end of the day. Half were told to spend it on themselves, and half on somebody else. At the end of the day, those who had spent the money on others reported being happier than those who had spent it on themselves, and the amount made little difference: five dollars on someone else did as well as twenty. A later study by Aknin and colleagues, analysing Gallup data from 136 countries, found that people who had donated to charity in the previous month reported higher wellbeing in the large majority of them, across rich countries and poor ones.

Some caution is needed, as it always is with psychology: effect sizes in this area are modest, and not every attempt to replicate a "kindness makes you happy" study has found a big effect. The honest summary is that kindness is not a miracle drug, but it is very rarely a *bad* investment, which is more than can be said for most of the things we spend money on.

Finally, kindness is catchy. In a 2010 paper in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, James Fowler and Nicholas Christakis ran an experiment in which participants played a repeated public-goods game, and found that generous behaviour by one player increased the generosity of those who later played with other people, and spread in turn to subsequent players, in a cascade that carried up to three degrees of separation. It is a clever study, though the results have been debated, and I would not hang a theory on it. But it gestures at something most people intuitively know: a tone set by one person tends to be taken up by the rest.

## Part three: the Franklin trick

We now come to the part that Gerald has been waiting for, and it is the practical hinge of this essay. If you do not feel kind, how are you supposed to *act* kind? Is that not simply hypocrisy?

The answer is one of the great and curiously neglected pieces of folk psychology, and it comes from Benjamin Franklin.

### The borrowed book

In his *Autobiography*, Franklin tells a story from his time as clerk of the Pennsylvania Assembly. A fellow member, a gentleman of means and education, had taken a decided dislike to him and made a speech against him. Franklin did not want to be the enemy of such a man. He did not flatter him, or do him a service. Instead, he did something peculiar: he asked to borrow a book. He had heard that the man owned a very scarce and curious volume, and he wrote to ask if he might read it for a few days. The man sent it at once. Franklin returned it about a week later with a note expressing his sincere thanks.

The next time they met in the House, the man spoke to Franklin, in Franklin's words, with great civility, and thereafter showed a readiness to serve him on all occasions, so that they became great friends and remained so until his death. Franklin draws a moral from an old maxim: *he that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another than he whom you yourself have obliged.*

This is now known as the **Ben Franklin effect**, and the interesting thing is that it has been tested. In 1969 Jecker and Landy ran an experiment in which participants took part in a competition for money, after which they were approached by either the experimenter, who asked them to return the winnings as a personal favour because he had been funding the experiment out of his own pocket, or by a secretary, who asked them to return it on behalf of the department, or by nobody at all. Those who had been asked a personal favour by the experimenter rated him as significantly more likeable than those in the other conditions. Having done him a kindness, they liked him more.

### Behaviour first, feelings later

The Franklin effect is a special case of a broader idea. In 1967 the psychologist Daryl Bem proposed "self-perception theory": people often work out what they believe and feel by *observing their own behaviour*, in much the way that an outsider would. If I find myself helping you, I infer that I must like you. If I find myself being polite to a colleague, I infer that I rather respect them. The feeling follows the action, not the other way round.

This is very good news for Gerald, who has hitherto assumed that action must wait for feeling, and that since he does not *feel* warmth he cannot honestly display any. The research suggests that this is backwards. A kind act, even a grudging one, shifts our own attitude a little in the direction of the person we helped. It is a way of building affection by doing, much as a person builds a taste for olives.

It also contains a trick that Gerald may find tactically useful. The most powerful thing he can do for a person he dislikes is not to flatter them or to bring them gifts, but to *ask them for a small favour* and then to thank them properly. Asking is a compliment. It signals that the other person is competent, trusted and worth asking. It starts a ledger. And it enlists the other person's own self-perception on your side.

### The empathy trap, and a warning about pretending

Notice what this does *not* require: it does not require Gerald to "feel what others feel". That is fortunate, because empathy, as it turns out, is not as reliable a guide as is generally supposed. The psychologist Paul Bloom, in his 2016 book *Against Empathy*, argued that the feeling of "emotional empathy", in which one shares another's pain, is a poor moral compass: it is parochial, favouring those who look and sound like us; it is innumerate, moving us more for one named child than for a thousand anonymous ones; and it can be exhausting, leading to burnout among carers. He distinguishes it from *compassion*, a warm concern for another's wellbeing that does not require you to feel their distress, and from *cognitive empathy*, which is simply the ability to understand what another person is thinking and feeling. For our purposes cognitive empathy and compassion are what matter, and both can be trained. You can understand perfectly well that a colleague is anxious about a deadline without having to catch the anxiety yourself.

A caution is in order, too. There is a difference between *behaving* kindly and *performing* a feeling you do not have. In her classic 1983 study of flight attendants at Delta Air Lines, *The Managed Heart*, the sociologist Arlie Hochschild described "emotional labour": the work of producing the appropriate emotional display as part of the job. She distinguished "surface acting", in which one simply puts on the face, from "deep acting", in which one tries to produce the underlying feeling. Subsequent research has tended to find that chronic surface acting is wearing: it is associated with exhaustion and burnout, whereas deep acting is less so.

So the advice is *not* "paste on a grin and hold it". It is: do the concrete, considerate thing; say the true, warm sentence you can sincerely say; and skip the theatre. "Thanks, that really helped" is something Gerald can say honestly. "I'm absolutely *delighted* to see you!" is not, and nobody will believe it. Sincere and small beats grand and false.

Which brings us, at last, to the toolkit.

## Part four: the toolkit

What follows is a set of ten tools. None requires a change of personality. Each takes under a minute. Most can be tried today, and several have been tested in controlled experiments. I have put them roughly in ascending order of difficulty, so Gerald can begin with the easy ones and work up to the humbling.

### Tool one: notice

Everything starts here, and the philosopher Simone Weil said it best. In a letter written in 1942 she described attention as the rarest and purest form of generosity. The line is quoted so often that it has lost its edge, but the point is a practical one. Most of what looks like rudeness is a failure of noticing: the person behind you at the till; the colleague who has gone quiet; the older man on the train who has been standing for twenty minutes; the fact that you have been talking for some time without drawing breath.

The tool is simple. *Before you speak or act in a social setting, take a three-second look around you.* Who is here? What is their state? Is anyone waiting for something? You are doing a risk assessment of the human environment, as you would on a building site. Gerald, a man who can spot a miscounted pallet at fifty paces, will find that he is rather good at this.

One variation is worth adopting immediately: **put the phone away.** A study by Ryan Dwyer and colleagues, published in 2018, sent groups out to restaurant dinners and asked half to keep their phones on the table and half to put them away. Those who kept their phones in sight were more distracted and enjoyed the meal less. Attention is zero-sum, and it is the first thing you give or withhold.

### Tool two: use their name, and greet them

Dale Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, published in 1936, has sold tens of millions of copies and been mocked about as often. It deserves better. Its central claim, that a person's name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language, is not a scientific finding, but it is a decent observation. Most of us are mildly warmed when someone remembers who we are.

The practical rule: greet people by name, and greet them *at all*. A "good morning" to the colleague at the next desk costs nothing and is, it turns out, a form of insurance. In offices, as in villages, the social temperature is set by the thousand tiny acknowledgements that make up a day. For many of the people Gerald works with, he is the one who walks past without a word, and they have concluded, reasonably, that he dislikes them.

If you are bad with names, do what the professionals do: repeat the name when you hear it ("Nice to meet you, Maryam"), use it once in the conversation, and write it down afterwards. Memory is a muscle, but it is also a filing system, and a filing system can be operated by a man with a notebook.

### Tool three: thank people, properly

Of all the small kindnesses, thanking is the one with the best evidence and the highest return, and also the one most under-used.

In 2018 Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley, of the Universities of Texas and Chicago, ran a series of experiments in which participants wrote a gratitude letter to someone who had helped them, and were then asked to predict how the recipient would feel on receiving it. The writers systematically *underestimated* how surprised and delighted the recipients would be, and *overestimated* how awkward the recipients would feel. We fear that a thank-you will seem effusive or embarrassing; in fact it is received as a pleasant shock. This pattern, an underestimate of how pleasing a kind word is, recurs throughout the research, and should make us suspicious of our own reluctance. When your instinct says "that would be awkward", the data suggests it will be fine.

A good thank-you has three parts: *the thing*, *the effect* and *the person*. Not "thanks for the report", but "thanks for getting the report in on Friday; it meant I could make the board meeting without panicking, and I noticed you'd flagged the supplier risk, which I would have missed." Specific thanks says: I noticed, and it mattered. Generic thanks is merely polite.

Gerald's homework: thank three people a day, with specifics. This will feel contrived for about a week.

The corollary, for British readers in particular, is that "cheers" and "ta" and "ta very much" and "thank you, driver" (shouted at the front of a London bus as one alights from the rear) are not trivial. They are a *system*, and keeping it running is a collective effort. Nobody has ever been annoyed by a bus passenger who said thank you.

### Tool four: say the nice thing out loud

It is a curious fact that people think kind thoughts about others all the time and then keep them to themselves. A colleague gave an excellent presentation. A neighbour's garden looks lovely. A friend's new haircut works. The thought occurs; the sentence does not.

Research suggests why. In a 2021 paper, Erica Boothby and Vanessa Bohns found that people who gave compliments systematically underestimated how good the compliment would make the recipient feel, and overestimated how awkward it would be to receive. Subsequent work by Xuan Zhao and Nicholas Epley found much the same: we underestimate the positive impact of compliments, and that underestimate is itself the barrier that stops us from giving them.

The rule is: *if you think something good about someone, and it is true, say it.* Do it quickly, before the discomfort builds, and keep it short. "That was a really clear explanation." "You were right about the supplier." "That's a great jacket." No hanging about for the reaction. And note that the compliment should be *about something*. Compliments on appearance are a minefield at work; compliments on competence and effort are almost always safe and almost always rare.

### Tool five: ask, and say yes more than you think you should

Two quick findings about asking for help. First, we are terrible at predicting whether others will say yes. In a series of experiments in 2008, Francis Flynn and Vanessa Lake found that people dramatically underestimate how likely strangers are to comply with a direct request for help, and, again, overestimate how awkward the asked person will feel. If you are a person who finds it difficult to ask for help, this is good news: your friends and colleagues are more willing than you think.

Second, as we saw with Franklin, asking is a kindness in itself. A request for help is a compliment, a vote of confidence. Gerald, who has always regarded asking as a sign of weakness, may find that asking for a small piece of advice from a colleague he has been neglecting does more for their relationship than any gift.

The flip side: **say yes when you can.** Treat requests as a small tax that society levies on the competent, and pay it cheerfully when it is cheap. Hold the lift. Take the photo. Look at the draft. Answer the email. The people who are the most pleasant to deal with are not those who say yes to everything (see Part six), but those who say yes to what is easy, at once, without a sigh.

### Tool six: talk to the stranger

Here is a test of nerve. In 2014 Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder asked commuters on trains and buses in the Chicago area to do one of three things: talk to a stranger sitting next to them, sit in solitude, or do whatever they would normally do. Beforehand they asked other commuters to predict how they would feel in each condition. The predictions were confident and wrong. People expected solitude to be the more pleasant option, and expected a conversation to be awkward and unwelcome. In fact, those who talked to a stranger reported a *more positive* commute than those who sat alone, and the conversation did not reduce their productivity. The researchers called the paper "Mistakenly Seeking Solitude".

A related finding, from a 2018 paper by Erica Boothby, Gus Cooney, Gillian Sandstrom and Margaret Clark, is what they call the "liking gap". After a conversation with a stranger, people consistently underestimate how much the other person liked them. We replay our own awkwardness and forget that the other person was doing precisely the same. The gap was found in conversations between strangers, between first-year students living together and in workshop settings, and it lasted for months. The upshot is the opposite of what Gerald assumes. People mostly like us more than we think, and the conversation we are dreading is probably going to go fine.

If small talk is not your thing, be reassured: it is not about what it seems to be about. The anthropologist Kate Fox, in her 2004 book *Watching the English*, argued that English conversation about the weather is not a conversation about the weather at all but a kind of ritual, a social greeting with rules of its own. "Cold enough for you?" means "I acknowledge you as a person, and I come in peace." The proper reply is not meteorological data but agreement and a bit of grumbling. A minute of weather is a polite way of saying hello. Practise it. You are allowed to be bad at it.

### Tool seven: listen, and resist the fix

The most undervalued kindness is listening, and the commonest failure is jumping straight to the solution.

Gerald's instinct, on hearing that a colleague is stressed about a project, is to give a three-step plan. The colleague, in nine cases out of ten, did not want a plan. They wanted to be heard. There is nothing wrong with solutions, but the sequence matters: acknowledge first, solve afterwards, and *only if invited*. A sentence like "That sounds exhausting. Do you want ideas, or do you just want to vent?" is worth more than an hour of unsolicited consulting.

Practical listening comes down to a few moves: *look at the person*; *let them finish*; *repeat back the gist* ("so the problem isn't the deadline, it's that nobody told you it moved"); and *ask one more question than you think you need to*. The last is worth emphasising. A follow-up question is the clearest evidence of interest that a person can give, because it can't be faked without listening.

There is also a very good technique for the moment when you feel an angry reply rising. In 2007 Matthew Lieberman and colleagues showed, in a brain-imaging study, that simply *putting a feeling into words*, a process called affect labelling, reduces the response in the amygdala, the brain region associated with threat. It is the neurological version of counting to ten, and it works on yourself. When you feel the heat, silently name it: *I'm annoyed. I'm embarrassed. I'm worried about the deadline.* The name takes some of the voltage out. Then you choose your sentence.

### Tool eight: apologise like an adult

Gerald's apologies, when they occur, resemble legal disclaimers. "I'm sorry if you felt that way." "Mistakes were made." The second of these is not, as it happens, merely a cliché: it is a famous one. When Ronald Reagan addressed the American people in 1987 about the Iran–Contra affair, he said that serious mistakes had been made, and the passive construction became a byword for an apology that declines to say who did what. Gerald is in distinguished company; it is not good company.

What does a good apology look like? In 2016 Roy Lewicki, Beth Polin and Robert Lount analysed what makes apologies effective and identified six components: an expression of regret, an explanation of what went wrong, an acknowledgement of responsibility, a declaration of repentance, an offer of repair, and a request for forgiveness. Of the six, the most important was *acknowledging responsibility*, and the second most important was the *offer of repair*. Bare regret, in other words, is the least of it. Notice that what matters is the part Gerald likes least: owning the error.

A workable formula, for use by men of few words: **"I got [X] wrong. It caused [Y]. I'm sorry. Here's what I'll do about it."** For example: "I snapped at you in the meeting. That was unfair, and it made you look bad in front of the team. I'm sorry. I'll say so to them tomorrow." Four sentences. No "but". No "if". It will feel like swallowing a stone. It works.

### Tool nine: disagree without wounding

A common fear among the Geralds of this world is that kindness means never disagreeing. It does not. The skill is to disagree about the *thing* while sparing the *person*.

The sociologist Erving Goffman gave us the useful concept of "face": the positive social image that each of us claims in an interaction, and that we are all quietly working to protect. The linguists Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson, building on Goffman in the late 1970s, developed a theory of politeness in which most of what we call manners is a set of devices for softening what they called "face-threatening acts": requests, criticisms, refusals, corrections. Every time you say "I wonder if we might consider" instead of "that's wrong", you are performing a repair. Politeness is not hypocrisy. It is a way of delivering bad news without a head injury.

The negotiation scholars Roger Fisher and William Ury, in *Getting to Yes* (1981), put the central principle in a phrase that has become a mantra: *separate the people from the problem.* Be hard on the problem and soft on the person. Attack the proposal, not the proposer. Ask "what would have to be true for this to work?" rather than "how could you possibly think that?"

And there is the best rule of argument I know, which the philosopher Daniel Dennett attributed to the same Anatol Rapoport whose Tit for Tat won Axelrod's tournament. Before you criticise a position, you should: (1) re-express the other person's view so clearly, vividly and fairly that they say, "Thanks, I wish I'd put it that way"; (2) list any points of agreement, especially ones that are not widely shared; (3) mention anything you have learned from them; and only then (4) say what you wish to say by way of rebuttal. This is ferociously difficult, and it makes you formidable in an argument for the best reason: you will have understood the opposing case before attacking it.

The English had an early master of the manner without the substance. Philip Stanhope, Earl of Chesterfield, wrote hundreds of letters of advice to his illegitimate son, published after his death in 1774 and long the standard guide to polished behaviour. They contain advice of impeccable shrewdness ("Be wiser than other people if you can, but do not tell them so") and a good deal of counsel about how to appear agreeable while pursuing one's own interest. Samuel Johnson, who had his own reasons for disliking Chesterfield (the earl's neglect of him during the writing of his *Dictionary* was the subject of a famously cold letter), judged the letters to teach the morals of a whore and the manners of a dancing master. It is a fair warning. Manners without consideration is a costume; the point of this essay is to give Gerald something underneath.

### Tool ten: give them the platinum rule

You will have heard of the Golden Rule, which is some version of "do as you would be done by". It is very ancient. In the *Analects*, when a disciple asks Confucius whether there is a single word that can guide a person throughout life, the master answers with the idea of reciprocity: what you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others. Versions appear in the Hebrew tradition, in the Christian gospels, in Hindu and Buddhist texts and elsewhere. It is probably the nearest thing humanity has to a universal ethical principle.

It has a flaw, which George Bernard Shaw spotted in 1903. In the "Maxims for Revolutionists" appended to *Man and Superman*, he wrote: "Do not do unto others as you would that they should do unto you. Their tastes may not be the same." Shaw's inversion is a useful warning for someone like Gerald, who, if asked what he would like, would say "to be left alone with a spreadsheet", and who might consequently leave everyone else alone with a spreadsheet as well. The rule is sound; the trouble is that we apply it with ourselves as the sample.

The upgrade is sometimes called the Platinum Rule: do unto others as *they* would like to be done unto. It requires one extra step, which is to find out. Some colleagues want public praise; others would die of it. Some want direct feedback; others need it softened. Some want you to fix their problem; others want you to shut up and nod. And the way to find out is the cheapest tool in the box: *ask*. "How do you like to get feedback?" "Would you rather I called or messaged?" "Do you want advice or just an ear?" You will rarely get a better question for the price.

### Bonus tool: the impartial spectator

Finally, there is a mental device from the man we met at dinner. In *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, Adam Smith argued that we form moral judgements by imagining how an impartial observer would view our conduct: a kind of "man within the breast", well-informed, fair and not on our side. We learn to see ourselves as others see us, and in the moment of temptation, to ask what the spectator would think.

Gerald can use this directly. Before sending an email in a temper, he can imagine a sensible stranger reading it aloud in a meeting. Before snapping at someone, he can imagine a fair-minded observer watching. It is not a perfect device, but it is cheap, and the spectator is usually right about whether you are being a git.

### A last tool for the hard days: charity

One more, borrowed from an emperor. The Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius, in the notebook that we now call the *Meditations*, which he wrote in Greek for himself and never intended to publish, opens the second book with a rehearsal of the morning's likely irritations. Today, he tells himself, I shall meet the meddling, the ungrateful, the arrogant, the deceitful, the envious and the unsociable. And then he gives the reason why he need not be angry: they behave like this because they do not know good from evil, and they are, after all, of the same kind as himself, made for cooperation.

You may think this is easy for an emperor. He was a man at the head of an empire, campaigning on the Danube frontier, who wrote this between battles and while struggling with illness. He had rather more reason than most to treat his fellow men as pests; and he chose to regard them as people who were mostly doing their best, not very well.

The modern version of the same thought is known as Hanlon's razor ("never attribute to malice what is adequately explained by stupidity", though the wording was sharper in the joke book of 1980 in which it was given its name). Gerald, who has been wronged by a great many people, would do well to adopt a gentler version: *assume the person who cut you up in the traffic did not see you.* They usually did not. And if they did, you will have wasted less of your day than if you had spent it plotting.

## Part five: the difficult terrains

Tools are one thing; the places where you use them are another. Gerald is not equally nasty everywhere. He is a different man in the queue, in the inbox, at the wheel, online and at home, and each setting has its own temptations and its own fixes.

### The queue

George Mikes, the Hungarian-born humorist, wrote in *How to be an Alien* (1946) that an Englishman, even if he is alone, forms an orderly queue of one. It is a joke with a serious kernel. The queue is a small, spontaneous institution founded on a principle of fairness: first come, first served. Nobody enforces it, which is why it is an excellent test of character. When you push in, you are not breaking a law; you are quietly informing a dozen strangers that your time matters more than theirs.

The rule for the queue is simple. Join at the end. Do not ask the person at the front if you can "just" do something. Have your payment ready. Say "thank you" to the person who serves you, and look at them while you do. And if somebody else pushes in, exercise the charity of the previous section before the indignation of this one: they may be foreign, flustered, or simply not looking.

### The inbox

A great deal of Gerald's nastiness occurs in writing, because writing strips away the face, the voice and the shrug that usually take the edge off a sentence. In 2005 Justin Kruger and colleagues, in a paper on "egocentrism over e-mail", asked people to send messages that were either serious or sarcastic, and to predict whether the recipient would grasp the tone. Senders predicted that they would succeed roughly three-quarters of the time; in fact, recipients got it right little more than half the time, barely above chance. The senders could "hear" the tone in their heads, and assumed that the recipient would too.

The consequence is that email defaults to the *worst available reading*. Terse is read as cold; cold is read as angry. Gerald's habit of stripping greetings "to save time" saves perhaps two seconds and costs him, in aggregate, the goodwill of a department.

The fixes are unglamorous. Open with a greeting and the person's name. Add one sentence of warmth where it is true ("thanks for turning this round so quickly"). Say what you want and by when, in plain words. Use "please", which costs one syllable. Reread anything written in irritation before sending, preferably after a cup of tea. And if an email thread has gone more than two rounds with rising heat, *pick up the phone or walk over.* Nine-tenths of workplace feuds are conducted by email between people who would get on perfectly well over a table.

### The internet

If email is bad, social media is worse. In 2004 the psychologist John Suler described the "online disinhibition effect": the way in which people say and do things online that they would never do face to face. He identified several ingredients, among them anonymity, invisibility, the delay between sending and being answered, and the sense that the online world is a game with different rules. The result is that mildly irritable people become astonishingly vicious, in a medium where the target is an avatar and the audience is invisible.

The tool here is a modification of Smith's impartial spectator. Before you post, ask: *would I say this, in this tone, to this person's face, in front of their mother?* If the answer is no, you have a choice between rewriting and deleting, and the best option is often the third one, which is to do neither and go and do something else. The unsent angry post is one of the great unrecorded triumphs of civilisation.

### The road

Cars are nasty-making machines. They shield us in a box that conceals our face and makes the other drivers into obstacles rather than humans, and they supply a sense of ownership of a stretch of tarmac that is, in truth, shared. Everything we know about disinhibition applies, with the added feature of two tonnes of steel.

A good tool: wave. When someone lets you in, raise a hand. When you let someone in, expect nothing. The ritual of the thank-you wave is one of the most successful bits of informal etiquette on the roads, because it costs nothing and rewards the courteous. Gerald, who tends to treat other drivers as personal affronts, could try something radical: assume each of them is somebody's sister.

### The home

Last, and most important, there is the place where nasty people are often at their worst: with the people who love them. We save our best manners for strangers, and our worst for the people who are least able to leave.

The psychologist John Gottman has spent decades watching couples in his "love lab" in Seattle, and he has reported a pattern that is easily summarised. Happy couples are not those who never argue, but those who maintain a high ratio of positive to negative interactions, roughly five to one during conflict, and who respond to what he calls "bids for connection": the small attempts that one partner makes to get the other's attention ("look at that bird", "have you seen this article?", a sigh from the next room). In one of his studies, couples who stayed together turned towards such bids around 86 per cent of the time; those who later divorced did so about a third of the time.

Think about what this implies. The glue of a relationship is not grand gestures but the small, constantly renewed acknowledgement that the other person has said something and that you heard it. "Mm, yes, I saw it" costs nothing, and it is the same as "I noticed you." For Gerald, who does not look up from the laptop when his wife says there is a heron on the pond, the lesson is so obvious that it is rather painful. *Look up.* Look at the heron.

## Part six: the limits of niceness

Having spent several thousand words recommending consideration, it would be dishonest not to say where it can go wrong. There are three ways.

**First, kindness is not the same as agreeableness.** A person who cannot say no, who agrees to everything, who hides every disagreement behind a smile, is not kind; they are *conflict-averse*, and the cost of this is usually borne later, by someone else. Honest feedback is a kindness, delivered with care. The manager who never tells an employee that their work is poor is not being nice; they are letting the employee fail slowly. The aim is to be *clear and considerate at once*, which is harder than either alone. Axelrod's programs, you will recall, were retaliatory as well as nice; the doormat strategy lost.

**Second, giving without limit is a path to ruin.** Grant's "givers" who finished at the bottom were those who gave indiscriminately and burned out. Compassion fatigue is a documented thing, particularly in health and care work. The sustainable kindness is *boundaried*: you decide in advance how much time, money and energy you can afford, you say no clearly when you are over that, and you recognise that a refusal can be delivered considerately. "I can't take that on this week, but I could look at it Thursday" is a kind sentence.

**Third, some people will take advantage.** There are, it must be said, people who treat kindness as weakness. Axelrod's findings are reassuring on this point: the successful strategy responds to exploitation. If somebody repeatedly takes without giving, you are entitled to stop giving. The art is to retaliate *proportionately and once*, to leave the door open for a return to cooperation, and not to turn a one-off slight into a vendetta. (In practice this often means declining the next favour, without a speech.) And there is a harder category: where kindness is being demanded by someone who is abusing you, bullying you or otherwise harming you, being considerate to them is not a virtue. Nothing in this essay is an argument for tolerating that. The right response to such a person is distance, support and, if necessary, outside help.

It is also worth saying that the instruction to be kind is *not* an instruction to be liked by everyone. Some people will not warm to you whatever you do. That is acceptable. The aim is that you should be *decent*, not universally beloved.

## Part seven: thirty days

Habits are built by repetition, and the most useful thing I can offer Gerald by way of conclusion is a schedule. It is deliberately modest. Do not attempt it all at once.

**Week one: noticing.** Each day, do three things. Take the three-second look at the room before you speak in a meeting. Say good morning, by name, to everyone you pass at work. Put the phone out of reach during at least one meal or conversation. Do not try to be kind yet; only to notice.

**Week two: thanking and saying.** Each day, thank three people with specifics, in person or in writing. Pay one sincere compliment that you would otherwise have kept to yourself. Write one email with a greeting, a "please" and a "thanks". Keep a tally in your notebook; the numbers will be small and then not so small.

**Week three: asking and listening.** Ask one colleague for a small favour or a piece of advice, and thank them properly afterwards. In one conversation per day, ask a follow-up question and resist the fix. Once, when you feel irritation rising, name it silently before replying.

**Week four: repair and disagreement.** Apologise, properly, for one thing you have been avoiding apologising for. Disagree once using the Rapoport rules, restating the other person's view before you criticise it. Ask someone how they like to be treated at work, and then do it.

At the end, ask two or three people you trust, "Has anything changed?" You may be surprised. Equally, you may find that nobody has noticed, which is the more likely result after a month and is not a failure: habits are noticed at the scale of seasons, not weeks. What you are aiming for is not applause but the quiet drop in friction that follows when a person becomes, for the first time, *easy to deal with*.

A short table of the whole thing, for the fridge:

| Situation | Nasty default | Considerate alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Someone holds a door | Walk through | "Thanks," and eye contact |
| Colleague finishes a task | Silence | A specific thank-you |
| Email | Terse, no greeting | Greeting, name, one warm line, "please" |
| Disagreement | "That's wrong." | Restate their view first; criticise the idea |
| You were wrong | "Mistakes were made" | "I got X wrong. Here's what I'll do." |
| Someone is venting | Offer a three-point plan | "That sounds awful. Want ideas, or an ear?" |
| Stranger on the train | Pretend they are furniture | A nod, or a remark about the weather |
| Partner says "look at that bird" | Grunt | Look at the bird |

## Epilogue: Gerald, a year later

Priya, who has not thought about Gerald in months, bumps into him in a supermarket. He is holding a basket with an alarming quantity of cheese.

"You look well," she says.

"I'm not *nice*," says Gerald, quickly. "Let's be clear. I haven't had a conversion."

"Of course not."

"But I do the thing with the thank-yous. And Dave in logistics has started copying me into things again. And last week, a girl on the till told me I was her favourite customer, which, frankly, I assumed was sarcasm."

"Was it?"

"I looked at her face. I think it was genuine." Gerald considers the cheese. "It is unnerving. I've done almost nothing. I say 'good morning'. I look at the people I'm talking to. I say 'sorry' when I've been an idiot, which turns out to be quite often. And it's as though somebody has turned down a noise I'd stopped hearing."

"That's what it tends to be like," says Priya.

"The odd thing," says Gerald, "is that I do not feel any *better*. I just feel as though there is less to be defended."

It is not a bad summary. The case for being considerate is rarely a thunderbolt. It does not offer you a transformation, a new self or an inner glow. It offers something much more modest and more reliable: the gradual removal of a thousand tiny frictions, the occasional unexpected kindness in return, and the discovery that most of the people around you were not in fact your adversaries. They were mostly in a hurry, as you were, and doing their best.

Thomas Hobbes feared that without a strong ruler life would be solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short. He was right that cooperation does not come for free. He was wrong, or at least incomplete, in thinking that a sovereign is the only way to get it. We build it, one considerate act at a time, in queues and inboxes and kitchens, and nobody is exempt from the work, least of all those of us who would rather be left alone.

You do not have to be nice. You have only to notice, to say thank you, and to look at the heron.

For those who wish to take any of this further, these are good places to start:

- Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984): the tournaments, in readable detail.
- Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759): far warmer than its author's reputation suggests.
- Charles Darwin, *The Descent of Man* (1871), especially chapters 3 and 4 on the moral sense.
- Peter Kropotkin, *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution* (1902).
- Benjamin Franklin, *The Autobiography*: the borrowed-book episode is in the account of his early career in Pennsylvania.
- John Darley and Daniel Batson, "From Jerusalem to Jericho" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1973).
- Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder, "Mistakenly Seeking Solitude" (*Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 2014).
- Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley, "Undervaluing Gratitude" (*Psychological Science*, 2018).
- Roy Lewicki, Beth Polin and Robert Lount, "An Exploration of the Structure of Effective Apologies" (2016).
- Roger Fisher and William Ury, *Getting to Yes* (1981).
- Kate Fox, *Watching the English* (2004).
- George Mikes, *How to be an Alien* (1946).
- Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*: any good modern translation.
- Robert Sutton, *The No Asshole Rule* (2007), for the workplace.
- Paul Bloom, *Against Empathy* (2016), for a sceptical view of feeling as a guide.
- John Gottman and Nan Silver, *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work* (1999).

*A note on the evidence: social psychology has had a difficult decade, and some well-known findings have failed to replicate. Where this essay cites individual studies, treat them as illustrations of a pattern that is supported by wider research, and not as settled laws of nature. If a result surprises you, go and read the original.*
