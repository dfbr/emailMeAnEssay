## The Night the Sky Caught Fire

In 1801 a painter called Philip James de Loutherbourg exhibited a picture of a small valley in Shropshire. It shows a night scene. In the foreground a horse and cart trudge along a rutted track; in the background, beyond a straggle of buildings, the sky has caught fire. Great billows of orange and crimson rise from the Bedlam furnaces in the Severn Gorge, lighting up the clouds from below. If you did not know what you were looking at, you would assume it was a battle, or a volcano, or the end of the world.

It was, in a sense, all three. Loutherbourg had painted an ironworks, and the ironworks of the Severn Gorge were the front line of the most important change in human life since farming. Visitors came from all over Europe to stare at them. Some were thrilled; some were horrified; most were both. The painting captures that double feeling exactly. It is a picture of power, and of something not quite under control.

This essay is about what was happening in that valley and in hundreds like it: the Industrial Revolution. It is a story with a lot of machines in it, and the machines matter. But it is mainly a story about people: a barber who became the richest commoner in England, a Scottish instrument-maker who could not stop worrying, a clergyman who invented a loom despite never having seen one work, the children who minded the spinning frames, the weavers who smashed them, and the millions who left the fields for the cities and never went back.

It is also a story we are still inside. The electricity you are reading this by, the clock that tells you when to start work, the town you probably live in, the carbon in the air and the very idea that next year should be richer than this one: all of these came out of that orange sky over Shropshire.

## The Graph That Goes Up

Economic historians have a favourite chart, and once you have seen it you never quite forget it. Plot the average income of a person on Earth, adjusted for prices, from the time of the Romans to today. For about seventeen centuries the line is almost perfectly flat. Empires rise and fall, plagues come and go, the printing press is invented and America is discovered, and the line barely twitches. Then, around 1800, it bends upwards. By 1900 it is climbing steeply. By 2000 it is shooting off the top of the page. The shape is usually called a hockey stick, and the blade begins in Britain.

To understand why this matters, you need to grasp how stuck the world had been. For most of history the rule was simple, and the English clergyman Thomas Malthus wrote it down in 1798, ironically just as it was about to stop being true. If a society found a way to produce more food, it got richer for a while; people ate better, married earlier and had more surviving children; the population grew until there were more mouths than the extra food could feed; and everyone was poor again. Improvements in technology produced more people, not richer ones. A labourer in 1700 lived much as a labourer had in 1300, and expected his grandchildren to live the same way.

The Industrial Revolution broke that trap. For the first time, an economy grew faster than its population, year after year, decade after decade, and kept doing so. Britain's population roughly doubled between 1801 and 1851, from about 10.5 million to nearly 21 million, and yet by the end of that period ordinary incomes were rising rather than falling. That had never happened before, anywhere, for long.

It is worth saying straight away what the revolution was *not*. It was not quick. The phrase "Industrial Revolution" conjures up a sudden explosion, a Bastille moment with steam instead of muskets. In fact economic historians spent much of the 1980s, led by Nicholas Crafts and Knick Harley, revising the growth figures downwards. Between about 1760 and 1830, British income per head grew at well under one per cent a year: respectable, but hardly a rocket. Most British workers in 1830 still did not work in factories. They farmed, they sewed, they served in big houses, they mended shoes. The revolution was a slow fuse that took a century to burn, not a bomb.

Nor was the name contemporary. People living through it did not know they were in "the Industrial Revolution". French writers used the phrase in the 1820s, Friedrich Engels used it in the 1840s, but it was the historian Arnold Toynbee (uncle of the more famous Arnold J. Toynbee) whose Oxford lectures, published after his death in 1884, made it stick in English. By then the revolution was a hundred years old.

But slow does not mean small. What changed was not the speed of growth so much as its permanence. Before, good times always ended. After, they compounded. One per cent a year sounds trivial; compound it over two centuries and an economy grows more than sevenfold. That is the blade of the hockey stick, and it is why your life looks nothing like that of your great-great-great-great-grandparents.

## Why Britain? Why Then?

This is the great question, and historians have been arguing about it for 150 years with no sign of stopping. Plenty of places had been rich and clever before. Song dynasty China had blast furnaces, paper money and gunpowder. The Dutch Republic in the seventeenth century was the most commercial society on Earth. Mughal India made the finest cotton cloth in the world, so fine that the English had to pass laws to stop people buying it. Why did the bend in the line happen on a damp, middling island off the north-west coast of Europe?

There is no single answer, but the serious candidates fit together rather well, so let us walk through them.

### Coal under the feet

Britain was sitting on an enormous amount of easily reached coal, and much of it lay close to rivers, coasts and, later, canals. By the eighteenth century Britain had already burned through a lot of its woodland, and the price of firewood was rising. Londoners had been burning "sea coal" shipped down from Newcastle since the Middle Ages, much to the disgust of anyone with a nose. Coal was the fossil sunlight of 300 million years ago, and it was there for the digging.

### Expensive people, cheap energy

The economic historian Robert Allen has made an influential argument about prices. In eighteenth-century Britain, wages were unusually high compared with most of Europe and Asia, while energy, thanks to coal, was unusually cheap. Put those together and you have a powerful incentive to invent machines that replace expensive human muscle with cheap coal-fired power. The same spinning jenny that was a bargain in Lancashire would not pay for itself in France or India, where labour cost so much less. In Allen's view, British inventors were not cleverer than anyone else; they were just responding to a peculiar set of prices.

Critics point out that this cannot be the whole story: high wages existed in the Netherlands too, and plenty of profitable inventions were never made. But the argument explains something important, which is why the first machines were so obviously aimed at saving labour.

### A culture of tinkering

The historian Joel Mokyr stresses something less tangible: ideas. Britain had come out of the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment with a culture that took practical knowledge seriously, and with networks through which it flowed. Mokyr calls it the "Industrial Enlightenment". Natural philosophers talked to craftsmen; craftsmen read the philosophers; everybody wrote letters.

The most famous of these networks was the Lunar Society of Birmingham, a dining club that met on the Monday nearest the full moon, so that members could ride home by its light. Its members included Matthew Boulton the manufacturer, James Watt the engineer, Josiah Wedgwood the potter, Erasmus Darwin the doctor (grandfather of Charles) and Joseph Priestley, the chemist who isolated oxygen. Imagine a dinner party where the conversation wanders from electricity to canal-building to the chemistry of glazes to the abolition of slavery, and where the guests go home and actually build things. They called themselves, cheerfully, the "Lunaticks".

### Laws, markets and money

Britain had also become a fairly stable, fairly unified market. After the political upheavals of the seventeenth century, the Glorious Revolution of 1688 put Parliament firmly in charge of taxes and gave property owners confidence that the Crown would not simply seize their wealth. There were no internal customs barriers of the kind that strangled trade in France and the German states. There was a patent system, imperfect and expensive but real. There was a sophisticated financial system centred on the Bank of England (founded 1694) and a growing network of country banks. And there was a large and growing consumer market: the British were eager buyers of cotton, crockery, buckles, buttons and tea.

### Food in the larder

Before you can move people into factories, someone has to feed them. English agriculture had become steadily more productive over the previous century or two, through new crop rotations (turnips and clover, which fed animals over winter and put nitrogen back into the soil), selective breeding, and enclosure, the often bitterly resented process of turning open fields and commons into private, hedged farms. Enclosure pushed many poor families off the land, which was a tragedy for them and, coldly, a source of labour for the new mills.

### Empire, trade and slavery

Finally, there is the question no honest account can skip. Britain in the eighteenth century was the world's leading slave-trading nation, and its sugar islands in the Caribbean made planters and merchants enormously rich. In 1944 the Trinidadian historian Eric Williams, later his country's first prime minister, argued in *Capitalism and Slavery* that the profits of the slave trade and plantations helped finance the Industrial Revolution.

Historians have argued about the "Williams thesis" ever since. Most now think that slave-trade profits were too small, relative to the whole British economy, to have paid for industrialisation directly. But the connections are real and deep. Liverpool and Bristol grew rich on the trade. The cotton that fed Lancashire's mills was, by the early nineteenth century, overwhelmingly grown by enslaved people in the American South; by the 1830s and 1840s, roughly three-quarters of Britain's raw cotton came from there. Kenneth Pomeranz, in his book *The Great Divergence* (2000), argued that coal and colonies together, the one underground and the other overseas, freed Britain from the land constraints that boxed in equally advanced regions such as the Yangtze delta. The mills of Manchester were built on Lancashire rain and Lancashire ingenuity, but also on the backs of people in Mississippi and Alabama who never saw a penny of the profits.

Put all this together and you get not a single cause but a recipe: coal, high wages, cheap energy, practical science, secure property, a big market, productive farms, and the profits and raw materials of a global empire. Many places had some of the ingredients. Britain, in the middle of the eighteenth century, happened to have all of them in the same kitchen.

## Cotton: The Fabric of Revolution

If you want to understand the early Industrial Revolution, follow the cotton.

At the start of the eighteenth century, the fashionable fabric in Britain was Indian calico: light, washable, brightly printed and far more comfortable than wool. The wool trade, which had been England's great industry since the Middle Ages, was furious, and in 1700 and 1721 Parliament passed the Calico Acts, restricting the import and then the wearing of printed cottons. Protectionism of this kind is not usually a recipe for innovation. In this case, by keeping Indian cloth out, it gave British manufacturers a market they badly wanted to fill themselves, if only they could make cotton cheaply enough.

The problem was that making cloth involves two quite different jobs. Spinning twists loose fibre into thread. Weaving interlaces threads into fabric. For centuries both were done by hand, usually at home, in what was called the "putting-out" or "domestic" system: a merchant delivered raw material to cottages, families spun or wove it, and the merchant collected the finished goods. It took several spinners to keep one weaver supplied with thread.

Then, one after another, a series of machines tipped the balance back and forth, each invention creating a bottleneck that the next one solved.

In 1733 John Kay, a Lancashire man, patented the **flying shuttle**, which let a weaver knock the shuttle across the loom with a cord rather than passing it by hand. Weaving became much faster, and the shortage of thread grew worse.

Around 1764, James Hargreaves, a weaver from Stanhill near Blackburn, built the **spinning jenny**, a hand-cranked frame that let one person spin eight threads at once, and later dozens. (The story that it was named after his wife or daughter is charming and probably untrue; "jenny" was a common slang term for an engine.) His neighbours, who earned their living spinning by hand, were not charmed: in 1768 a mob broke into his house and smashed his machines, and Hargreaves moved to Nottingham.

![An improved spinning jenny, from the Penny Magazine of 1836. One operator turned the wheel while drawing out many threads at once.](/assets/essays/smoke-and-steam/images/jenny.jpg)
*Hargreaves' spinning jenny, woodcut from the Penny Magazine (1836). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The jenny still needed a person to work it, and its thread was rather weak. The real breakthrough came from a man who could hardly have been less like the polite philosophers of the Lunar Society.

### The barber of Preston

Richard Arkwright was born in Preston in 1732, the youngest of seven surviving children of a poor tailor. He trained as a barber and wig-maker and was, by most accounts, ambitious, energetic, quarrelsome and not overly troubled by other people's intellectual property. In 1769 he patented the **water frame**, a spinning machine driven by a water wheel that produced strong cotton thread suitable for the lengthwise threads of cloth. Whether he really invented it is still disputed; a clockmaker called John Kay (no relation to the shuttle man) had worked on a similar machine with a reed-maker named Thomas Highs, and Kay later helped Arkwright build his. The courts eventually overturned Arkwright's patents in 1785.

But Arkwright's genius was less in the machine than in what he did with it. In 1771, at Cromford in Derbyshire, beside a fast stream, he built a mill that was designed from the start around machines, run on water power, staffed by workers who came to the machine rather than the other way round, and operated on strict shifts round the clock. It is usually considered the first true factory system. He built houses for his workers, a chapel, a market and a pub, and recruited whole families, preferring those with many children because children were cheap and nimble.

![Arkwright's Mills by night, painted by Joseph Wright of Derby around 1795-96: the lit windows of the Cromford mills shining out over the Derwent valley.](/assets/essays/smoke-and-steam/images/arkwright.jpg)
*Joseph Wright of Derby, Arkwright's Mills (c. 1795-96), Derby Museum and Art Gallery. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Joseph Wright of Derby painted Arkwright's mills at night, their rows of windows glowing in the dark valley. To the people who saw them, they were astonishing: a building lit up like a palace at midnight, full of people working when everyone else was asleep. Arkwright copied his model across Derbyshire, Lancashire and Scotland, was knighted in 1786 and died in 1792 worth something like half a million pounds, a fortune that put him among the richest commoners in the country. Not bad for a barber.

Let us imagine a conversation at Cromford in about 1785. The facts in it are true; the people are invented.

> **A visiting gentleman:** I am told your mill never stops. Surely your people must sleep?
>
> **The mill manager:** They do, sir, in turns. The bell rings at six, morning and evening, and one shift takes over from the other. The machines don't tire.
>
> **Gentleman:** And those small figures by the frames, are they children?
>
> **Manager:** Most of our hands are children, sir, from about seven years old. Their fingers are quick, and they piece the broken threads better than any man. Their parents are glad of the wages.
>
> **Gentleman:** And if a child falls asleep at the frame?
>
> **Manager:** Then the overlooker wakes him, sir.

### Mules, looms and the Lancashire boom

In 1779 Samuel Crompton, a shy young man from near Bolton, combined the jenny and the water frame into a hybrid that produced thread both strong and fine. Because it was a cross between two machines, it was called the **spinning mule**. Crompton could not afford to patent it and was talked into sharing the design for a subscription that raised a pittance. The mule went on to become the workhorse of the cotton industry, with millions of spindles turning across Lancashire; Parliament eventually awarded Crompton £5,000 in 1812, a fraction of what his invention had made for others.

With spinning mechanised, the bottleneck swung back to weaving. In 1785 Edmund Cartwright, a clergyman and poet who had reportedly never seen a weaver at work, patented a **power loom** after a conversation in which some Manchester men told him that weaving could never be mechanised. His first version was clumsy, and it took decades of improvement by others before power looms became reliable. When they did, from the 1820s onwards, they wiped out the livelihoods of hundreds of thousands of hand-loom weavers, once among the best-paid craftsmen in England, whose wages collapsed to starvation levels.

![Power loom weaving in 1835, engraved after a drawing by Thomas Allom: long rows of looms in a mill, tended mainly by women.](/assets/essays/smoke-and-steam/images/powerloom.jpg)
*Power loom weaving in 1835, drawn by Thomas Allom and engraved by J. Tingle, from Edward Baines's History of the Cotton Manufacture in Great Britain. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The numbers that followed are almost absurd. Britain's imports of raw cotton grew more than a hundredfold between the 1760s and 1850. Cotton became Britain's biggest export, and Lancashire became the workshop of the world's wardrobe. In the early nineteenth century, a striking reversal took place: India, which had clothed the world for centuries, became a market for British machine-made cloth, and its own hand-weavers were devastated. Political power helped. The East India Company ruled much of the subcontinent and was in no hurry to protect Indian weavers from Lancashire competition.

The machines that did all this were, at first, powered by water. That is why the earliest mills sat in steep Pennine valleys beside fast-flowing streams, often in places that seem oddly remote today. To build cities of mills you needed a source of power that could go anywhere. Fortunately, a man in Glasgow had been worrying about exactly that.

## Iron and Fire

Before we get to steam, we need iron, because without cheap iron there are no engines, no rails and no bridges.

For thousands of years iron had been smelted with charcoal, made by slowly burning wood. Charcoal is clean and burns hot, but it devours forests, and by the late seventeenth century English ironmasters were running short of trees. The obvious alternative was coal, but raw coal contains sulphur and other impurities that make iron brittle and useless.

The man who solved the problem was Abraham Darby, a Quaker ironmaster who in 1708 leased an old furnace at Coalbrookdale, in the Severn Gorge, the very valley Loutherbourg would later paint. In 1709 Darby succeeded in smelting iron using **coke**: coal that has been baked to drive off its impurities, in much the same way that wood is turned into charcoal. Shropshire's local coal happened to be low in sulphur, which helped. At first coke-smelted iron was used mostly for cast-iron pots and pans, a business in which Darby did very nicely. Over the following decades his son and grandson improved the process, and coke slowly replaced charcoal across Britain.

The Darbys were Quakers, part of a remarkable network of Quaker families (Lloyds and Barclays in banking, Frys and Cadburys in chocolate, Peases in railways) who were barred by law from universities and public office, and so poured their energies into business. They were known for honesty and for trusting each other with credit, which in an age without limited liability was worth a great deal.

### The bridge that advertised a valley

In 1779 Abraham Darby III cast the parts for a bridge across the Severn, to a design by the Shrewsbury architect Thomas Farnolls Pritchard. It was the first major bridge in the world made of cast iron. Its builders, having no precedent to follow, assembled it using joints borrowed from carpentry, dovetails and mortise-and-tenons, as though the iron were timber. It used about 380 tonnes of iron, opened to traffic on New Year's Day 1781, and spans thirty metres. It is still there, and the town that grew up at its end is called, simply, Ironbridge.

![The Iron Bridge over the Severn, painted by William Williams in 1780: the new cast-iron arch spanning the gorge, with boats on the river below.](/assets/essays/smoke-and-steam/images/ironbridge.jpg)
*William Williams, The Iron Bridge (1780). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The bridge was useful, but it was also an advertisement. Darby commissioned paintings and engravings of it and sold prints across Europe, and tourists came in droves. The message was unmistakable: here is what iron can do. The Ironbridge Gorge became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986, and anyone who has stood on the bridge on a misty morning, looking down at the brown river, can feel the strangeness of the spot: a quiet, wooded Shropshire valley that was once the most industrial place on Earth.

In the 1780s Henry Cort patented the **puddling** and rolling processes, which turned brittle pig iron into tougher wrought iron in quantities that had never been possible. A puddler's job, stirring molten iron in a reverberatory furnace with a long iron bar until it came together into a glowing ball, was among the hottest and most exhausting work of the age, and few puddlers lived long. British iron production soared, from tens of thousands of tonnes a year in the mid-eighteenth century to millions by the 1850s.

### The man who was mad about iron

One ironmaster deserves a special mention. John "Iron-Mad" Wilkinson was obsessed with the stuff. He built iron boats (people said they would sink; they didn't), iron pulpits, an iron office and, inevitably, an iron coffin, in which he was eventually buried in 1808 after several changes of burial site. More usefully, in 1774 he patented a machine for boring cannon barrels with unprecedented accuracy. It would turn out that the same machine could bore the cylinders of a steam engine accurate to roughly the thickness of an old shilling, which was precisely what a certain Scotsman needed.

## The Engine That Moved the World

The steam engine did not begin with James Watt, and it did not begin as a way of powering factories. It began as a way of getting water out of mines.

As mines went deeper, they flooded. Pumping them out with horses was slow and expensive. In 1712 Thomas Newcomen, an ironmonger from Dartmouth in Devon, built a working "atmospheric engine" near Dudley Castle in the West Midlands. It worked like this: steam filled a large cylinder under a piston; cold water was squirted in; the steam condensed, creating a partial vacuum; and the weight of the atmosphere pushed the piston down, rocking a great wooden beam that worked the pump. Then the cylinder was refilled with steam and the cycle began again, a dozen times a minute or so, with a noise like an asthmatic giant breathing.

Newcomen engines worked, and hundreds were built over the following decades. But they were spectacularly inefficient. Every stroke, the cold water cooled the cylinder down, and the next lot of steam had to heat it all up again. Newcomen engines ate coal. That did not matter much at a coal mine, where fuel was almost free, but it made them hopeless almost anywhere else.

### A walk on Glasgow Green

In the winter of 1763-64, a young instrument-maker working at the University of Glasgow was asked to repair the university's model Newcomen engine. His name was James Watt, and he was a thin, anxious man prone to headaches and gloom, who once wrote that he would rather face a loaded cannon than settle an account. He got the model working, and then could not stop thinking about how much steam it wasted.

The answer came to him, as he later remembered it, during a Sunday walk on Glasgow Green in 1765. Why cool the cylinder at all? Why not condense the steam in a *separate* vessel, connected to the cylinder but kept permanently cold, so that the cylinder itself could stay permanently hot? It sounds obvious once you have heard it. It was one of the most important ideas in the history of technology. Watt's engines would use roughly a quarter of the coal of a Newcomen engine doing the same work.

![James Watt, painted by Carl Frederik von Breda in 1792: the Scottish engineer whose separate condenser made steam power practical.](/assets/essays/smoke-and-steam/images/watt.jpg)
*Carl Frederik von Breda, James Watt (1792), detail. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Having an idea and building an engine are two very different things, and the next decade was miserable for Watt. He patented the separate condenser in 1769, but his first backer, John Roebuck, went bankrupt, and Watt spent years working as a canal surveyor to make ends meet. The cylinders that the craftsmen of the day could make leaked steam all round the piston; one engine had to be packed with everything from cork to horse dung to keep it tight.

Rescue came in the shape of Matthew Boulton, a Birmingham manufacturer of buttons, buckles and what were called "toys" (small metal goods), who ran the enormous Soho Manufactory just outside the city. Boulton had the money, the workshops and the salesmanship that Watt lacked, and in 1775 the two went into partnership. The same year, Parliament extended Watt's patent until 1800. With Wilkinson boring the cylinders, the engines finally worked.

Boulton knew exactly what he was selling. In 1776 James Boswell, the biographer of Samuel Johnson, visited the Soho works and recorded Boulton telling him: "I sell here, Sir, what all the world desires to have, POWER." It may be the best one-line summary of the Industrial Revolution ever spoken.

Here is a scene that captures the partnership. Again, the dialogue is imagined; the facts are not.

> **Watt:** Boulton, the Cornish mine owners will not pay. They say the engines save them coal but they cannot see why they should hand us a share of the savings for twenty-five years.
>
> **Boulton:** Then we shall show them the figures. We will fit a counter to every engine, James, and charge them a third of what they save against an old engine. They will pay, because they will still be better off.
>
> **Watt:** And if the engine breaks?
>
> **Boulton:** Then Murdoch will mend it. Stop worrying, man. You have invented a machine that turns coal into money. All we must do is persuade people to let us.

That is roughly how it worked. Boulton and Watt charged a royalty based on the fuel their engines saved, and they sent their brilliant Scottish engineer William Murdoch to Cornwall to keep the engines running (Murdoch, in his spare time, pioneered gas lighting). Watt also coined the unit of **horsepower**, so that customers could compare his engines with the horses they replaced, and the international unit of power, the **watt**, is named after him.

### From pumping to turning

The early Watt engines, like Newcomen's, simply moved a beam up and down. That was fine for pumping but useless for turning machinery. In 1781 Watt patented the "sun and planet" gear to convert up-and-down motion into rotary motion (the simpler crank was tied up by someone else's patent), and soon afterwards he added the double-acting engine, which pushed the piston both ways, and the centrifugal governor, a pair of whirling balls that automatically regulated the engine's speed. The governor is often cited as one of the earliest examples of automatic feedback control, the ancestor of every thermostat and autopilot.

Now steam could drive spinning mules and power looms. Mills no longer had to huddle beside fast streams in remote valleys. They could go wherever coal could be delivered, which increasingly meant towns, which meant cities, which meant Manchester.

Watt himself, cautious to the end, disliked high-pressure steam, which he considered dangerous (he was not wrong; boilers exploded with grim regularity). He used his patent to discourage rivals, and some historians think this slowed innovation until it expired in 1800. After that, a new generation of engineers took steam to much higher pressures, made engines smaller and more powerful, and put them on wheels.

## Water Roads

Before the railways came the canals, and before the canals, British roads were frankly terrible: muddy tracks that turned to quagmire in winter, on which a horse could drag perhaps a tonne. On water, the same horse could pull a barge carrying thirty tonnes or more.

In 1761 Francis Egerton, the third Duke of Bridgewater, opened a canal to carry coal from his mines at Worsley to Manchester. He had been jilted (his engagement to a famous society beauty collapsed) and threw himself into the project, possibly in consolation. His engineer was James Brindley, a self-taught millwright who could barely write and reputedly solved hard problems by going to bed until he had worked them out. The Bridgewater Canal crossed the River Irwell on an aqueduct, which visitors came to marvel at as boats sailed over their heads. The price of coal in Manchester is often said to have halved.

The success of the Bridgewater set off a canal boom. By the 1830s England had a network of some thousands of kilometres of canals, linking the Mersey to the Trent, the Severn to the Thames, and the coalfields to almost everywhere. Josiah Wedgwood was a keen promoter of the Trent and Mersey Canal, because carrying delicate pottery on bumpy roads caused ruinous breakages, and his new Etruria works in Staffordshire stood right beside it.

The canals' golden age was brief. Within a few decades, a faster, cheaper competitor would take most of their trade. But they mattered enormously for a while, and they set a pattern: private investors, raising money by subscription and getting Acts of Parliament to build infrastructure in hope of profit. The same pattern would build the railways, and blow up spectacularly in the process.

## The Railway Age

Steam engines on wheels were tried long before anyone thought they would amount to much. In 1804 the Cornish engineer Richard Trevithick ran a high-pressure steam locomotive on a tramway at the Penydarren ironworks near Merthyr Tydfil in South Wales, hauling ten tonnes of iron and seventy men for nearly sixteen kilometres. He did it to win a bet. The locomotive was too heavy for the cast-iron rails, which kept breaking, and Trevithick, a brilliant man with no talent at all for business, moved on to other schemes and died poor.

The next generation of engineers came from the coal pits of the north-east of England, where horse-drawn wagonways had long carried coal to the rivers. The most important of them was George Stephenson, the son of a colliery fireman, who taught himself to read as a teenager and became the chief engineer of the Stockton and Darlington Railway. It opened on 27 September 1825, with Stephenson's engine *Locomotion No. 1* pulling a train of wagons loaded with coal, flour and passengers. It was the first public railway to use steam locomotives, although for years most of its traffic was still hauled by horses.

### The Rainhill Trials

The real start of the Railway Age was the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, linking the great port to the great cotton town. Its directors could not decide whether to use locomotives or stationary engines hauling trains with ropes, so in October 1829 they held a competition at Rainhill, near Liverpool, with a prize of £500 for the best locomotive. Crowds of thousands came to watch.

There were five entrants. One, the *Cycloped*, was powered by a horse walking on a treadmill, which was disqualified, not least when the horse fell through the floor. Another, the *Novelty*, was light and fast and the crowd's favourite, but kept breaking down. The *Sans Pareil* cracked a cylinder. The winner was *Rocket*, built by George Stephenson's son Robert, which combined a multi-tube boiler with a blast pipe that used exhaust steam to draw the fire. It reached speeds of around 48 kilometres an hour, faster than any human being had travelled on land other than on a galloping horse.

The Liverpool and Manchester opened on 15 September 1830 with immense ceremony, the Prime Minister, the Duke of Wellington, riding in a special carriage. It also produced the world's first widely reported railway fatality. During a stop for water, William Huskisson, MP for Liverpool and a great champion of the railway, stepped down onto the track to speak to the Duke, failed to get out of the way of the approaching *Rocket*, and had his leg crushed. He died that evening. The railway carried on regardless, and within months it was carrying hundreds of thousands of passengers a year.

*Coloured view on the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, plates by S. G. Hughes and H. Pyall after Thomas Talbot Bury (1831). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

### Mania

What followed was a frenzy. In the mid-1840s, during what was called the **Railway Mania**, Parliament authorised thousands of kilometres of new lines, and investors from dukes to clergymen to servants poured their savings into railway shares. The hero of the boom was George Hudson, the "Railway King", a draper from York who controlled a vast network of companies, paid dividends out of capital, and was eventually exposed and disgraced. When the bubble burst in 1847, many investors were ruined. But the lines got built. Britain had around 10,000 kilometres of railway by 1850, and more than double that by 1870.

The railways changed everything they touched. They made coal and food cheap in cities far from the pits and the farms. They gave the working classes the seaside holiday (Blackpool, Scarborough, Brighton). Thomas Cook organised his first excursion, for temperance campaigners from Leicester to Loughborough, in 1841. Fresh milk could reach London from the countryside, and fresh fish could reach the Midlands from Grimsby, giving the world, eventually, fish and chips. Newspapers could be printed in London at night and read in Manchester at breakfast.

They also changed time itself. Before the railways, every town kept its own local time, set by the sun: Bristol was about ten minutes behind London, Plymouth about sixteen. That did not matter when the fastest journey was by coach. It mattered a great deal when a timetable had to work across a network. In 1840 the Great Western Railway adopted London time across its system, and by the late 1840s most British railways had followed. "Railway time", which became Greenwich Mean Time, was only made the legal standard across Great Britain in 1880. For a while some town-hall clocks had two minute hands, one for local time and one for the trains.

### The little giant

No figure embodies the swagger of the age better than Isambard Kingdom Brunel, son of a French émigré engineer, who stood about five foot four in his stovepipe hat and smoked forty cigars a day. Brunel built the Great Western Railway from London to Bristol, which he wanted to be the finest railway in the world, laid it with a broad gauge of just over two metres (most other lines used George Stephenson's 1,435 millimetres, inherited from colliery wagonways), and lost the resulting "gauge war" when Parliament standardised on Stephenson's narrower track in 1846. He designed the Clifton Suspension Bridge (finished only after his death), dug the Box Tunnel and three revolutionary steamships: the *Great Western*, the iron-hulled, screw-driven *Great Britain*, and the colossal *Great Eastern*.

![Isambard Kingdom Brunel standing before the launching chains of the Great Eastern, photographed by Robert Howlett in 1857.](/assets/essays/smoke-and-steam/images/brunel.jpg)
*Robert Howlett, Isambard Kingdom Brunel and the launching chains of the Great Eastern (1857), cropped. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The *Great Eastern* was so large that it had to be launched sideways into the Thames, and the launch, which began in November 1857, stuck, and took until January 1858 to complete. The photograph above, by Robert Howlett, was taken during that ordeal: Brunel, hands in pockets, cigar in mouth, trousers muddy, stands in front of the chains, looking like a man who has seen everything and fears nothing. In fact he was exhausted and ill. He suffered a stroke on board the ship shortly before her maiden voyage in 1859 and died ten days later, aged fifty-three. In the 2002 BBC poll of the greatest Britons, he came second, behind Winston Churchill.

## Cottonopolis

Now let us go to the city that the machines built.

In the early 1770s Manchester was a busy market town of perhaps 25,000 people. By 1851 it had over 300,000, and together with neighbouring Salford it was the centre of the greatest concentration of industry the world had ever seen. People called it **Cottonopolis**. Visitors came to stare at it the way they had once stared at Coalbrookdale, and many of them recorded the same mixture of awe and horror.

![Manchester from Kersal Moor, painted by William Wyld in the 1850s: a pastoral foreground of figures and goats, with the smoking chimneys of Cottonopolis spread across the horizon.](/assets/essays/smoke-and-steam/images/manchester.jpg)
*William Wyld, Manchester from Kersal Moor (1852), Royal Collection. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons (Google Art Project).*

William Wyld's painting, commissioned for Queen Victoria, shows the city from the hills to the north. In the foreground, figures relax on the grass with a few goats. In the distance, the city is a forest of chimneys under a brown sky. It is a polite picture of something that was not polite at all.

The French writer Alexis de Tocqueville visited Manchester in 1835 and described it in terms that still sting: from this foul drain, he wrote, the greatest stream of human industry flows out to fertilise the whole world; here humanity attains its most complete development and its most brutish; here civilisation works its miracles, and civilised man is turned back almost into a savage. Ten years later a young German, Friedrich Engels, sent to Manchester to work in his father's cotton business, Ermen & Engels, published *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845). He walked the slums along the River Irk and described courts where dozens of families shared a single privy, cellars that flooded with sewage, and a river so filthy it gave off bubbles of gas. Engels later co-wrote *The Communist Manifesto* with Karl Marx, funded by the profits of the family mill. The Industrial Revolution produced, among its many by-products, the theory of its own overthrow.

### The price of crowding

The new industrial towns grew far faster than anyone could build drains, water supplies or decent housing for them. Builders threw up rows of **back-to-back** houses, sharing a rear wall so that they had no back windows and no through draught. Water came from standpipes or polluted wells. Sewage went into cesspits, streets and rivers.

The results showed up in the death rates. In 1842 Edwin Chadwick, a civil servant and reformer, published his *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain*. One of its most quoted findings compared the average age at death of different classes. In Manchester, it was 38 for the gentry and professional classes, but just 17 for labourers and their families; in Liverpool, 35 and 15. Those averages were dragged down by the appalling number of babies and small children who died, rather than meaning that adults routinely died in their teens, but they were damning all the same. A child born into a working family in a big industrial town had a far worse chance of reaching adulthood than one born in the countryside.

Then came cholera, a new and terrifying disease from Asia that reached Britain in 1831-32 and returned in 1848-49 and 1853-54, killing tens of thousands in each epidemic. Most doctors believed it was caused by "miasma", bad air. In 1854 a London doctor called John Snow mapped the cases in an outbreak in Soho and showed that they clustered around a single water pump in Broad Street. He persuaded the authorities to remove the pump handle. It was one of the founding moments of epidemiology, although it took years for Snow's waterborne theory to be fully accepted.

What finally forced action in London was not death but a smell. In the hot summer of 1858, the Thames, which received the sewage of over two million people, stank so badly that Parliament, sitting on its banks, could barely work. They hung curtains soaked in chloride of lime over the windows. The "Great Stink" did what years of reports had failed to do, and Parliament voted the money for Joseph Bazalgette's great system of intercepting sewers, which still serves London today. In Victorian Britain, reform often came when the problem reached the noses of the people in charge.

### Children of the mills

The early factories relied heavily on child labour. Children had always worked, on farms and in cottage industries, but the factory was something new: long hours, the relentless pace of the machine, strict discipline and real danger. Children were employed as **piecers**, mending broken threads on spinning mules, and **scavengers**, crawling under the moving machinery to clear away fluff. Parish workhouses sent orphans and pauper children to distant mills as "apprentices", where they could be worked twelve hours or more a day.

![Addie Card, aged twelve, a spinner in a cotton mill in North Pownal, Vermont, photographed by Lewis Hine in 1910. Hine's campaign photographs show American mills, but the scenes would have been familiar in Lancashire decades earlier.](/assets/essays/smoke-and-steam/images/spinner.jpg)
*Lewis Hine, Addie Card, 12 years, spinner in North Pownal Cotton Mill, Vermont (1910). Metropolitan Museum of Art, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Photography came too late to record the worst years of British factory childhood, so the image above is from the United States, where the American photographer Lewis Hine documented child labour in the early twentieth century. Addie Card was twelve, or said she was; the girls at the mill told Hine she was ten. Britain's own record comes in words, from the parliamentary inquiries of the 1830s, where former mill children described being beaten to keep them awake, falling asleep at their meals, and growing up with deformed legs from standing all day.

Reform was slow, opposed by mill owners who said it would ruin them and by many parents who depended on their children's wages. The 1802 Health and Morals of Apprentices Act was largely ignored. The real turning point was the **Factory Act of 1833**, which banned the employment of children under nine in textile mills, limited children aged nine to thirteen to nine hours a day, and, crucially, appointed four paid inspectors to enforce it. Four inspectors for the whole country was absurdly few, but it established the principle that the state could and would regulate the workplace. The Mines Act of 1842, passed after an inquiry whose illustrated report showed children hauling coal carts on all fours, banned women, girls and boys under ten from working underground. The Ten Hours Act of 1847 limited the working day for women and young people in textile mills. Each step was a fight.

## Breaking the Machines

Not everyone accepted the new world quietly.

In 1811, in the hosiery villages of Nottinghamshire, groups of masked men began breaking into workshops at night and smashing knitting frames. The trouble spread to the woollen districts of Yorkshire and the cotton towns of Lancashire. The machine-breakers claimed to follow a leader called **General Ned Ludd** or **King Ludd**, who supposedly lived in Sherwood Forest, like Robin Hood. He almost certainly never existed. His followers became known as **Luddites**.

Today "Luddite" means someone who hates new technology, which is not quite fair to the original Luddites. Many of them were skilled workers who were not against machines as such, but against particular employers using them to cut wages, flood the trade with cheap untrained labour and produce shoddy goods. They were, in a sense, trying to enforce old customs about how a trade should be run, at a time when wartime trade blockades had already plunged the textile districts into hardship. Breaking machines was a form of collective bargaining in an age when trade unions were illegal under the Combination Acts.

The government responded with force. It made frame-breaking a capital offence in 1812, despite a passionate maiden speech in the House of Lords against the bill by the young Lord Byron. It is often said that more soldiers were deployed against the Luddites than Wellington had under his command in the Peninsular War at the time; whatever the exact figure, the numbers were very large. In April 1812 Luddites attacked William Cartwright's mill at Rawfolds in Yorkshire and were driven off by gunfire, with two attackers killed; weeks later, a mill owner called William Horsfall was shot dead. At York in January 1813, more than a dozen men were hanged. The movement faded by 1816.

### Peterloo

The anger did not go away; it turned political. Industrial cities such as Manchester had no MPs of their own, while some ancient "rotten boroughs" with a handful of voters sent two each. Only a small minority of men could vote at all.

On 16 August 1819 a crowd of perhaps 60,000 people, many in their Sunday best, carrying banners demanding reform and universal suffrage, gathered on St Peter's Field in Manchester to hear the radical orator Henry "Orator" Hunt. The local magistrates panicked and ordered the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry, a part-time cavalry force made up largely of local businessmen and shopkeepers, to arrest Hunt. The yeomanry rode into the crowd with sabres drawn, and regular hussars followed. Around eighteen people were killed and hundreds injured, including many women.

![The Massacre of Peterloo, a satirical print by George Cruikshank published in 1819, showing the yeomanry cutting down unarmed men, women and children on St Peter's Field.](/assets/essays/smoke-and-steam/images/peterloo.jpg)
*George Cruikshank, The Massacre of Peterloo (1819). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

A local newspaper dubbed it **Peterloo**, a bitter pun on the Battle of Waterloo, fought just four years earlier, in which many of the same regular soldiers had been heroes. The government's response was to crack down further, with the repressive "Six Acts". But Peterloo became a rallying cry, and it helped inspire the founding of the *Manchester Guardian* in 1821. The Great Reform Act of 1832 finally gave the industrial cities seats in Parliament, though it extended the vote only to about one adult man in five in England and Wales.

The disappointed working-class reformers regrouped as the **Chartists**, named after the People's Charter of 1838. Its six demands were: a vote for every man over 21; a secret ballot; no property qualification for MPs; payment for MPs; equal-sized constituencies; and annual elections. Parliament rejected Chartist petitions with millions of signatures in 1839, 1842 and 1848, and the movement faded after a great rally on Kennington Common in April 1848 failed to start the revolution that its enemies feared and some of its supporters hoped for. Yet all of the Charter's demands except annual parliaments eventually became law. The industrial working class had been born, and it had learnt to organise.

## Were People Better Off?

This brings us to one of the longest-running arguments in economic history, known as the **standard of living debate**. Did the Industrial Revolution make ordinary people better or worse off?

The pessimists, from Engels to the great historian E. P. Thompson, whose *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963) set out to rescue its subjects from what he called "the enormous condescension of posterity", point to the slums, the disease, the child labour, the destruction of craft skills and the harsh discipline of the factory clock. The optimists point to rising real wages, cheaper clothes, cheaper food and, eventually, longer lives.

The best modern estimates suggest both sides are partly right, but at different times. Work by Charles Feinstein and Robert Allen suggests that the real wages of ordinary workers barely rose between about 1780 and the 1830s, even as output per worker grew substantially. The gains went to profits, rents and investment. Allen calls this the **"Engels' pause"**, after the man who complained most eloquently about it. Only from around the 1840s did wages start to rise steadily, and they kept rising for the rest of the century. Average heights, a good measure of childhood nutrition, actually fell for some groups born in the early nineteenth century. Life expectancy in the big industrial towns lagged far behind the countryside until sanitary reform began to bite in the later Victorian period.

So the honest answer is: in the long run, yes, overwhelmingly. Your life is longer, healthier and richer than that of any eighteenth-century labourer because of what began in those decades. But the people who lived through the first two or three generations of the revolution, especially those displaced from the old trades, paid a terrible price for benefits that mostly went to their grandchildren. Economists have a dry phrase for this: "transition costs". It sounds a great deal more comfortable than it was.

### A new kind of time

One of the subtlest changes was to time itself. On a farm, or at a cottage loom, people worked by the task and the season. They worked furiously at harvest and lazily in winter; many weavers famously took Monday off, a habit known as "Saint Monday", and made up the time later in the week. The factory ran by the clock. The bell rang, the gates shut, and late arrivals were fined. E. P. Thompson wrote a famous essay in 1967 about how industrial capitalism taught people "time-discipline", and the battle over it was fought in every mill.

The potter Josiah Wedgwood, who pioneered the division of labour in his works at Etruria, introduced clocking-in, written rules, and fines for drinking and bad workmanship. He wrote that he wanted to "make such machines of the Men as cannot err". He also produced the famous anti-slavery medallion of a kneeling enslaved man asking "Am I not a man and a brother?", and paid relatively good wages. The industrialists were complicated people.

## The Crystal Palace

By the middle of the nineteenth century, Britain was the richest and most powerful industrial nation in the world, and it decided to show off.

The **Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations** opened in Hyde Park on 1 May 1851. Its champion was Queen Victoria's husband, Prince Albert, and its building was designed by Joseph Paxton, a gardener who had made his name building giant greenhouses for the Duke of Devonshire. The Crystal Palace, as *Punch* magazine christened it, was a vast prefabricated structure of iron and glass, more than 560 metres long and tall enough to enclose several of the park's full-grown elm trees. It was built in a matter of months from standardised parts: in itself an advertisement for industrial production.

![The transept of the Crystal Palace during the Great Exhibition of 1851, from a lithograph after J. McNeven: iron, glass, flags, statues and full-grown trees under one roof.](/assets/essays/smoke-and-steam/images/crystalpalace.jpg)
*J. McNeven, The Transept from the Grand Entrance, Souvenir of the Great Exhibition (1851). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Inside were around 100,000 exhibits from Britain, its empire and dozens of other countries: steam hammers and locomotives, a vast lump of coal, the Koh-i-Noor diamond, Colt revolvers from America, a Sheffield sportsman's knife with some seventy-five blades, and a "tremendous alarum" bed that tipped its occupant out in the morning. There were roughly six million visits in five and a half months, at a time when Britain's population was about 21 million. Cheap "shilling days" and railway excursions brought working people from all over the country. Visitors could also use the first public flushing toilets in a major building, designed by the plumber George Jennings, for a charge of one penny, which is often said to be the origin of the British euphemism "to spend a penny".

The exhibition made a profit of around £186,000, which was used to buy land in South Kensington where the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Science Museum and the Natural History Museum now stand. You can still go and look at a good deal of the Industrial Revolution there, including *Rocket* in the Science Museum.

Yet the exhibition also carried a warning that few British visitors noticed. The Americans showed off mass-produced firearms with interchangeable parts, and reapers that out-performed British ones. Within a few decades, the pupils would overtake the teacher.

## The Revolution Goes Abroad

Britain tried hard to keep its secrets. It was illegal for skilled artisans to emigrate until 1825, and the export of many kinds of machinery was banned until 1843. None of this worked very well.

In 1789 a young apprentice from Derbyshire called Samuel Slater, who had worked for Jedediah Strutt, Arkwright's partner, sailed to America disguised as a farm labourer, carrying the design of Arkwright's machines in his head. In Pawtucket, Rhode Island, he helped build the first successful water-powered cotton-spinning mill in the United States. Americans called him "the Father of the American Industrial Revolution". In Derbyshire, he was remembered as "Slater the Traitor".

Belgium was the first country on the continent to industrialise, helped by an English mechanic, William Cockerill, and his son John, whose ironworks and engineering shops at Seraing became among the largest in Europe. France, the German states and the northern United States followed, each in its own way. Germany, unified in 1871, built a formidable industrial economy based on coal, steel, chemicals and a superb system of technical education. Japan, after the Meiji Restoration of 1868, deliberately imported Western technology and experts, opening the state-run Tomioka silk mill in 1872 with French machinery and advisers.

From the 1850s onwards, a **Second Industrial Revolution** began, built on cheap steel (Henry Bessemer's converter of 1856), chemicals, electricity, the internal combustion engine and the telephone. Science and industry became formally linked in research laboratories rather than in the workshops of self-taught tinkerers. Britain, which had led the first revolution, was slow in the second. By the 1890s both the United States and Germany were producing more steel than Britain. The age of British industrial supremacy, so confidently celebrated in 1851, lasted less than a century.

The story is still being written. In the late twentieth century, industrialisation swept through South Korea and Taiwan, and then China, where hundreds of millions of people moved from farms to factory cities in a single generation, repeating the Lancashire story on a scale its pioneers could never have imagined, with many of the same features: astonishing growth, smoky skies, crowded dormitories and exhausting hours, followed in time by rising wages and new demands for regulation.

## Living Inside the Revolution

So what is left of all this? Almost everything, it turns out, though it is so familiar that we no longer see it.

**Growth itself.** The most profound legacy is the expectation that the economy should grow, and the political crises that follow when it doesn't. Before 1800 nobody expected their children to be much richer than they were. Now governments rise and fall on growth rates of one or two per cent.

**Where we live.** In 1851 the census showed, for the first time, that more people in England and Wales lived in towns than in the countryside. Britain was the first predominantly urban society in history. Today more than half of all humanity lives in towns and cities, and the share is still rising.

**How we work.** The clock, the shift, the commute, the separation of home from workplace, the weekend (the Saturday half-holiday was a Victorian industrial invention), the factory inspector, health and safety rules, the trade union, the minimum age for work: all of these were forged in the conflicts of the Industrial Revolution.

**Our politics.** The left-right divide between labour and capital, the workers' parties, the welfare state, socialism and communism, free-market liberalism: the political vocabulary of the past two centuries was largely written in response to industrialisation. Marx and Engels are unthinkable without Manchester.

**The air.** When James Watt took his walk on Glasgow Green, the concentration of carbon dioxide in the Earth's atmosphere was about 280 parts per million. It is now over 420. Almost all of that increase is the result of burning the fossil fuels that the Industrial Revolution taught us to use. British coal output rose from around fifteen million tonnes a year in 1800 to some 287 million tonnes in 1913, and the world followed. Climate change is, in the most literal sense, the long tail of the Industrial Revolution. So is the hope of solving it, because the habit of systematic invention that the revolution created is the same habit now building solar panels, batteries and wind turbines.

**The fear of machines.** Every time a new technology threatens jobs, from the power loom to the computer to artificial intelligence, someone reaches for the word "Luddite". The original Luddites lost, and in the long run the economy found new work for their grandchildren. But they were not simply wrong. They saw, correctly, that the gains from new machines do not automatically flow to the people whose work the machines replace, and that how the transition is managed, who bears its costs and who shares its benefits, is a political choice. That argument is worth remembering whenever someone says that technological change is simply a force of nature.

*J. M. W. Turner, Rain, Steam and Speed: The Great Western Railway (1844), National Gallery, London. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

## A Hare on the Line

In 1844 J. M. W. Turner, then nearly seventy, exhibited a painting called *Rain, Steam and Speed: The Great Western Railway*. It shows a locomotive charging towards the viewer across Brunel's bridge over the Thames at Maidenhead, its dark funnel emerging from a blur of rain and golden light. Down on the river, a small boat drifts along. In a field, a farmer ploughs with horses. And on the track itself, just in front of the train, almost invisible unless you know where to look, a hare is running for its life.

Turner did not tell anyone what he meant by the hare. Perhaps it was a joke: nature's fastest creature, about to be overtaken. Perhaps it was a lament for a world that was about to be run down. Perhaps it was simply a hare. But it is hard to look at it without thinking of all the people in this story who found themselves running in front of something they could not stop: the hand-loom weavers, the children at the frames, the Luddites at Rawfolds, the crowd at St Peter's Field, and William Huskisson on the line at Parkside.

And yet the train in Turner's painting is also beautiful, as the furnaces in Loutherbourg's painting are beautiful. That is the double vision of the Industrial Revolution, and we have never quite resolved it. It brought the longest, healthiest, richest lives in human history, and it brought the slums of Manchester, the cotton fields of Mississippi and a warming planet. It gave us power, exactly as Matthew Boulton promised. What we do with that power has been the main question of modern history ever since.

The next time you flick a switch, check the clock, board a train or pull on a cotton T-shirt, spare a thought for the orange sky over the Severn Gorge. We are all still living under it.
