## Why Keep Chickens?

There is a particular sound that a hen makes when she has laid an egg. It is not subtle. It starts as a series of indignant clucks and builds into a proud, repetitive announcement that can be heard three gardens away: *buk-buk-buk-BA-GAWK*. Scientists call it the "egg song". Everybody else calls it showing off. The first time you hear it from your own garden, and walk down the path to find a warm brown egg sitting in the straw, you will understand why so many otherwise sensible people have turned a corner of their lawn over to a small flock of feathered dinosaurs.

Keeping chickens has gone through several revivals. During the Second World War, the British government encouraged households to keep hens in their back gardens, and at one point well over a million domestic poultry keepers were supplying a substantial share of the nation's fresh eggs. The habit faded in the age of the supermarket, then came roaring back in the early twenty-first century, driven by a mixture of nostalgia, concern for animal welfare, a desire to know where food comes from, and the discovery that chickens are among the most entertaining pets you can own. Today, hens live in suburban gardens, on allotments, in school grounds and on the roofs of city restaurants.

The appeal is easy to list. Chickens give you eggs that are fresher than anything you can buy, with yolks that, if the birds are foraging on grass, can be startlingly orange. They eat slugs, snails, insects and weeds. They turn kitchen-garden waste into some of the best compost activator there is. They are cheap to feed, relatively easy to care for and, unlike a dog, do not need walking. They have distinct personalities, a strict social order, and an endless capacity for minor drama, which makes them better than most television. Children love them. So, in a slightly embarrassed way, do most adults who get to know them.

It is only fair to be honest about the other side, too. Chickens need looking after every single day, including Christmas Day and the morning after a party. They attract rats if you are careless with their food. They are a favourite meal for foxes, which are present in almost every part of Britain, including city centres. They can be noisy, especially if you keep a cockerel. They scratch up lawns and flower beds with the enthusiasm of a small, feathered rotavator. They catch parasites, get ill, stop laying for reasons of their own, and eventually die, sometimes suddenly. And while they can save you money on eggs, very few beginners ever make a profit in any accountant's sense of the word.

So this essay is about fun first and profit second. It is written for the complete beginner, with a British garden in mind, though most of the advice applies wherever you live. We shall go through everything you need to know to get started: the rules, the housing, the space, the breeds (with plenty of pictures), feeding, daily care, health, how many eggs to expect at different times of year, what to do with them, and how to make a little money if you want to. At the end there is a wildcard chapter on the other birds and animals that can, and cannot, share a home with chickens.

First, though, a little planning.

## Before You Begin: Rules, Neighbours and Honest Questions

The most important decisions about chicken keeping happen before you buy anything. A little time spent here saves a great deal of grief later.

### Ask yourself the honest questions

Chickens can live for six to ten years, although commercial hybrid hens often live rather less. They need someone to let them out in the morning, check their food and water, collect the eggs and shut them in at dusk, every day. Who will do this when you are on holiday? A friendly neighbour, paid in eggs, is the traditional answer, and it works surprisingly well. But it is worth arranging before the birds arrive rather than the night before you fly.

You also need to think about what happens when a hen stops laying, falls ill or needs to be put down. Most hobby keepers keep their old hens as pets in retirement, which is perfectly reasonable, but it means that a flock gradually fills with pensioners who eat but do not lay. Decide early whether you are comfortable with that, and whether you are willing to pay a vet or learn how to dispatch a bird humanely if it is suffering. This is the least pleasant part of keeping animals, and it is better faced in advance.

### Check that you are allowed

In most of Britain, there is no general law stopping you from keeping a few hens in your garden. But there are some important exceptions and rules.

First, check your property deeds, lease or tenancy agreement. Some houses, especially on newer estates, have covenants that forbid keeping livestock or poultry, and many rental agreements say no pets. Council-house tenancies vary. Allotment societies often allow hens but have their own rules.

Second, register your birds. In Great Britain, since October 2024, everyone who keeps poultry must register with the Animal and Plant Health Agency (APHA), even if they only have one hen as a pet. Registration is free and is done online. It matters because, during outbreaks of avian influenza, the government uses the register to contact keepers in affected areas and tell them what to do. In Northern Ireland, keepers register with the Department of Agriculture, Environment and Rural Affairs. Rules can change, so check the current government guidance before you start.

Third, pay attention to bird flu. In recent winters, avian influenza has spread through wild birds across Britain and Europe, and the government has repeatedly declared prevention zones requiring all poultry, including garden hens, to be kept under cover and away from wild birds for weeks or months at a time. When you design your run, it is worth thinking about how you could cover it with a solid roof or netting if you had to.

Fourth, there is one rule that surprises almost every new keeper. In the UK, it is illegal to feed chickens kitchen scraps, or anything that has passed through a kitchen, even if you are vegetarian or vegan. This dates from the foot-and-mouth outbreak of 2001, which is believed to have started when pigs were fed contaminated waste, and it applies to all farmed animals, including pet hens. Fruit and vegetables taken directly from your garden or allotment, without going through the kitchen, are a different matter. We shall come back to feeding later.

### Talk to the neighbours

Hens are generally quiet, apart from the egg song and the occasional alarm call. Cockerels are another matter. A cockerel crows at dawn, which in June in Britain is before five in the morning, and he does not stop at one crow. There is no law against keeping a cockerel in most places, but a crowing bird can be treated as a statutory noise nuisance if the neighbours complain to the council, and it is a very common source of disputes. You do not need a cockerel to get eggs. Hens lay perfectly well without one; the eggs simply will not be fertile. For most beginners in a residential area, the best advice is simple: no cockerel.

It is still worth telling your neighbours about your plans, offering them the occasional box of eggs, and siting the coop away from their windows and fences. Good relations are much easier to keep than to repair.

### Decide how many birds

Chickens are flock animals, and a single hen will be lonely and stressed. Three is generally regarded as the minimum, and three or four is ideal for a beginner with a normal garden. That number will give a household of two to four people a steady supply of eggs for most of the year, without overwhelming the garden. Beware the condition known among poultry keepers as "chicken maths", in which three hens quickly become five, five become eight, and eight become a small farm. It is entirely real, it affects almost everybody, and the only defence is a coop that is not quite big enough to tempt you.

## The Coop and the Run: Equipment and Facilities

A chicken's home has two parts: the house, or coop, where the birds sleep, lay and shelter, and the run, an enclosed outdoor area where they spend the day. Some keepers let their hens roam freely in the garden during the day, but even free-range hens need a secure house at night and, ideally, a run for days when you are not around.

![Cross-section of a well-designed hen house](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/coop-diagram.png)

### The house

The house does five jobs. It keeps the birds safe from predators at night. It keeps them dry. It gives them somewhere to perch. It gives them somewhere private to lay. And it lets enough air move through to stop ammonia and damp building up, without being draughty.

**Perches.** Chickens sleep on perches, gripping with their toes, and they like to roost as high as is available. Allow about 20 to 30 centimetres of perch per bird, more for large breeds. Perches should be wide and flat or gently rounded, about 4 to 5 centimetres across, with the corners taken off, so the birds can rest their feet flat and cover their toes with their feathers in cold weather. Thin round dowel, of the kind used in budgie cages, is bad for their feet. Place the perches higher than the nest boxes, or the hens will sleep in the nest boxes and fill them with droppings.

**Nest boxes.** Allow one nest box for every three or four hens. They will all want to use the same one anyway, often at the same time, and you will regularly find two hens squeezed into one box while the others sit empty. A box about 30 centimetres square is right for most breeds. Put them in the darkest, most private part of the house, and line them with clean straw, hay or wood shavings.

**Ventilation.** This is the most commonly overlooked feature, and poor ventilation causes more health problems than cold. Chickens produce a great deal of moisture and ammonia, and a sealed house quickly becomes damp and unhealthy. Good houses have vents high up, above the perches, so that warm, stale air can escape without creating draughts on the sleeping birds. Chickens cope well with cold, but they do not cope well with damp and draughts.

**Cleaning.** You will be cleaning the house regularly, so choose a design that makes this easy. Look for a large door or removable roof, a removable floor tray, and removable perches and nest boxes. A droppings board or tray under the perches, where most of the night-time droppings land, makes daily cleaning a matter of minutes.

**Materials.** Traditional houses are wooden, which is warm, sturdy and attractive, but wood has gaps and cracks in which red mite can hide and breed. Plastic houses, of which the best-known are the colourful modern designs that look like miniature space capsules, are easier to clean and much harder for mites to colonise, though they are more expensive and some keepers find them less charming. Recycled plastic "wood" is another option. Whatever you choose, avoid the very cheapest flat-pack wooden houses sold online. They are often made from thin, untreated wood, held together with staples, and their advertised capacity tends to be wildly optimistic. A house that claims to hold six hens will often comfortably hold three.

**The pop hole.** This is the small door through which the birds go in and out. It needs to be secure at night. Many keepers now use an automatic door opener, which opens at dawn and closes at dusk, controlled by a light sensor or a timer. These are one of the best investments a chicken keeper can make. They mean you do not have to be home at dusk to shut the birds in, and foxes, which often hunt at dawn and dusk, find the door shut. You still need to check each evening that everyone is inside.

### The run

The run is where your hens will spend their days, and it needs to be secure, dry and interesting.

**Fox-proofing.** Foxes are the single biggest threat to garden hens. They are intelligent, persistent and can dig, climb and squeeze through surprisingly small gaps. A fox that gets into a run will often kill every bird, not just the one it eats. The traditional "chicken wire", with its hexagonal holes, is designed to keep chickens in, not foxes out; a determined fox can tear it. Use strong welded mesh instead, ideally with holes no larger than about 25 by 13 millimetres, which also keeps out rats. To stop foxes digging under, either bury the mesh about 30 centimetres deep, or lay an "apron" of mesh flat on the ground around the outside of the run, extending 30 to 60 centimetres outwards and pegged down. A fox that tries to dig at the base of the fence hits the apron and gives up. Cover the top, too, with mesh or a roof. Foxes can climb, and a covered run also keeps out wild birds during bird flu outbreaks.

![Red fox](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/red-fox.jpg)

*The red fox: clever, persistent, and found almost everywhere in Britain, including city centres. Design every part of your set-up with this animal in mind.* {{credit:red-fox}}

**Flooring.** A run on bare earth quickly turns into a muddy, smelly bog in a British winter, especially with a small flock in a small space. Grass does not survive long under chickens' feet. Many keepers cover the floor of their run with a deep layer of wood chips or bark chips, which drains well, can be raked, and gives the birds something to scratch in. It needs topping up and eventually replacing. Others use sand. If you can, roof at least part of the run with clear corrugated sheeting, so the birds have somewhere dry to stand and the floor does not become waterlogged.

**Interest.** Chickens are intelligent, curious animals, and a bored chicken can become a bad-tempered one. Give them things to do. A log or tree stump to perch on, a pile of leaves to scratch through, a hanging cabbage to peck at, and a patch of dry soil for dust bathing will all keep them busy.

**Dust baths.** Chickens do not bathe in water. They bathe in dust. A hen will dig a shallow hollow, lie down in it, throw dust over herself with her wings and feet, and then shake it out. It looks alarming the first time you see it, as if your hen has collapsed, but it is one of the most important things she does. Dust bathing helps keep their feathers in good condition and controls parasites such as lice and mites. In a covered corner of the run, provide a shallow box of dry soil, sand, or a mixture of the two. Some keepers add a little wood ash or diatomaceous earth.

![A hen enjoying a dust bath](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/dust-bath.jpg)

*A hen enjoying a dust bath. It looks like a collapse; it is actually a spa treatment.* {{credit:dust-bath}}

### Other equipment

You will need a few other pieces of kit.

**A feeder.** Hanging tube feeders or treadle feeders, which open when a hen stands on a plate, keep food clean and dry and are harder for rats and wild birds to raid. Do not simply scatter feed on the ground or leave it in an open bowl, or you will feed the local wildlife and attract rats.

**A drinker.** Chickens need clean, fresh water at all times. Plastic or galvanised drinkers that hang or stand off the ground stay cleaner. In winter, check every morning that the water has not frozen. Some keepers have two drinkers and swap them over on frosty days.

**Grit.** Chickens have no teeth. They grind their food in a muscular organ called the gizzard, using small stones that they swallow. Free-ranging birds pick up enough grit on their own, but run-kept birds should have a pot of insoluble flint grit available at all times. They also benefit from a separate pot of crushed oyster shell, which is a source of calcium for strong eggshells.

**Bedding.** Dust-extracted wood shavings, chopped straw, hemp or shredded cardboard are all used. Avoid hay for the floor of the house, which can harbour mould spores, although it can be fine in nest boxes.

**Storage.** Keep feed in a metal bin with a tight-fitting lid. Plastic bins can be gnawed through by rats.

**A carrier.** A cat carrier or a sturdy cardboard box with air holes is useful for transporting birds and for isolating a sick or broody hen.

**A notebook.** It sounds old-fashioned, but keeping a simple record of how many eggs you collect each day, when you treated the birds for parasites and when each hen arrived is extremely useful. It tells you quickly when something has changed.

## How Much Space Do Chickens Need?

This is the question every beginner asks, and the honest answer is: more than you think, and more than the manufacturer says.

There are no legal minimum space requirements for garden hens in Britain, but there are well-established guidelines. Commercial free-range egg producers in the UK must give each hen at least 4 square metres of outdoor range. Hobby keepers do not need anything like that much, but they should take note of the direction it points in. Chickens that are crowded become stressed, bored and aggressive, peck at one another, and are more prone to disease.

A good rule of thumb for a small garden flock of standard-sized hens is:

- **In the house:** at least 0.3 square metres of floor space per bird, plus 20 to 30 centimetres of perch each.
- **In the run:** an absolute minimum of 1 square metre per bird, but ideally 2 to 4 square metres or more. The more space, the happier the birds and the slower the ground turns to mud.
- **Free ranging:** if your hens can roam the garden for at least part of the day, the run can be smaller, but they will still need it on days when you are away.

Bantams, the miniature versions of many breeds, need roughly half as much space as large fowl. Large, heavy breeds such as Brahmas need more.

![Space per bird chart](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/space-chart.png)

*Guideline floor area per standard-sized hen. These are comfortable minimums for a garden flock, not targets; more space is always better.*

So a flock of four standard hens needs a house of at least 1.2 square metres of floor space and a run of at least 4 square metres, and preferably 8 to 16. A run 2 metres by 4 metres, with a house inside or attached, makes a good, comfortable home for four birds. It is better to have three happy hens in a generous space than six miserable ones in a cramped one.

The other thing to think about is where you put it all. Choose a level, well-drained spot, with some shade in summer (chickens suffer more from heat than from cold) and some shelter from the prevailing wind. Keep it within easy reach of the house, because you will be walking there every day, often in the rain and in the dark. Keep it away from the neighbours' windows. And, if you can, choose a site where you could move the run, or rotate it between two patches of ground, to give the soil a rest.

## Choosing Your Breeds

There are hundreds of chicken breeds, and choosing between them is one of the great pleasures of the hobby. Breeds vary enormously in size, colour, temperament, hardiness, egg colour and, above all, the number of eggs they lay. Some are prolific layers that will give you an egg almost every day. Others are beautiful, calm and charming but lay only a couple of eggs a week, and stop altogether in winter.

Broadly, there are three kinds of chicken a beginner might choose: **hybrids**, **pure breeds** and **rescued commercial hens**.

### Hybrids

Hybrids are birds bred by crossing two or more pure breeds to produce hens that lay a lot of eggs. They are the backbone of the commercial egg industry, but there are also many hybrids bred specifically for back gardens, in a range of colours and sometimes with fanciful names. Hybrids typically lay 250 to 320 eggs a year in their first laying season, are usually friendly and easy to handle, and are generally vaccinated against common diseases before sale. They are an excellent choice for beginners who want plenty of eggs.

The downside is that their productive lives are often short. Hybrids lay heavily for two or three years, then tail off, and they are more prone to reproductive problems, such as egg peritonitis, because their bodies are working so hard. They also rarely go broody, which is a good thing if you want eggs and a bad thing if you want to hatch chicks.

![ISA Brown hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/isa-brown.jpg)

*The ISA Brown, or one of its many close relatives sold under different names, is the classic brown commercial hybrid: friendly, easy and capable of well over 300 eggs in her first year.* {{credit:isa-brown}}

**ISA Brown and similar brown hybrids.** These ginger-brown hens, sold under many names, are the most common laying hens in the world. They are docile, curious and very easy to tame, and they lay large brown eggs at an astonishing rate. They make wonderful first chickens.

**Black Rock.** A hardy hybrid developed from Rhode Island Red and Plymouth Rock bloodlines, with glossy black feathers shot through with red and green. Black Rocks are known for coping well with cold, wet weather and for laying steadily for longer than many hybrids.

### Pure breeds

Pure breeds, sometimes called heritage or traditional breeds, are the descendants of the farmyard chickens of the past. They breed true, meaning that their chicks look like their parents. They are generally hardier and longer-lived than hybrids, and many are spectacularly beautiful. They usually lay fewer eggs, but over a longer lifetime the total can be respectable. Here are some of the best for beginners.

![Light Sussex hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/light-sussex.jpg)

*The Light Sussex: white body, black neck and tail, and one of the most reliable dual-purpose breeds ever developed.* {{credit:light-sussex}}

**Light Sussex.** An old English breed from the south of England, with a white body, black-striped neck feathers and a black tail. Sussex hens are calm, friendly and hardy, lay 220 to 260 cream or light brown eggs a year, and are good foragers. They are one of the best all-round breeds for a beginner. They come in several other colours, including Buff, Speckled and Coronation.

![Rhode Island Red hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/rhode-island-red.jpg)

*The Rhode Island Red: a deep mahogany American classic, and the ancestor of many modern brown-egg hybrids.* {{credit:rhode-island-red}}

**Rhode Island Red.** An American breed developed in the nineteenth century in, unsurprisingly, Rhode Island. The hens are a rich, deep red-brown and lay 200 to 250 brown eggs a year. They are hardy and productive, though the cockerels can be aggressive and some strains are rather bossy with other breeds.

![White Leghorn hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/leghorn.jpg)

*The Leghorn: slim, alert and flighty, and the champion layer of white eggs.* {{credit:leghorn}}

**Leghorn.** An Italian breed, named after the port of Livorno (once called Leghorn in English). Leghorns are slim, active, flighty birds with large combs, and they lay a great many white eggs, often 250 to 300 a year. They are not the friendliest of birds, are good fliers, and dislike being confined, but if eggs are your priority, they are hard to beat.

![Barred Plymouth Rock hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/plymouth-rock.jpg)

*The Barred Plymouth Rock, with its striking black-and-white "cuckoo" barring.* {{credit:plymouth-rock}}

**Plymouth Rock.** Another American breed, best known in the barred variety, with black and white stripes across every feather. Plymouth Rocks are calm, sturdy and friendly, lay around 200 brown eggs a year, and cope well with cold weather.

![Buff Orpington hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/buff-orpington.jpg)

*The Buff Orpington: large, golden, fluffy, and about as gentle as a chicken can be.* {{credit:buff-orpington}}

**Buff Orpington.** Developed in the 1880s in Orpington, Kent, by William Cook, this is the gentle giant of the garden. Buff Orpingtons are large, golden, fluffy and very docile, which makes them excellent with children. They lay around 150 to 180 light brown eggs a year, and they are enthusiastic mothers that often go broody. They are a breed for people who want a calm pet first and eggs second.

![Silver Laced Wyandotte hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/wyandotte.jpg)

*The Silver Laced Wyandotte: each white feather edged in black, like a living piece of lace.* {{credit:wyandotte}}

**Wyandotte.** An American breed with a rounded, compact shape and a neat, low "rose" comb, which makes it resistant to frostbite. The Silver Laced variety, with each feather outlined in black, is one of the most beautiful chickens there is. Wyandottes are calm and hardy and lay around 180 to 200 eggs a year.

![Black Australorp hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/australorp.jpg)

*The Australorp, whose glossy black feathers shine green in the sunlight. One famously laid 364 eggs in 365 days.* {{credit:australorp}}

**Australorp.** An Australian breed developed from Orpingtons in the early twentieth century, with a focus on egg production. Australorps are black, glossy, gentle and very productive. In the 1920s an Australorp hen was recorded laying 364 eggs in 365 days, a figure that is still quoted with awe at poultry shows. In real life, expect 220 to 250 light brown eggs a year.

![Marans hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/marans.jpg)

*The Marans, from France, famous for its dark chocolate-brown eggs.* {{credit:marans}}

**Marans.** A French breed, from the town of Marans near La Rochelle, famous for laying very dark, chocolate-brown eggs. The Cuckoo Marans, with grey barred feathers, is common in Britain, and the Black Copper Marans lays the darkest eggs of all. Marans lay 150 to 200 eggs a year. A box of their eggs mixed with white and blue ones looks wonderful.

![Two blue-green Araucana eggs in a hand](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/araucana.jpg)

*The Araucana's claim to fame: eggs with blue or blue-green shells, coloured right the way through.* {{credit:araucana}}

**Araucana.** A breed descended from chickens in Chile, best known for laying eggs with blue or blue-green shells, a colour that runs right through the shell rather than sitting on the surface. Many British Araucanas have small crests. They lay around 150 to 200 eggs a year and are lively, independent birds.

![Welsummer hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/welsummer.jpg)

*The Welsummer, from the Netherlands, lays dark terracotta eggs with darker speckles.* {{credit:welsummer}}

**Welsummer.** A Dutch breed from the village of Welsum, famous for laying beautiful dark terracotta eggs with darker freckles. The cockerel is said to be the model for the rooster on the Kellogg's Corn Flakes box. Welsummers are friendly, active foragers that lay 160 to 200 eggs a year.

![Brahma hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/brahma.jpg)

*The Brahma: enormous, feather-footed and calm. Not a bird for small runs or muddy ground.* {{credit:brahma}}

**Brahma.** A huge breed with feathered feet, developed in America from birds imported from Asia. Brahmas are calm, gentle giants (the largest cockerels can stand around 75 centimetres tall), and lay around 150 eggs a year, often continuing through winter better than other breeds. They need more space, low perches and dry ground, because their feathered feet get muddy and caked.

### Bantams and ornamental breeds

Bantams are miniature chickens, typically a quarter to a fifth of the size of large fowl. Many large breeds have bantam versions, and there are also "true bantams", which have no large equivalent. Bantams need less space and less food, are easier for children to handle, and lay small eggs, often 100 to 150 a year. Two bantam eggs roughly equal one large egg.

![Pekin bantam](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/pekin.jpg)

*The Pekin bantam: a small, round, feather-footed ball of fluff, and a superb chicken for families.* {{credit:pekin}}

**Pekin.** A true bantam, small and round, with feathered feet and a waddling walk. Pekins are among the friendliest and calmest of all chickens and are ideal for families with young children. They lay small cream eggs and go broody often.

![Silkie hen](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/silkie.jpg)

*The Silkie, whose feathers lack the tiny hooks that hold ordinary feathers together, giving it a coat like fur.* {{credit:silkie}}

**Silkie.** Perhaps the strangest chicken of all. Silkies have fluffy, fur-like feathers that lack the tiny barbs that hold normal feathers together, black skin, five toes rather than four, and feathered feet. They cannot fly and cannot cope with getting soaked, so they need a dry run. They lay only around 100 small cream eggs a year, but they are famously broody, and many keepers keep a Silkie or two purely to hatch other hens' eggs.

### Rescue hens

Every year, millions of commercial laying hens in Britain are sent to slaughter at around 72 weeks old, when their egg production starts to fall below the level that is commercially profitable. Charities such as the British Hen Welfare Trust rehome many thousands of these hens to private homes each year, for a small donation.

Rescue hens can be a wonderful choice. They are hybrids, so they will still lay well, often four or five eggs a week, and the experience of watching a scruffy, half-bald hen grow back her feathers and discover grass, sunshine and dust baths for the first time is deeply rewarding. They do need a little extra care at first, as they may be nervous, underweight, short of feathers and unused to perching or being outdoors. They are also less long-lived than pure breeds. But many keepers start with rescue hens and never look back.

## Getting Your Birds

Once your house and run are ready, it is time to buy your birds. Most beginners start with hens at "point of lay", which means they are around 16 to 22 weeks old and will start laying within a few weeks. Chicks are cheaper and great fun, but they need a heat lamp, a brooder and several months of care before they lay, and when bought "straight run" (unsexed), around half of them will turn out to be cockerels, which you will then need to find homes for or dispatch.

Buy from a reputable breeder or poultry supplier, ideally one recommended by other keepers or a local poultry club. Avoid buying birds from the back of a van or from markets where you cannot see where they have come from. Ask whether the birds have been vaccinated, particularly against Marek's disease, a common viral disease of chickens. Look at the birds before you buy. They should be alert, with bright eyes, clean nostrils, a clean vent (the opening under the tail), smooth legs, and no signs of lice or mites. Avoid any bird that is hunched, fluffed up, sneezing or has a runny nose.

Expect to pay roughly £15 to £30 for a point-of-lay hybrid, and £20 to £50 or more for a pure breed, depending on the breed and quality. Rescue hens are usually rehomed for a small donation.

When you get them home, put them straight into the house and keep them shut in, or confined to the run, for a day or two so they learn that this is home and where to roost. Most new hens will take a few days to settle in, and it is normal for them not to lay for a week or two after a move.

If you are adding new birds to an existing flock, be patient. Chickens have a strict social hierarchy, the famous "pecking order", and newcomers will be bullied while it is re-established. Ideally, keep new birds in quarantine, in a separate enclosure where they can be seen but not reached, for a couple of weeks, both to check they are healthy and to let the flock get used to them. Then introduce them at night, when everyone is sleepy, and watch carefully for the first few days. Some pecking is normal; bloodshed is not.

## What to Feed Them

Feeding chickens is simpler than it looks, as long as you remember one thing: a hen that lays an egg almost every day is doing something biologically extraordinary, and she needs a balanced diet to do it.

### Layers pellets or mash

The basis of every laying hen's diet should be a commercial "layers" feed, available as pellets or as mash (a coarse powder). Layers feed is formulated to contain everything a laying hen needs: around 16 to 18 per cent protein, the right balance of vitamins and minerals, and extra calcium for eggshells. Pellets are less wasteful and less messy than mash, and most keepers use them. Make the feed available all day, in a covered feeder, and let the birds help themselves. Chickens are good at regulating how much they eat.

A standard-sized hen eats roughly 110 to 130 grams of layers feed a day, a little more in cold weather and a little less in summer, when there is more to forage. Bantams eat about half that. A 20-kilogram sack of layers pellets will therefore feed four large hens for around five to six weeks.

If you are raising chicks, they need "chick crumbs", a finer, higher-protein feed, for their first six to eight weeks, then "growers" pellets until they are close to laying. Do not give layers feed to young birds, because the high calcium content can damage their developing kidneys.

### Grit and calcium

As mentioned earlier, chickens need insoluble grit to grind their food, and benefit from a supply of crushed oyster shell for extra calcium. Keep both in separate pots, available at all times. The hens will take what they need.

### Treats

Chickens love treats, and treats are the quickest way to tame them. A handful of mixed corn (whole wheat, cracked maize and sometimes barley) scattered in the run in the afternoon will be greeted with rapture, gives the birds something to scratch for, and helps them go to bed with full crops on cold nights. But mixed corn is the chicken equivalent of crisps: it is low in protein and high in energy, and too much makes hens fat and reduces laying. As a rule, treats should make up no more than about a tenth of a hen's diet.

Other popular treats include dried mealworms (very high in protein, and especially useful during the moult), fresh greens from the garden, and fruit and vegetables grown on your allotment. Remember the British rule: nothing that has passed through a kitchen. Defra's guidance is clear that kitchen scraps, including leftover vegetables and peelings that have been in your kitchen, must not be fed to chickens, however healthy they look.

Some things should never be given to chickens in any case: avocado skin and stones, raw or dried beans, chocolate, salty food, mouldy food, and the green parts of potatoes and tomatoes.

### Water

A laying hen can drink a quarter of a litre to half a litre of water a day, and much more in hot weather. An egg is about three-quarters water, and a hen that runs short of water will stop laying very quickly. Clean the drinker regularly, refill it every day, and check it twice a day in hot or freezing weather. A spoonful of apple cider vinegar in the water is a popular folk remedy among keepers; it does no harm in small amounts, though the scientific evidence for its benefits is thin.

## Daily, Weekly and Seasonal Care

Chickens are not hard work, but they do need a routine. Here is a typical schedule for a small garden flock.

### Every day

- **Morning:** Let the birds out (or check the automatic door has opened). Check food and water. Look at every bird: are they all bright, active and eating?
- **During the day:** Collect the eggs at least once, ideally twice. Eggs left in the nest get dirty, get broken, and encourage hens to start eating them.
- **Evening:** Make sure every bird has gone to roost inside the house and shut the door securely (or check the automatic door has closed). Scatter a little corn in the afternoon if you like.
- **Clean:** Remove droppings from the droppings board or under the perches. It takes a couple of minutes with a scraper and keeps the house fresh.

### Every week

- Clean out and replace the bedding in the house, and replace any soiled nesting material.
- Wash and refill the drinker thoroughly.
- Rake over the run floor and remove any droppings or spoiled food.
- Check the birds more closely: pick each one up, feel her weight and crop, look under her feathers for lice and mites, and check her legs and feet.
- Check the house and run for damage, holes and signs of digging.

### Every month or so

- Do a deep clean of the house: take everything out, scrub it, treat for red mite, let it dry, and replace with fresh bedding.
- Top up or replace the run floor material.
- In spring and autumn, worm the flock, or have a sample of their droppings tested to see whether worming is needed.

### Common problems

**Red mite.** The red poultry mite is the bane of every chicken keeper's life. It is a tiny, grey mite that hides in cracks and crevices in the house during the day, especially at the ends of perches, and crawls out at night to feed on the sleeping birds' blood, after which it turns red. A bad infestation can make hens anaemic, stop them laying and even kill them. Check regularly by wiping a white tissue along the underside of the perches at night, or by looking in crevices for grey dust and tiny moving specks. Prevention is better than cure: choose a house with few crevices, clean regularly, and treat with an approved mite powder or spray. Some vets can prescribe a treatment given in the birds' drinking water. Once established, red mite are very hard to get rid of.

**Lice and scaly leg mite.** Lice live on the bird itself and can be seen around the vent and under the wings. Scaly leg mite burrows under the scales of the legs, making them look thick, crusty and raised. Both are treatable with products from a vet or poultry supplier.

**Worms.** Chickens pick up intestinal worms from the ground. Signs include weight loss, pale combs, reduced laying and diarrhoea. In the UK, there is a licensed wormer for chickens that can be mixed into the feed and has no egg-withdrawal period, which means you can keep eating the eggs during treatment. Many keepers worm routinely twice a year; others send a sample of droppings for a worm count first, which is better practice and helps avoid resistance.

**Broodiness.** A broody hen is one that has decided it is time to hatch some eggs. She will sit in the nest box all day, fluff up, growl and peck when disturbed, and stop laying. If you have fertile eggs and want chicks, this is wonderful. If not, it can be a nuisance, as the hen will sit for weeks on nothing, losing condition, and other hens may join in. To "break" a broody hen, keep removing her from the nest, block off the nest box, or put her for a few days in a wire-floored cage with food and water, which cools her underside and resets her hormones.

**Moulting.** Every year, usually in late summer or autumn, chickens shed their old feathers and grow new ones. This is the moult. It can be dramatic, with hens looking bald and miserable for a few weeks. During the moult, hens usually stop laying, because feathers are almost pure protein and the hen needs all her protein to grow them. Give extra protein, such as mealworms, and leave them in peace. They will look magnificent once it is over.

**Heat and cold.** Chickens are surprisingly hardy in cold weather, as long as they are dry and out of draughts. They are much more vulnerable to heat. In hot weather, make sure they have plenty of shade, ventilation and fresh, cool water. Some keepers freeze fruit or water bottles to put in the run on very hot days.

**Signs of illness.** A sick chicken often hides it until she is quite ill. Signs to watch for include a hunched, fluffed-up posture, sitting apart from the flock, closed eyes, a pale comb, not eating, diarrhoea, sneezing, discharge from the eyes or nostrils, laboured breathing, or a swollen, squishy crop in the morning. If you see these signs, isolate the bird, keep her warm and quiet, and consult a vet. It is worth finding a vet who treats poultry before you need one, as not all do.

**Bird flu.** If your birds show signs such as sudden deaths, swollen heads, blue combs and wattles, difficulty breathing or a sudden drop in egg production, you must report it immediately to the authorities. Avian influenza is a notifiable disease. Follow government guidance on biosecurity: keep wild birds away from your hens' food and water, clean your boots, and do not let your birds mix with wild ducks and geese.

**Rats.** Rats are attracted by spilled food. Keep feed in metal bins, use rat-proof feeders, take feeders in at night or use treadle feeders, and never leave food scattered on the ground overnight. A rat problem can very quickly become serious.

*A broody hen with her chicks. When it works, there is no better incubator and no more devoted parent.* {{credit:hen-with-chicks}}

### Breeding and hatching

Sooner or later, most chicken keepers are tempted to hatch some chicks. To do so, you need fertile eggs, which means either keeping a cockerel or buying fertile "hatching eggs" from a breeder. You then need either a broody hen or an incubator.

A broody hen is by far the easiest option. Put a clutch of six to ten fertile eggs under her in a quiet, secure nest box, with food and water close by, and she will do almost all the work. Chicken eggs take about 21 days to hatch. The hen will turn the eggs, keep them at the right temperature and humidity, and then raise the chicks, teaching them to find food and protecting them fiercely.

Incubators are more work, but give you more control. You need to keep the temperature and humidity steady, turn the eggs (most modern incubators do this automatically), and then raise the chicks in a heated brooder for several weeks.

The big catch with hatching is that roughly half of the chicks will be cockerels. A few can be rehomed, but there are far more cockerels than homes for them. Before you hatch, decide what you will do with the surplus males. Many keepers raise them for the table; others have them humanely dispatched. It is not a pleasant decision, but it is an unavoidable one.

### The end of life

Hens can live for a long time after they stop laying, and many keepers let their old girls live out their days as pets. But every chicken keeper will eventually face a bird that is seriously ill or injured and needs to be put down. A vet can do this humanely for a fee. Many experienced keepers learn to do it themselves, quickly and correctly, which spares the bird a stressful journey. If you plan to learn, do so from an experienced keeper and practise the technique properly before you need it. The worst outcome is a suffering bird and a panicking owner.

## How Many Eggs to Expect

This is the question that matters most to most beginners, and the answer is: it depends, and it changes through the year.

### The basics

A young hen usually starts laying somewhere between 18 and 24 weeks old, depending on her breed and the time of year. Hybrids tend to start earlier; large, slow-maturing pure breeds later. Her first eggs are often small, oddly shaped, or have thin or no shells. These "pullet eggs" are perfectly good to eat, and things settle down within a few weeks.

An egg takes about 24 to 26 hours to form inside a hen, which means that even the most productive hen cannot lay more than one egg a day, and her laying time drifts slightly later each day until she skips a day and starts again. A good laying hybrid in her first season might lay five or six eggs a week; a heritage breed three or four; a Silkie perhaps two.

### The seasons

The biggest influence on egg production is daylight. Hens need around 14 hours of light a day to lay at full capacity, and when the days shorten in autumn and winter, production falls, often dramatically. In the depths of a British winter, when there are only eight hours of daylight, many pure breeds stop laying entirely, and even hybrids slow down. As the days lengthen after the winter solstice, laying picks up again, and spring and early summer are the time of plenty, when you may struggle to use all your eggs.

![Typical eggs per hen per week through the year](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/seasonal-laying.png)

*An illustration of how laying typically rises and falls through a British year for a first-season hybrid and a heritage-breed hen. The autumn dip is the moult and shortening days; individual birds, strains and weather all vary.*

The annual moult, usually in late summer or autumn, adds to the dip. Most hens stop laying while they moult, for anything from a few weeks to a couple of months.

Some keepers put a light in the hen house on a timer to extend the "day" in winter and keep the hens laying. Commercial egg farms do this routinely. It works, but many hobby keepers prefer not to, on the grounds that the winter break gives the hens a natural rest and may help them stay healthy for longer. Hens are born with a finite number of potential eggs, and pushing them through the winter is thought by many keepers to use up their laying life faster. It is your choice, but there is nothing wrong with accepting fewer eggs in winter.

### Age

Hens lay most in their first laying season and less each year after that. A rough pattern for a hybrid is a drop of perhaps 15 to 20 per cent a year, so a hen that lays 300 eggs in her first year might lay 250 in her second, 200 in her third, and fewer after that. Pure breeds start lower but tend to decline more gently and lay for longer. Most hens will lay little or nothing after five or six years, though some go on for longer.

### Other things that stop hens laying

If your hens suddenly stop laying, the cause is usually one of the following: shortening days, moulting, broodiness, stress (from a fox visit, a move, new birds, bad weather or a scary noise), heat, a poor diet, lack of water, parasites (especially red mite), illness, age, or a hen that is laying somewhere you have not found. Free-ranging hens are notorious for setting up secret nests under bushes, and many a keeper has found a stash of twenty eggs in the shrubbery after weeks of wondering where they had gone.

### Egg problems

You will occasionally get odd eggs: tiny "wind eggs" with no yolk, double-yolkers, eggs with wrinkled or ridged shells, soft-shelled eggs, and eggs with spots of blood in the yolk. Most are harmless and occasional. Frequent thin or soft shells suggest a shortage of calcium, so check that the hens have access to oyster shell and that they are not eating too many treats. Egg-eating, where a hen breaks and eats eggs in the nest, is a bad habit that spreads through a flock; collect eggs often, make sure the nest boxes are dark and well padded, and consider putting a ceramic or wooden "dummy egg" in the nest to teach the culprits that eggs are not worth pecking.

## What to Do With the Eggs

You have eggs. Lots of them. Now what?

![A basket of home-produced eggs in many colours](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/egg-colours.png)

*The egg box of a mixed flock: from Leghorn white and Sussex cream to Araucana blue, Welsummer terracotta and Marans chocolate.*

### Collecting and cleaning

Collect eggs at least once a day, and twice if you can. Clean eggs straight from a clean nest box need no washing. In fact, in Britain, washing is generally discouraged. A freshly laid egg is covered in a thin, invisible coating called the bloom, or cuticle, which seals the pores in the shell and helps keep bacteria out. Washing removes it. If an egg is dirty, wipe it gently with a dry cloth or brush, or, if it is very dirty, use it yourself quickly rather than storing it. Keeping the nest boxes clean is the best way to get clean eggs.

### Storing

Store eggs pointed end down, which keeps the air sac at the top and the yolk centred. Eggs keep well at a cool, steady room temperature or in the fridge; the important thing is to avoid big changes in temperature, which cause condensation on the shell. Fresh eggs from your own hens will keep for at least three weeks, and often longer. It is good practice to write the date on each egg in pencil, or keep them in boxes in date order, so you use the oldest first.

### The float test

If you are not sure how old an egg is, try the float test. Gently put it in a bowl of cold water. A very fresh egg sinks and lies flat on its side. As an egg ages, moisture evaporates through the shell and the air sac inside it grows, so an older egg tilts up on its end, and a very old one floats. Floaters are best thrown away.

![The egg float test](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/float-test.png)

*The float test. As an egg ages its air sac grows, so it tilts and eventually floats.*

### Using them

Fresh eggs are a different food from shop-bought ones. Poached eggs hold together beautifully, cakes rise higher, and a soft-boiled egg with a deep orange yolk is a small daily luxury. One curiosity: very fresh eggs are hard to peel when hard-boiled. Use eggs that are a week or two old for hard-boiling, or steam them rather than boiling, which makes them much easier to peel.

In spring, when you have more eggs than you can eat, there are plenty of ways to use or keep them. Make lemon curd, custards, quiches, pasta, meringues and mayonnaise. Pickle hard-boiled eggs in spiced vinegar, the traditional British pub snack. Freeze them: crack eggs into a bowl, beat them lightly with a pinch of salt or sugar, and freeze them in ice cube trays or small tubs; they keep for months and are excellent for baking. Or give them away. Nothing makes neighbours more tolerant of your hens than a regular box of fresh eggs on the doorstep.

### Selling eggs

Many keepers sell their surplus eggs, often from a box at the garden gate with an honesty tin. In Britain, small-scale producers can sell eggs directly to consumers, from their own premises or door to door, without grading or stamping them, but there are rules about how they can be labelled (you cannot claim a size or a grade, for example), and different, stricter rules apply if you sell at markets or to shops, cafés and bakeries. The rules are enforced by the Egg Marketing Inspectorate and can change, so check the current government guidance before you start selling, and always keep eggs clean, fresh and cool. Write a best-before date on the box; in the UK, eggs sold to consumers should carry one, and it is usually set at no more than 28 days from laying.

## Fun and Profit: The Honest Economics

So can you make money from chickens? The short answer is: a little, sometimes, if you are careful, but most people do not, and most people do not mind.

Let us do some rough sums for a typical beginner's flock of four hybrid hens in their first year. Prices vary widely by region, supplier and year, so treat these as illustrations, not quotations.

- **Feed:** four hens eating around 120 grams a day each will get through about 175 kilograms of layers pellets a year, roughly nine 20-kilogram sacks. At something like £12 to £18 a sack, that is perhaps £110 to £160.
- **Other running costs:** grit, oyster shell, mixed corn, bedding, mite treatments and wormer might add another £50 to £100.
- **Eggs:** four hybrids might lay a thousand eggs in their first year, or a little over 80 dozen.

That gives a running cost of very roughly £2 to £3 a dozen, which is not far from what free-range eggs cost in a supermarket. You have not yet paid for the house, the run, the feeders and the birds, which can easily add up to several hundred pounds, or well over a thousand for a large walk-in run and a premium plastic house. And egg production falls after the first year while feed costs do not.

So, in pure financial terms, home-produced eggs are about as expensive as good shop-bought ones once you count the set-up costs. The things that tip the balance are the things that do not appear on the spreadsheet: the quality of the eggs, the pleasure of the birds, the slugs eaten, the compost, the children's education, and the knowledge that your hens have had a good life.

If you want to make a real profit, there are a few routes. Selling surplus eggs at the gate can cover your feed bill, especially if you charge a fair price for genuinely fresh, free-range eggs. Selling hatching eggs from good-quality pure breeds, or raising pure-breed point-of-lay pullets to sell, can be more lucrative, but requires space, skill, an incubator, a plan for surplus cockerels, and a good reputation. Chicken manure is an excellent fertiliser and can be sold or swapped with gardeners. But for most beginners, the realistic aim is for the hens to pay for their own feed, and for everything else to be counted as fun.

## The Wildcard: Who Else Can Share the Garden?

Once you have chickens, it is very tempting to add other creatures. Some mix well with hens; others definitely do not. Here is a quick guide.

![Indian Runner ducks](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/runner-ducks.jpg)

*Indian Runner ducks: upright, comical, excellent layers and enthusiastic slug hunters.* {{credit:runner-ducks}}

**Ducks.** Ducks are probably the most popular companions for chickens, and several breeds are prolific layers. Khaki Campbells can lay close to 300 eggs a year, and Indian Runners, which stand upright and run around like animated wine bottles, are excellent layers and superb slug hunters. Duck eggs are larger and richer than hens' eggs, and bakers love them. Ducks and chickens can share a garden or run, but they have different needs. Ducks need water deep enough to dunk their whole heads, to keep their eyes and nostrils clean, and they splash it everywhere, which turns a chicken run into a swamp. They do not perch, and they prefer to sleep on the floor, so they are best given their own house. Drakes can also try to mate with hens, which can injure them. Ducks are hardy and rarely get red mite, but they are just as attractive to foxes.

![A domestic goose](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/geese.jpg)

*A domestic goose: grazer, guardian, and very loud.* {{credit:geese}}

**Geese.** Geese are grazers that eat mostly grass, so they need a decent-sized paddock or orchard. They make superb guard animals, honking loudly at anything unfamiliar, and they will sometimes see off a fox or a stray dog. They can live alongside chickens if there is enough space, but they can be bossy and territorial, especially in the breeding season. They are also very noisy, which neighbours may not appreciate. Not for small gardens.

![Coturnix quail](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/quail.jpg)

*Japanese quail: tiny birds, speckled eggs, and an astonishing laying rate for their size.* {{credit:quail}}

**Quail.** Japanese, or Coturnix, quail are small, quiet birds that can lay more than 250 tiny speckled eggs a year and need very little space. They are popular with people in small gardens. They should not be kept with chickens, though: they are much smaller, need a higher-protein diet, are easily bullied or killed by hens, and are best kept in their own secure aviary or hutch.

![Helmeted guineafowl](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/guinea-fowl.jpg)

*Guineafowl: the farmyard's burglar alarm, and voracious eaters of ticks and insects.* {{credit:guinea-fowl}}

**Guineafowl.** Guineafowl are half-wild, speckled birds from Africa. They eat huge numbers of insects, including ticks, and raise a deafening alarm at the slightest disturbance, which can help protect the flock from predators. They are often kept with chickens on farms. But they are very noisy, like to roost in trees, wander widely and are difficult to keep in a garden. They are best suited to a smallholding with forgiving neighbours.

![Turkey](/assets/essays/keeping-chickens-for-fun-and-profit/images/turkey.jpg)

*Turkeys are friendly and impressive, but should not be kept with chickens because of the risk of blackhead disease.* {{credit:turkey}}

**Turkeys.** Turkeys can be surprisingly friendly and personable, but they should not be kept with chickens. Chickens commonly carry, without much harm to themselves, a parasite that causes histomoniasis, or "blackhead", a disease that is often fatal to turkeys. The parasite can survive in the soil for years, so turkeys should not even be put on ground where chickens have been. Keep them separately, or not at all.

**Mammals.** Rabbits and guinea pigs are often suggested as companions for chickens, but they are generally better kept apart: they have different diets, chickens may peck them, and they can share diseases and parasites. Goats, sheep, ponies and alpacas can share a paddock with chickens perfectly well, and some smallholders claim that alpacas and some breeds of dog help to deter foxes. Dogs and cats need careful introduction: many dogs will chase and kill chickens, though many can be trained to ignore or even guard them, and while most cats will leave adult hens alone, they may go for chicks. And then there are bees, which have nothing to do with chickens at all, but tend to be the next thing chicken keepers want.

## Conclusion: The Good Life, One Egg at a Time

Keeping chickens is not complicated, but it is a commitment. It asks for a secure house and run, a decent amount of space, a sack of layers pellets every month or so, clean water, a little time every day, and a willingness to deal with mites, mud, foxes and the occasional sad goodbye. It will probably not make you rich. It may not even save you money.

In return, it gives you something that is hard to buy. It gives you eggs that taste the way eggs are supposed to taste. It gives you a garden with fewer slugs and better compost. It gives you a small, funny, endlessly entertaining society of birds, each with her own personality, which will greet you at the run door every morning as if you were the most important person in the world, which, to a chicken holding out for a handful of corn, you are. It connects you, in a small way, to where food comes from and to the seasons, as the eggs dwindle in the dark days of December and come flooding back with the light in spring.

Start small, with three or four hens of a friendly, hardy breed. Build or buy a house that is bigger and better than you think you need, and a run that a fox cannot get into. Feed them properly. Check them every day. Keep a notebook. Learn from other keepers, who will be only too happy to help. And when you hear that first proud *buk-buk-buk-BA-GAWK* from the bottom of the garden, go and look in the nest box. There is no better welcome to the world of chicken keeping.
