## The Lie That Gets You Home

Consider the most useful lie you will be told this week. It will probably be delivered to you on a screen, in bright primary colours, by a company that wants to know where you are. It says that the world is flat, that the roads are tidy lines, that your journey is a single blue ribbon from here to there, and that everything on the surface of the Earth can be laid out for inspection like a tablecloth. None of this is true. The Earth is a lumpy, slightly squashed ball; the roads are messy, contested and constantly being dug up; and your journey will involve a great many decisions that the blue ribbon politely declines to mention. Yet you will follow the lie, and it will get you home.

This is the central paradox of maps, and it has been true for as long as people have made them. Every map is a distortion, a simplification, an argument. A map that showed everything would be as large as the thing it described and about as helpful. (Lewis Carroll joked about this in 1893, in *Sylvie and Bruno Concluded*, where a country builds a map on the scale of a mile to the mile; the farmers object that it blocks out the sun, and so the country itself is used as its own map instead. Jorge Luis Borges later gave the idea a darker twist in a one-paragraph story about a map that outlives the empire that made it and rots in the desert.) The art of the map-maker is the art of deciding what to leave out. A good map is a lie that has been carefully chosen to be useful.

That is why the history of maps is so much more than the history of geography. It is a history of power, because whoever draws the map decides what is on it, and what is on the map tends to become real. It is a history of money, because most great advances in cartography were paid for by merchants, navies, tax collectors and kings. It is a history of mathematics, because the problem of flattening a sphere onto a page defeated the greatest minds for centuries and, in a precise sense, can never be solved. It is a history of mistakes, some of them hilarious (California was an island for about a century) and some of them catastrophic (a line drawn in five weeks across the Punjab). And, perhaps most surprisingly, it is a history that stretches far beyond geography, to maps of disease, of poverty, of the stars, of the ocean floor, of the genome and of Middle-earth.

In this essay we shall travel from the earliest scratches on bone and stone to the satellites that now watch you cross the road. We shall meet a Babylonian scribe who put his own city at the centre of the cosmos, a Greek librarian who measured the Earth with a stick, a Sicilian king who commissioned a silver map of the world, a German monk's workshop that accidentally named two continents, a London draughtsman who made the most imitated diagram of the twentieth century, and a doctor who stopped an epidemic by putting dots on paper. We shall also try to answer a few awkward questions along the way. Why does Greenland look as big as Africa? Why is the Tube map wrong, and why does that not matter? And what happens to the human brain when it stops needing to know where it is?

First, though, we have to go back a very long way: to a time before there was writing, let alone atlases.

## Scratches in the Dark: The First Maps

Nobody knows what the first map was, and the reason is not just that the evidence is old. It is that we cannot agree on what counts. If a map is a representation of space intended to help someone understand or navigate it, then the first map was probably made long before anything survived. Imagine a hunter in the Pleistocene scratching a river bend in the mud to show a companion where the herd was drinking. That is a map. It is also gone by lunchtime.

What does survive are objects that a few archaeologists, with varying degrees of confidence, have read as maps. The most contested is also the most romantic. In the Lascaux caves in the Dordogne, painted perhaps 17,000 years ago, there are clusters of dots that some researchers have argued represent star patterns, including, in one much-discussed reading, the Pleiades. If so, it would be a sky map before it was a land map. The trouble is that dots are dots, and humans are astonishingly good at finding patterns in them. Most scholars regard the claim as intriguing, but unproven.

Firmer ground, so to speak, is found at Mezhyrich in Ukraine, where excavators discovered a settlement built from the bones of mammoths. One of the objects found there is a carved mammoth tusk, roughly 12,000 to 15,000 years old (the dating is debated), whose incised lines have been interpreted as a plan of the settlement itself, with huts and what may be a river. In the Abauntz cave in Navarre in northern Spain, researchers described an engraved stone of around 13,000 years old that seems to show mountains, a river and perhaps the animals that were hunted there. Both interpretations are disputed, and the honest position is that these are *possibly* the oldest maps in the world, with the possibility doing most of the work. At Çatalhöyük in Anatolia, a Neolithic town of mud-brick houses, a wall painting from around 6600 BCE was once hailed as the oldest town plan ever made: a grid of rectangles that looked like houses, with a double-peaked shape above it that looked like an erupting volcano. Later researchers have suggested that it may instead be a decorative geometric pattern, and the volcano may be a leopard skin. The world's oldest map, in other words, may not be a map. It may be wallpaper.

The truth is that early humans very probably did make maps, and some of those traditions survived into modern times in forms that make the point beautifully. The Inuit of Greenland carved coastlines onto pieces of driftwood, wearing them as three-dimensional maps in which every cove and headland could be felt with a mitten on, in the dark, and which floated if they fell in the sea. In the Marshall Islands of the Pacific, navigators built "stick charts" from the ribs of coconut palm fronds, tied together with fibre, with small shells marking islands and the curved sticks showing the way that ocean swells bend around them. These are not maps of places at all. They are maps of *waves*. A navigator learnt to read the swells with his body, lying in the bottom of the canoe, feeling the pattern of the sea against the hull, and the stick chart was a teaching aid that was rarely carried on the voyage itself. It is one of the first hints in this essay that a map need not look like what we think a map looks like.

Equally, the great Polynesian voyagers who settled islands across an area of ocean larger than any land empire in history did so without a single piece of paper. They carried, in memory, a map in the form of star paths, bird flights, swell patterns and cloud shapes, and passed it on in songs. We call such things "mental maps" or "oral maps", but the label does them an injustice. They were maps in everything but medium, and they got people across thousands of kilometres of open sea to targets a few kilometres wide.

So the first maps, whatever they were, were probably practical, small-scale and local: where the water is, where the animals go, where the neighbours live. The idea of a *map of the world* had to wait for something that early hunters did not have. It needed cities.

## Clay, Papyrus and Taxes: Maps in the Ancient Near East

Cities, it turns out, require two things that make maps almost inevitable. They need to know who owns what, so that the tax collector can do his job; and they need to know where things are, so that the temple, the army and the canal-diggers can do theirs. The oldest maps that we can confidently identify as maps come from the world's first urban civilisations, and they are all about property and plumbing.

In Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates, scribes pressed their records into clay with reed styluses, and some of those clay tablets are plans. A tablet from the site of Gasur (later called Nuzi), in what is now northern Iraq, dates from around 2300 BCE and shows a plot of land between two hills, with a stream running through it and the cardinal directions marked in cuneiform. It is a deed with a diagram attached. Another, from the city of Nippur, drawn around 1500 BCE and now in a museum in Jena, in Germany, shows the city in remarkable detail: the walls, the gates, the temple of the god Enlil, the canal, the river Euphrates and a park. It was apparently drawn to scale, and when archaeologists later excavated Nippur, they found that the tablet's layout matched the ruins. Here, we can say with confidence, is a city plan that was used.

Meanwhile, in Egypt, the Turin Papyrus, drawn around 1150 BCE in the reign of Ramesses IV, depicts the area of the Wadi Hammamat in the Eastern Desert, where the pharaoh's quarrymen dug a hard dark stone for statues and where there were gold mines nearby. The papyrus shows the wadi, the roads, the mines, a settlement and the hills, with the hills coloured in different shades to indicate different kinds of rock. It is, in effect, an ancient geological map, made for an expedition that wanted to extract something valuable and get home again. It is also, as far as anyone can tell, the oldest surviving map to be made specifically for a resource-extraction project, which tells you something about the priorities of the human race.

But the one that everyone wants to talk about is the Babylonian Map of the World, a clay tablet in the British Museum that was excavated at Sippar in the nineteenth century and acquired by the museum in 1882. The tablet is about the size of a large mobile phone, 12 centimetres tall and 8 wide, and dates from somewhere between the ninth and sixth centuries BCE (a later date in that range is more probable). On it, a circle is inscribed in cuneiform. Inside the circle, the river Euphrates runs down the middle, and the city of Babylon sits upon it, slightly north of the centre. Other places are shown as little circles: Susa, the capital of Elam; Urartu, up in the north-east; and the cities of Assyria. Around the whole thing is a ring labelled "bitter river", which is to say the salt ocean. Beyond the ocean are triangles that the scribe labelled *nagu*, meaning "regions", each at a stated distance, and accompanied by tantalising notes on the reverse of the tablet. One of the regions is described as a place where a winged bird cannot complete its journey. Another has been plausibly linked, by the British Museum's Irving Finkel, to the mountain on which the survivor of the Flood, Utnapishtim, landed his ark.

Let us try to imagine the scene. This is an entirely invented conversation, but the sort of thing that might have been said in a scribal school.

*"Master, why is Babylon in the middle?"*

*"Because that is where we are."*

*"But the map says the Euphrates runs through the middle of the world. What about the Nile?"*

*"The Nile is on the other side of the bitter river, and the bitter river is not for boys to cross. Write what you are told."*

The Babylonian Map of the World is not a navigation aid; you could not use it to find your way from Sippar to Susa, and it was never meant to be. It is a statement about the cosmos, a piece of geography fused with creation myth, and it shares the habit of nearly every world map in history, which is to put the map-maker's own homeland at or near the centre. We will see that habit again, in medieval Europe, in imperial China and in the modern world. The difference between the Babylonian scribe and a twenty-first-century satnav is not that one is self-centred and the other is not. It is that the satnav puts *you* at the centre, quite literally, with a little blue dot.

The Babylonians gave us other things that matter to our story. They divided the circle into 360 degrees, a convention that survives on every map and compass today, and they developed the base-sixty arithmetic that explains why an hour has sixty minutes. Mesopotamian mathematicians could calculate the area of a field and the length of a diagonal. All of this fed, through the Greeks, into the idea that the Earth could be measured. And it is to the Greeks that we now turn, since they were the first people to ask a question that sounds silly and is not: how big is the world?

## Measuring the Sphere: Greeks and Romans

The Greeks did not invent maps, but they did something almost as important: they argued about them. Around 550 BCE, Anaximander of Miletus is said to have drawn a map of the known world, and his successor Hecataeus improved on it. These early Greek maps were disc-shaped, ringed by Ocean, with the Mediterranean in the middle and Greece, naturally, rather near the centre. The historian Herodotus, writing a century later, poked fun at them. He had met, he said, a great many people who drew the Ocean flowing around the Earth in a perfect circle, as if with a compass, and he found it laughable that Europe, Asia and Libya (as Africa was then called) were all drawn as if they were the same size. (He was right to laugh, although his own picture of the world had its eccentricities.)

The decisive step was the realisation that the Earth is a sphere. Pythagoras's followers may have suggested it; Aristotle, in the fourth century BCE, gave arguments that anyone can check: the Earth's shadow on the Moon during an eclipse is always round, and travelling south makes new stars appear above the horizon. Once the world was a ball, it could be measured, and the man who did it was Eratosthenes of Cyrene, the chief librarian at the great Library of Alexandria in the third century BCE.

Eratosthenes had heard that at midday on the summer solstice, in the southern city of Syene (modern Aswan), the Sun shone straight down a deep well, lighting the water at the bottom and casting no shadow. In Alexandria, at the same moment, a vertical stick did cast a shadow. The angle of that shadow was about one-fiftieth of a circle. If Alexandria was about 5,000 *stadia* north of Syene, then the whole circumference of the Earth must be fifty times that: 250,000 *stadia*. Nobody is sure exactly how long Eratosthenes's *stadion* was, and so we cannot be sure how close he got; depending on the unit used, his answer is somewhere between a couple of per cent and about sixteen per cent away from the true figure of roughly 40,000 kilometres. For a man with a stick, a well and a camel-driver's estimate of distance, this is astonishing. He also drew a map of the known world on a grid of parallels and meridians, giving the first proper scientific framework for cartography. A century later, Hipparchus suggested dividing the globe into 360 degrees and fixing places by latitude and longitude, borrowing the Babylonian number.

Here is the imagined Eratosthenes, a little irritable, dealing with a visitor.

*"You are saying the world is round, and you have measured it with a shadow."*

*"I am saying that I have measured the world with a shadow and a pedometer-man. Do not mention the pedometer-man."*

*"Why not?"*

*"Because he is the weak point in the experiment."*

(Eratosthenes did employ trained walkers, known as *bematists*, who counted their paces, and the ancient Egyptians had been measuring distances this way for centuries. The joke is the author's; the method is real.)

The pinnacle of Greek cartography came in the second century CE, when Claudius Ptolemy, working in Alexandria, compiled the *Geographia*. It is not so much a map as a manual for making maps, and it listed the coordinates, in latitude and longitude, of roughly 8,000 places. Ptolemy explained how to project a spherical surface onto a flat page, with curved lines of latitude and longitude. If you had his book, a pen and enough patience, you could reconstruct the world map yourself. That, indeed, is exactly what Europeans did, a millennium and a half later.

Ptolemy was wrong about a great many things. He used a figure for the Earth's circumference that was roughly a quarter too small, borrowed from the philosopher Posidonius in preference to Eratosthenes's far better value. He stretched Eurasia too far east. He closed off the Indian Ocean with a vast southern continent, so that it became a sort of lake. These errors mattered. When Ptolemy's work reached Renaissance Europe, the underestimate of the Earth's size became one of the arguments for the belief that Asia could be reached by sailing west, an idea in which a certain Genoese mariner, equipped with a still smaller figure of his own, had considerable faith. Ptolemy's mistake, in other words, helped to cause the European discovery of the Americas. We shall return to the consequences.

The Romans were, for the most part, less interested in theory than in logistics, and Roman cartography was largely about roads, boundaries and taxes. Land surveyors, the *agrimensores*, laid out grids of farmland across the empire, and some of their cadastral plans were engraved in bronze and marble (large fragments of one such cadastre survive from Orange, in southern France). In Rome itself, under the emperor Septimius Severus, around 203 to 211 CE, a marble plan of the entire city was carved on a wall in the Temple of Peace: the *Forma Urbis Romae*. It was about 18 metres wide and 13 metres high, at a scale of roughly 1 to 240, and it showed every street, building and staircase. Only fragments survive, perhaps a tenth to a seventh of the original, and scholars still gather in basements fitting together the pieces of the world's largest jigsaw puzzle, with a certain amount of Roman graffiti to puzzle out.

The most famous Roman map, however, is one that nobody in Rome ever saw in its current form. The *Tabula Peutingeriana* is a medieval copy, made in about the thirteenth century, of what may have been a fourth-century original. It is a parchment scroll roughly 6.8 metres long and only 34 centimetres high, and it shows the Roman road network from Britain to India. Because the scroll's format squashes the Mediterranean world into a long thin strip, the map has to sacrifice geography to preserve the order of things: the Mediterranean is a narrow channel, and Italy is a strange knobbly shape. What the map shows is not *where* places are but how to get from one to another, with the distances between road stations marked in numbers, and symbols for spas, lighthouses and inns. In this sense it is the ancestor of every route diagram ever drawn, including a certain famous one that we shall meet in a later section. It is now in the Austrian National Library in Vienna.

## East, West and the Middle: China, Islam and Medieval Europe

While the Mediterranean world was learning to measure, another map tradition was developing independently on the other side of the continent. China has the longest unbroken tradition of cartography in the world. Wooden boards found in a tomb at Fangmatan in Gansu province, dating from the third century BCE, are painted with maps of rivers and forests, and a set of silk maps from a tomb at Mawangdui, sealed in 168 BCE, shows a kingdom's topography and a military deployment in such detail that archaeologists could verify features against the landscape. In the third century CE, the official Pei Xiu set out six principles for making maps, including the use of a grid, scale, the measurement of distance and the allowance for slopes and winding roads. Maps, in the Chinese tradition, were administrative tools, and so they had to be *measured*.

The most striking example is the *Yu Ji Tu*, or "Map of the Tracks of Yu", carved in stone in 1137 in the Song dynasty and still preserved in Xi'an. It is overlaid with a grid of squares, each representing 100 *li*, and its depiction of China's rivers and coastline is startlingly close to the real thing. A Korean world map from 1402, the *Kangnido*, went further, showing Africa as a triangle and Europe, drawn from information passed along the Mongol-Islamic world, nearly a century before Columbus. The Mongol empire, which linked East Asia to the Middle East, had become the world's fastest information highway.

In the Islamic world, scholars preserved and extended Ptolemy. The mathematician al-Khwarizmi, whose name gave us "algorithm", compiled a geography in the ninth century, and al-Biruni, working in the eleventh, calculated the Earth's radius with a remarkable method involving the dip of the horizon from a mountaintop. The greatest map of the period, however, was made by a man who worked for a Christian king. Muhammad al-Idrisi, born in Ceuta, was invited to Palermo by Roger II, the Norman ruler of Sicily, who wanted the best map of the world that money and learning could produce. Al-Idrisi spent about fifteen years at it, interviewing travellers and merchants and reconciling their stories with Ptolemy and with Arabic sources. The result, completed in 1154, was a book, the *Kitab Rujar* ("Book of Roger"), together with a silver disc engraved with the world map, which was said to weigh some 400 Roman pounds and which was lost in the unrest that followed Roger's death. The surviving manuscript maps are drawn with south at the top, which looks upside-down to a modern eye but was a common convention among Arab cartographers. This was the best map of the world for three centuries.

If the Islamic world preserved the Greek tradition, medieval Christian Europe drew its maps for rather different purposes. The typical medieval *mappa mundi* is a "T-O" map: a circle (the O) representing the world-ocean, divided by a T-shaped body of water into three continents, Asia at the top, Europe at lower left and Africa at lower right, with Jerusalem at the centre and the East (and the Garden of Eden) at the top. These were maps for contemplation. They were not meant for finding your way to Lübeck. They were theology rendered as geography, and nobody who painted them supposed that the Earth was literally shaped like a pie chart.

The grandest surviving example is the Hereford Mappa Mundi, made around 1300 and drawn on a single calf-skin of about 1.6 by 1.3 metres. It is crammed with detail: Noah's Ark, the Tower of Babel, the Golden Fleece, the Pillars of Hercules, and a menagerie of monstrous peoples, from the Blemmyae, who have faces in their chests, to the Sciapods, who shade themselves with an enormous single foot. Britain and Ireland sit in the bottom left corner, a sort of provincial footnote, while the figure of Christ presides over the whole at the top. It is displayed in Hereford Cathedral, where in the late 1980s its fate hung in the balance. The cathedral's chapter, hard up and worried about the building's upkeep, proposed to sell the map at auction; a public outcry, a grant from the National Heritage Memorial Fund and a large donation from the philanthropist Paul Getty secured it for the nation, and it now lives in a purpose-built home alongside the cathedral's chained library. (A still larger map, at Ebstorf in Germany, some three and a half metres across, was destroyed in the bombing of Hanover in 1943, and is known only from copies and photographs.)

It would be a mistake to think the Middle Ages produced nothing but mystical maps. The same centuries saw the rise of the *portolan chart*: a navigation aid from the Mediterranean, drawn on vellum, covered in a web of straight lines radiating from compass roses, and annotated with the names of ports along the coast written inland in a crowd of tiny script. Their purpose was to tell a sailor which compass bearing to follow between harbours. The oldest surviving example, the *Carta Pisana*, dates from around 1290. These charts were astonishingly accurate for the Mediterranean and Black Sea, to the surprise of modern analysts, who cannot easily explain how the coastlines were so well proportioned, given the available tools. The best answer seems to be: compass, log-book, and a collective and very practical form of crowd-sourcing among pilots. Late in the fourteenth century, the Majorcan workshop of Abraham Cresques produced the Catalan Atlas of 1375, a magnificent world map that combined portolan accuracy with scraps of news from the interior of Africa and Asia. In the west of Africa, a black king wearing a crown sits on a throne and holds a gold nugget: Mansa Musa of Mali, whose pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 had scattered so much gold in Cairo that it depressed the price for years.

Closer to home for the readers of this essay, the Gough Map (held in the Bodleian Library in Oxford) shows Britain with a network of roads and towns, dating from the mid-fourteenth century, although the exact date is debated. It is the earliest surviving map to show Britain in anything like a recognisable form with a road network, and while it puts east at the top and gives Scotland an odd twist, its depiction of the English south-east is startlingly good. The author has not been identified. Whoever drew it had access to considerable local knowledge, and perhaps to the journals of royal messengers and itinerant officials. Maps, even then, were assembled from other people's footsteps.

By the fifteenth century, the stage was set for an explosion. Portugal and Spain wanted routes to the spice trade. Italian cities wanted to hold on to their middle-man profits. Ptolemy's *Geographia* had been translated into Latin around 1406 and was spreading through Europe, soon to be printed. And ships were getting better. What happened next was the most dramatic change in the history of mapping, and the first thing to change was the shape of the world itself.

## The Map That Named a Continent: The Age of Discovery

In 1492, a German merchant and navigator named Martin Behaim, working in Nuremberg, finished a globe, the *Erdapfel* ("earth apple"). It is the oldest surviving terrestrial globe, and it is a handsome thing, with the world as the best-informed Europeans understood it. It also has no Americas. Between Europe and Asia there is only a great expanse of ocean, with Japan (Cipangu) floating in the middle, more or less where California, Mexico and the Pacific should be. Behaim finished it, we assume, before news of Columbus's first voyage arrived. It is a snapshot of the last moment in which Europeans could look at the world and fail to see it.

Within a decade, every map had to be redrawn. The best evidence of how fast the knowledge moved, and how jealously it was guarded, is a Portuguese map of 1502 known as the Cantino planisphere. It shows the new lands in the west, the African coast, the Indian Ocean and the line agreed at the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494, by which Spain and Portugal, with papal blessing, divided the non-European world between them along a north-south meridian in the Atlantic. The map was the property of the Portuguese crown, and its production was a state secret. But an agent of the Duke of Ferrara, Alberto Cantino, smuggled a copy out of Lisbon for a payment of twelve gold ducats, and so it survives. Mapmaking was already a matter of national security, and map-theft was espionage. In Seville and Lisbon, the master chart of the discoveries, the *Padrón Real*, was guarded by officials sworn to secrecy, and pilots were not permitted to take it out of the country.

The Americas, meanwhile, needed a name. In 1507, in the small town of Saint-Dié in the Vosges mountains of eastern France, a group of scholars and printers attached to the court of the Duke of Lorraine produced a world map on twelve woodcut sheets which, assembled, measured around 2.4 by 1.3 metres. Its chief author was Martin Waldseemüller, a cartographer; his colleague Matthias Ringmann was a humanist poet and scholar. They had read an account of the voyages of the Florentine navigator Amerigo Vespucci, who had argued that the lands to the west were not Asia but a "New World", and they decided to honour him. On their map, across the southern continent, they printed a word: AMERICA. It was the first time the name had been used. (The usual explanation is that they simply decided it would be a good idea to name a continent after someone, and that since the other three had feminine names, Europa, Asia and Africa, Americus became America. Waldseemüller later thought better of it, and omitted the name from a later map in 1513, but it was too late; the name had caught on.)

About a thousand copies of the map were printed, and for centuries all of them were lost. Then, in 1901, a German Jesuit historian named Joseph Fischer found one in the library of Wolfegg Castle in Baden-Württemberg, bound into a volume with other maps. It is the only known copy. In 2003, the Library of Congress in Washington bought it from the Prince of Waldburg-Wolfegg for $10 million, and displays it today in a climate-controlled case, as "America's birth certificate".

![The 1507 world map of Martin Waldseemüller, the first to use the name "America".](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Waldseemuller_map_2.jpg?width=1000)

*Martin Waldseemüller's world map, 1507. Public domain; via Wikimedia Commons (original held by the Library of Congress).*

Not every famous map from this period is quite what it is said to be. The Piri Reis map, drawn in 1513 on gazelle skin by an Ottoman admiral and rediscovered in the Topkapı Palace in Istanbul in 1929, is a genuine treasure, and the surviving fragment, the western part of a larger world map, includes information from Columbus's own charts. It is also a staple of pseudo-history, in which its southern landmass is said to show Antarctica, ice-free, as drawn by a lost civilisation or visiting aliens. It does nothing of the kind. The southern land is simply South America, bent eastwards to fit onto the skin, as cartographers commonly did when their parchment ran out. Maps invite fantasy, and this is a good example of why it is worth checking the details.

Europe's mapmakers now had two jobs: to record the discoveries and to find ways to make them useful. The Low Countries took the lead, helped by Antwerp's position as a centre of commerce and printing. In 1570, a merchant of maps named Abraham Ortelius published in Antwerp the *Theatrum Orbis Terrarum* ("Theatre of the World"), a collection of around fifty maps of uniform size, engraved on copper and bound into a single volume, each accompanied by descriptive text. It is considered the first modern atlas. It was an instant success, ran to many editions in several languages, and made its author rich. Its publication coincided with a revolution in a different but related aspect of map technology: copper-plate engraving gave finer detail than woodcuts, and allowed the same plate to be reprinted many times. Map-making had become an industry.

The most famous name in this world, though, is Gerardus Mercator, a Flemish geographer who had been briefly imprisoned for heresy, and who made a world map in 1569 on eighteen sheets with a particular property: lines of constant compass bearing were straight. For a navigator, this was a gift. If you wanted to sail from Lisbon to Brazil, you could draw a straight line on Mercator's chart, read off its angle with a protractor, and set your compass to it. (He also gave us the word "atlas" as a name for a bound collection of maps, by using Atlas, the Titan, on the title page of a work that his son published after his death in 1595.) The price of the straight-line property is that the further you go from the equator, the more the map stretches the land. Mercator's world is, as we shall see, one of the great distortions of history. But it was no mistake. It was a tool, and an excellent one.

## The Problem That Cannot Be Solved: Flattening the Globe

Why does Greenland look the size of Africa? The answer is that Greenland is not the size of Africa; Africa is about fourteen times larger. But on a Mercator map, Greenland, which lies far to the north, is stretched in both directions, while Africa, straddling the equator, is barely stretched at all. The result is a distortion that has been fooling schoolchildren, and some adults, for four and a half centuries. It is a bit like looking at yourself in a funfair mirror that makes your head enormous but leaves your feet alone.

The reason for this is not carelessness; it is mathematics. In 1827 the German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss proved what is known as the *Theorema Egregium*, the "remarkable theorem", which has a consequence that every cartographer must live with: a sphere cannot be flattened onto a plane without distortion. You can keep angles correct, as Mercator did (a property called *conformal*), but then areas will be wrong. You can keep areas correct (*equal-area*), but then shapes will be distorted. You can compromise, with a bit of both, but you cannot have it all. The simplest demonstration is to peel an orange and try to lay the skin flat. It tears, or it stretches, or it wrinkles. There is no way of doing it neatly.

Cartographers have responded with a gallery of projections, each with its own personality. The Gall–Peters projection, named after a Scottish clergyman, James Gall, who devised it in 1855, and a German filmmaker and historian, Arno Peters, who promoted it a century later, is an equal-area map in which Africa and South America are properly big, though at the cost of stretching them like pulled toffee. Peters presented his map in 1973 as a corrective to Eurocentrism, and a long and rather bad-tempered row followed, in which professional cartographers complained that Peters was claiming credit for an old idea and that his map was no better than many others. The argument went mainstream when an episode of the American television drama *The West Wing*, in 2001, featured a fictional "Organization of Cartographers for Social Equality" lobbying the White House staff on the subject, with the cartographers eventually reducing a bemused official to despair with the intricacies of the Mercator projection. In 2017, Boston's public schools announced that they would use the Peters map in classrooms. In 1998, the National Geographic Society, after long years of using other projections, settled on one called the Winkel tripel, a compromise designed to keep distortions of area, direction and distance all low, without eliminating any of them. Today, if you want the most reasonable-looking flat world, that is your best bet.

Here is an imagined exchange between two cartographers at a conference dinner, since this is the kind of thing that really is argued about at conference dinners.

*"Mercator is a crime against geography."*

*"Mercator is a perfectly good map for sailing in 1569."*

*"But your daughter's atlas uses it."*

*"My daughter's atlas should be using a different one. But your Peters map makes Africa look like a limp wet sock."*

*"Peters's Africa has the correct area."*

*"It has the correct area and an incorrect everything else. Pass the wine."*

The deeper point is that there is no "correct" world map, only maps that are correct for particular purposes. Web mapping services today use a variant of Mercator called Web Mercator, chiefly because it preserves local shapes, which means that when you zoom in on a street corner, it looks like a street corner and not a warped version of itself. This is the real reason Mercator has survived. When you look at a street, shape matters more than relative area. When you look at the world, you may be better advised to use a globe.

## Triangles, Empires and Everest: Mapping the Nation

The great technical achievement of the eighteenth century was to make maps measured rather than guessed. The method was *triangulation*: choose a baseline on level ground, measure it with enormous care, and then, using a theodolite to measure the angles to a distant landmark, calculate the distances to everything else by trigonometry. Extend the network of triangles across a region, and eventually a whole country, and you have a framework against which the details can be hung.

The French led the way. The Cassini family, four generations of astronomers, began in 1747 to survey the entire kingdom of France by triangulation. By the time it was complete, in the 1790s, after the Revolution had taken the project over from the family, it consisted of 181 sheets at a scale of about 1 to 86,400, and it was the first map of an entire country based on rigorous triangulation. Britain, to its mild embarrassment, discovered that the French could now say exactly where Britain was in relation to France. In 1784 a Scottish military engineer, William Roy, measured a baseline across Hounslow Heath, west of London, using glass rods, and began the work of linking the British triangulation network with the French across the Channel, using a theodolite by the instrument maker Jesse Ramsden, of a quality unmatched at the time. Roy had learned his trade a generation earlier, in the Military Survey of Scotland, mapping the Highlands after the Jacobite rising of 1745, so that the government could find its enemies. That is how a military need produced a national map.

In 1791, a year after Roy's death, the work was taken on by the Board of Ordnance, and the Ordnance Survey was founded. Its first map, of Kent, was published in 1801, in the shadow of an expected French invasion: the south coast was the priority. The OS evolved from a military body into a civil one, and is today the national mapping agency of Great Britain. Along the way, it covered the island with hundreds of concrete triangulation pillars (the "trig points" still beloved of walkers on hilltops, built mostly between the 1930s and the 1960s), and its maps became so detailed that the phrase "an Ordnance Survey map" could stand for the entire idea of an authoritative guide to the land.

Empire made surveying bigger and bolder. In 1802, a British East India Company officer, William Lambton, began the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India, intending to measure an arc of the meridian from the southern tip of the subcontinent to the Himalaya. The work took more than sixty years. Lambton's successor, George Everest, who became Surveyor General, carried on the work with obsessive accuracy; the Great Theodolite, an instrument weighing about half a tonne, had to be carried by teams of men and hauled up towers specially built for the purpose. The survey eventually found that a peak in Nepal, designated "Peak XV", was higher than any other in the world. The computations were carried out in 1852 by a team that included Radhanath Sikdar, an Indian mathematician and the head of the computing office, who is often credited with identifying it. Announced in 1856, with a height of 29,002 feet (about 8,840 metres), it was named after Everest in 1865, even though Everest himself had never seen it and had objected to such naming practices. (He also pronounced his name "Eve-rest", not "Ever-rest", so strictly speaking the world's highest mountain has been mispronounced for 160 years.) The current accepted height, after measurements in 2020 by Nepal and China, is 8,848.86 metres.

The trigonometrical survey could not enter Tibet, which was closed to foreigners. So the British trained Indian "pundits", men from the Himalayan borderlands who could pass as pilgrims or traders. Each was taught to walk at a precise pace, so that 2,000 paces made a mile, and to count them with a rosary of 100 beads rather than the usual 108, each bead standing for 100 paces, so that one full circuit of the rosary marked about eight kilometres. They hid their notes in prayer wheels and disguised their instruments as religious objects. One of them, Nain Singh Rawat, reached Lhasa in 1866, fixed its position and altitude, and walked roughly 2,000 kilometres. These men produced the first scientific map of the Tibetan plateau, and their bravery has been rather overlooked.

The surveying instinct had a darker side. Imperial maps did not just record territory; they claimed it. In 1884 and 1885, delegates of the European powers, together with the United States and the Ottoman Empire, met in Berlin to settle how Africa should be divided, with no African present. The borders that resulted were drawn with rulers on maps that were often blank in the interior, and they cut across language groups, kingdoms and trade routes, a legacy still felt today. In 1916, the British diplomat Mark Sykes and the Frenchman François Georges-Picot agreed on a secret division of the Ottoman Arab provinces into zones of influence; Sykes is reputed to have described the idea to ministers by drawing a line from the "e" in Acre to the last "k" in Kirkuk. And in 1947, a British barrister named Cyril Radcliffe, who had never before visited India, was given roughly five weeks to draw the borders that would partition British India into India and Pakistan, using out-of-date maps and census figures. The line he drew, through Punjab and Bengal, was made public only after independence, on 17 August 1947, and its consequences included one of the greatest mass migrations in history, with perhaps 10 to 20 million people displaced and many hundreds of thousands killed. A line drawn by a map-maker in a hurry is no longer a line on paper. It is a fence, and sometimes a wall.

## Maps That Are Not of Places

So far, every map in this essay has shown land, sea or sky. But the real intellectual revolution in cartography, and the reason the subject matters so much beyond geography, was the discovery that the map is a *method*. If you can take a mass of information and lay it out in space so that a human eye can see patterns that were invisible in a table, you have a map, whether or not the information has anything to do with territory. The method goes back at least to 1686, when the astronomer Edmond Halley published a chart of the trade winds, and to 1701, when he drew the Atlantic with lines joining places of equal magnetic compass variation: the first isolines. In 1826, the French engineer Charles Dupin shaded the departments of France by the level of illiteracy, inventing what we now call the choropleth map. The stage was set for the most famous non-geographic map in history.

### The Pump on Broad Street

In late August 1854, cholera broke out in Soho, in London. Within ten days, more than 500 people living within a few hundred metres of one another had died, and in the end the toll was about 600. The prevailing medical wisdom held that cholera was spread by "miasma": foul air rising from filth and decay. A local physician, John Snow, did not believe it. He had argued for years that cholera was carried in water. So he did what a miasma theorist would not have thought to do: he went from door to door, asking who had died and where the victims got their water. And then he drew a map of the district, marking each death with a black bar at the address, and each water pump with a cross.

The pattern was plain. The deaths clustered around the pump on Broad Street (now Broadwick Street). There were revealing exceptions: a workhouse nearby, with its own well, had almost no cases; the workers at a local brewery, who drank beer, or water from the brewery's own well, were largely spared; and a woman who lived far from Soho but liked the taste of the Broad Street water, and had it delivered, had died. Snow took his evidence to the parish board of guardians, and the pump handle was removed on 8 September. The curse of this story is that it has been over-simplified. The outbreak was already waning by then, as people fled the area. Snow's map did not immediately convert the medical establishment, which stuck with miasma for years. And the map was drawn partly to persuade, after the investigation, rather than to discover. A local curate, the Reverend Henry Whitehead, who had begun as a sceptic, continued the investigation and found the probable source of the outbreak: a baby at 40 Broad Street who had fallen ill with diarrhoea in the days before, whose soiled nappies had been washed into a cesspool that leaked into the well. Snow died in 1858, still not widely believed. But in the longer run the map became the founding image of epidemiology, and every modern disease dashboard, including the ones that shaped our lives during the pandemic of 2020, is its direct descendant.

### Poverty, Colour-Coded

Thirty years later, in the 1880s, a Liverpool shipowner named Charles Booth became troubled by claims that a quarter of London's people lived in destitution, and decided to find out for himself. He sent teams of researchers to walk every street in London, accompanied by local police officers and school board visitors, and to note down the condition of each household. Then he coloured the streets. Black was for the "lowest class, vicious, semi-criminal"; dark blue for the "very poor"; light blue for the "poor"; purple for mixed; pink for "fairly comfortable"; red for "well-to-do middle class"; and yellow for the "upper-middle and upper classes, wealthy". The maps, published from 1889, were the first to show a city's social structure at the level of the street. They showed what is hard to forget: that a black street and a yellow street could be a few steps apart. They are now held at the London School of Economics, which has put them online, and they remain a marvel. They also helped to persuade a generation that poverty was not a character flaw but a feature of a place, and that the state might do something about it. Booth's work led, indirectly, to the introduction of old-age pensions in Britain in 1908.

### The Worst Journey in the World, in One Picture

In 1869, a retired French civil engineer called Charles Joseph Minard published a single sheet on which a thick tan band runs from left to right, narrowing as it goes, with a black band returning below it. It is the story of Napoleon's invasion of Russia in 1812. The tan band shows the *Grande Armée* advancing: 422,000 men at the Niemen river, dwindling to about 100,000 by Moscow. The black band shows the retreat, thinning rapidly to about 10,000, with a line graph of the bitter temperature below it. Everything is on one page: the size of the army (the width of the band), its position on the map, its direction, the date and the temperature. The American statistician Edward Tufte, who made the image famous in the 1980s, said it might be the best statistical graphic ever drawn. It is hard to argue. You do not need to read French to know that something went terribly wrong at the river Berezina.

### A Circuit Diagram for the Underground

If there is one non-geographic map that you have used, it is probably the London Underground map. In the 1920s, the Tube map was geographical. It showed the lines as they actually curved beneath the city, which meant that the central area was a tangle and the outer suburbs spread out to the edge of the page. A young engineering draughtsman at the Underground's signals office, Harry Beck, who was in his late twenties, thought this was the wrong approach. A traveller underground, he reasoned, does not care about the surface geography. They care about the order of the stations, and where they can change trains. Taking his inspiration from electrical circuit diagrams, he drew the lines as straight horizontals, verticals and 45-degree diagonals, with evenly spaced stations, and expanded the crowded centre while shrinking the outskirts. He submitted his design in 1931 and was turned down by the publicity department as too radical. He tried again, and a trial edition appeared in 1933. It was an immediate success. Beck was paid, it is said, five guineas.

The map is wonderful, and it is also, in important respects, wrong, which is exactly the point. The Thames, which Beck added later and which twists in a regular curve, makes a mockery of the real river. Distances are distorted: Leicester Square and Covent Garden, which look like separate neighbourhoods on the map, are about 250 metres apart, a walk of three or four minutes, and the fare for the Tube ride between them is a notorious rip-off. People have been known to arrive at their destination, having changed trains twice, when they could have walked in less time. The map's design has been copied by almost every city in the world with a metro system, from Moscow to Sydney. It has spread to other networks too, to the point that its approach, the *schematic* map, is now almost a language in itself. It is a masterclass in the principle that a good map must lie, because the truth would only confuse you.

### The Floor of the Sea

Some of the greatest maps of the twentieth century were maps of places nobody had seen. In the 1950s, a geologist named Marie Tharp, working at Columbia University's Lamont Geological Observatory in New York, was given the job of turning sonar soundings of the Atlantic Ocean into a map of the sea floor. Women were not allowed on research ships at the time, so she worked from the data brought back by her colleague Bruce Heezen. Plotting the profiles, she noticed something odd: down the centre of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a great underwater mountain range, ran a deep valley. A rift. She suggested that it might be the place where the ocean floor was being pulled apart, and that it supported the idea of continental drift. Heezen, a firm sceptic of continental drift, dismissed her suggestion, reportedly as "girl talk". But within a few years the evidence mounted, and the rift valley became one of the pillars of the theory of plate tectonics. In 1977 Tharp and Heezen's data was turned into a spectacular painted world map of the ocean floor by the Austrian artist Heinrich Berann, and it transformed the way the public imagined the planet. Tharp, who died in 2006, was honoured by the Library of Congress as one of the greatest cartographers of the twentieth century.

### Maps of the Sky, the Body and the Mind

The principle works at every scale. The oldest surviving star chart on paper is a manuscript from the Silk Road cave-library at Dunhuang, in western China, dating from the seventh century; it shows more than a thousand stars in their constellations. Today, the European Space Agency's Gaia satellite, launched in 2013, has produced a catalogue of the positions of roughly 1.8 billion objects in the Milky Way. Gaia ended its observations in January 2025, and a further data release is expected around the end of this year. At a still larger scale, the Cosmic Microwave Background, the leftover glow of the early universe, has been mapped by a succession of satellites, including COBE, WMAP and Planck, producing a map of the sky as it was when the universe was about 380,000 years old: a blotchy mottled oval of hot and cold spots which is, quite literally, a baby photograph of everything.

At the smallest scales, cartography moves into the body. In 1913, a 21-year-old student named Alfred Sturtevant, working in T. H. Morgan's "fly room" at Columbia, realised that the frequency with which pairs of inherited traits in fruit flies were separated could be used as a measure of how far apart the genes were on the chromosome. He went home and, as he later recalled, spent most of the night drawing the first genetic map. The same logic, scaled up enormously, led to the Human Genome Project, declared complete in 2003, and to a gap-free sequence of every human chromosome, published in 2022. The brain, too, has been mapped: in 1909 the German neurologist Korbinian Brodmann divided the cerebral cortex into 52 areas by the structure of their cells, a numbering that neuroscientists still use. Even the periodic table is a map. When Dmitri Mendeleev set out the elements in 1869, he left gaps for elements that had not been discovered, and he predicted the properties of three of them, including gallium and germanium. They were found within twenty years. A map that predicts what is not yet on it has passed the strictest test there is.

### Treasure Island and the Hundred Acre Wood

Finally, there is the matter of imaginary countries. In 1881, in a rented cottage at Braemar in the Scottish Highlands, on a wet day, Robert Louis Stevenson drew a map to entertain his young stepson. He was soon filling in names such as "Spy-glass Hill" and "Skeleton Island", and he later said that the story grew out of the map. *Treasure Island* has been in print ever since. Tolkien began *The Hobbit* with a map, and wrote *The Lord of the Rings* with another, redrawn by his son Christopher, at his side; Tolkien wrote that he had wisely started with a map and made the story fit it. And the endpapers of *Winnie-the-Pooh*, drawn by E. H. Shepard in 1926, show the "Hundred Acre Wood" with Pooh's house, Piglet's house and a bridge from which sticks could be dropped, a map so clear that children have gone looking for it in the real forest it was based on, Ashdown Forest in Sussex, where they can indeed find a bridge for playing Poohsticks.

These maps are not lies; they are promises. They tell the reader that the world they are about to enter has an inside, with distances and directions, in which events happen in particular places, and in which it is therefore possible to be lost. The Polish-American scholar Alfred Korzybski put the caution that goes with all this in 1931: "the map is not the territory". It is a thing worth saying about all maps, from the Babylonian tablet to the one in your pocket. It is also worth saying that without maps, the territory would be harder to see.

## Honest Mistakes and Useful Lies

Maps go wrong in two ways: by accident and on purpose. The accidents are the more charming. For about a century, from the early 1620s, a great many European maps showed California as an island, thanks to a misreading of a Spanish friar's account; the error persisted despite evidence to the contrary until a Jesuit explorer, Eusebio Kino, crossed the land route to the head of the Gulf of California in 1701, and a Spanish royal decree of 1747 declared that California was not an island. In West Africa, a range called the Mountains of Kong, invented by the British cartographer James Rennell in the 1790s on the strength of a rumour, stretched across maps for the best part of a century, until a French explorer walked through the supposed barrier in the 1880s and found savannah. And at the end of the nineteenth century the American astronomer Percival Lowell mapped an elaborate network of "canals" on Mars, which turned out to exist only in his eyes and the limits of his telescope. (The dragons, incidentally, are mostly a myth: the phrase "here be dragons" appears on only a couple of surviving early globes, notably the Hunt–Lenox Globe of around 1510, and almost never on the maps of the period.)

The deliberate lies are more sinister. Mapmakers have long inserted tiny fictions into their work as copyright traps, so that a copyist would give himself away. The best-known example is Agloe, New York, a hamlet invented in the 1930s by two employees of the General Drafting Company, who coined the name from their initials. Years later, it turned up on a rival's map. The rival, it seemed, had stolen the fiction. But by then a general store at the crossroads had opened and, using the name on the map, called itself the Agloe General Store: the lie had become true. States have lied too. The Soviet Union deliberately distorted its public maps for decades, shifting rivers and roads, and omitting or misplacing details, as a defence against spies, while its military produced astonishingly detailed secret maps of the rest of the world, including British towns, with notes on bridge load limits. The authorities admitted the practice only in the late 1980s.

Even now, the question of whose map is on your screen matters. Digital map services show some disputed borders and names differently depending on where the user is located, so that a person in one country sees a boundary that a person in another does not. Every map has a point of view. The question is only whether the viewer knows it.

## From Paper to Pocket: The Digital Revolution

The modern map was born of two Cold War inventions and a tragedy. The first was the computer, which made it possible to store and analyse geographic information as layers of data. The Canadian geographer Roger Tomlinson, working on the Canada Land Inventory in the 1960s, built what is generally regarded as the first geographic information system (GIS) and became known as its "father". In 1969 a young American, Jack Dangermond, founded a company called Esri, which now sells the world's most widely used GIS software.

The second was satellite navigation. The American military's Navstar GPS programme launched its first satellite in 1978, and the full constellation of 24 was operating by the mid-1990s. For years the system was intended for the military, and civilians were given a deliberately degraded signal. Then, in September 1983, a Korean Air Lines passenger aircraft, Flight 007, strayed into Soviet airspace and was shot down, with the loss of all 269 people on board. In response, President Reagan announced that GPS would be made available for civilian use. In 2000, the deliberate degradation, known as Selective Availability, was switched off, and the accuracy available to a hiker with a handset jumped from about a hundred metres to a few metres. (The geodetic modelling behind it owed much to mathematicians such as Gladys West, who spent decades calculating the Earth's shape for the US Navy.) Today, GPS is joined by Russia's GLONASS, Europe's Galileo and China's BeiDou, and the timing signal from these satellites underpins not just your satnav but banking, power grids and mobile networks.

The third ingredient was the internet. Google Maps launched in February 2005, built partly on a start-up that Google had acquired; Google Earth followed the same year; Street View appeared in 2007. Apple, in 2012, launched its own Maps app so badly that its chief executive felt obliged to apologise. In 2004, a student at University College London, Steve Coast, started OpenStreetMap, a map of the world that anyone could edit, on the model of Wikipedia. Sceptics doubted that a map could be made that way; today OpenStreetMap is the backbone of many commercial services, and when a catastrophic earthquake struck Haiti in 2010, volunteers around the world traced roads and buildings from satellite imagery within days, giving rescuers a map where none had existed.

The consequences for how we think are only beginning to be understood. In 2000, the neuroscientist Eleanor Maguire and her colleagues at University College London scanned the brains of London taxi drivers, who must pass an exam known as "the Knowledge", demanding mastery of some 25,000 streets within roughly ten kilometres of Charing Cross and about 20,000 landmarks, and found that the rear part of the hippocampus, a region associated with spatial memory, was larger than in other people. A later study followed trainees over several years and found that those who qualified developed larger hippocampi than those who did not. Does the reverse hold? Is the part of our brain used for navigation shrinking, now that we follow the blue dot? The evidence is thin: studies have found that people who rely heavily on GPS tend to have a poorer memory for routes, but this does not prove that the satnav is the cause. What is certain is that every person who has ever driven into a lane that was too narrow, on the instructions of an app, has experienced the old truth of cartography: a map without judgement is an invitation to trouble.

## What Comes Next

The map in your pocket is already a very different kind of object from the map on the wall. A paper map is the same for everyone, and shows what the cartographer decided you should see. A digital map is dynamic, personal and alive: it shows the traffic as it is this minute, the restaurants you might like and a route chosen for you. It is also, in a sense, no longer a map but a conversation, in which you tell the system where you are, and the system tells you where to go. It knows where millions of people are, and in what direction they are moving. This is the first time in history that the map is mapping the mapper.

Look at what is being built. Self-driving vehicles use "high-definition" maps, accurate to centimetres, which record every lane marking, kerb and traffic light, and compare them with the vehicle's own sensors. Indoor mapping is turning shopping centres, airports and hospitals into navigable spaces. "Digital twins" (live, data-rich models of entire cities) are being used to plan everything from flood defences to bus routes. A consortium called the Overture Maps Foundation, set up in 2022 by Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and TomTom under the Linux Foundation, is attempting to produce open map data as a shared foundation. A company called what3words has divided the world into three-metre squares, each with a unique three-word address, which has found enthusiastic users among rescuers and some trenchant critics. And artificial intelligence is now able to produce maps from satellite pictures at a scale and speed that would have appalled Ordnance Survey's early draughtsmen.

And there are still unfinished frontiers. The seabed, the least-known surface on Earth, remains substantially unmapped at high resolution. The Seabed 2030 project, a collaboration between the Nippon Foundation and the General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans, aims to have the whole ocean floor charted by the end of this decade, and at the last count something over a quarter had been done. Orbiters around Mars and the Moon have given us maps of other worlds that are, in places, more detailed than our maps of our own ocean. We know the surface of Mars better than the bottom of the sea.

The deeper questions are not technical. Who owns a map of the world, when the map is a continuously updated, privately owned database? What happens to privacy when every movement is a data point? What happens to shared reality when everyone's map is different? And who draws the line, literally, in a world where borders are contested? These are the questions that the Berlin Conference and the Radcliffe Line raised in another age, and they will not go away because the pen has been replaced by an algorithm.

## The Dot in the Middle

![The Earth photographed from Apollo 17 in December 1972, the first time the whole sunlit globe was seen in a single photograph by a human crew.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/The_Earth_seen_from_Apollo_17.jpg?width=900)

*The Earth seen from Apollo 17, December 1972. Credit: NASA / Apollo 17 crew. Public domain; via Wikimedia Commons.*

Think again of the Babylonian scribe, 2,500 years ago or so, drawing a circle on a lump of clay and putting Babylon in the middle. He was making a claim about what mattered, and what was far away, and what could be safely ignored beyond the bitter river. He would recognise our own maps at once, not because they look like his, but because they do the same thing. Your phone puts a blue dot in the centre of the world and the rest of the planet arranges itself around you.

That is the great thing and the dangerous thing about maps. They make the world manageable, and they do so by deciding what to leave out. A good map is honest about its purpose: a Tube diagram does not claim to show you the streets above, and a Mercator chart does not claim to show you the true size of Greenland. A bad map is one that has forgotten, or hidden, what it is for. So the next time you follow the blue ribbon home, spare a thought for the long line of scribes, surveyors, monks, spies, draughtsmen and doctors who taught us to read it. And, if you have the time, look up from the screen at the real, unmapped, lumpy world, which is always bigger than the picture of it.
