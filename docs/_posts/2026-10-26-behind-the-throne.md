## The Other Chair

Westminster Abbey, on a wet Tuesday in October. A tour guide, a cheerful woman in a waterproof with a lanyard full of laminated cards, has stopped her group beside the tombs.

"Now, everybody asks about the kings," she says. "But look who's lying next to them."

A man in a rugby shirt peers at an effigy. "Who's she, then?"

"That's Eleanor of Castile. Edward I loved her so much that when she died he built a stone cross at every place her coffin stopped on its way here. Twelve of them."

"And her?"

"Philippa of Hainault. Talked her husband out of executing six men at Calais. On her knees, heavily pregnant, according to the chronicler."

"And who's that one, on her own?"

"Ah," says the guide, with the relish of someone who has been waiting all morning for this question. "That depends which queen you mean. One of them deposed her husband. One of them was buried with her husband's heart. And one of them had her body put on display for so long that Samuel Pepys kissed her on his birthday."

The group looks at her.

"Coffee break," she says. "Then I'll tell you everything."

Our previous essay, *Heavy Is the Head*, told the story of Britain's monarchs: how they won the crown, what they did with it and how they lost it. This is its companion, about the people in the other chair. Royal consorts have had no constitutional power at all. No law has ever given a queen consort the right to make war, raise taxes or sign a statute. And yet, over a thousand years, consorts have run the country as regents, raised armies, toppled favourites, led political parties, bankrolled rebellions, founded colleges, changed the national religion and, on one memorable occasion, invaded England and deposed the king.

Their power came from four sources. The first was **intimacy**: nobody had the monarch's ear, or the monarch's pillow, quite like a spouse. The second was **the heir**: a queen who produced a son became the mother of the next king, and a queen who failed to could find herself in very serious trouble. The third was **family**: a consort brought relatives, allies, money and sometimes an entire foreign alliance. And the fourth was **absence**: when the king went to war, somebody had to mind the shop, and often it was his wife.

For each consort we will ask, as before, the same questions. How did they get there? What power did they have over the monarch? How did their time as consort go, how did it end, and what did they leave behind? There are a great many of them (Henry VIII alone contributes six), so, as before, we will move quickly.

## The First Queens: Wives, Widows and Kingmakers (1002 to 1154)

### Emma of Normandy: the queen who married two kings

In Anglo-Saxon England a king's wife was not always called queen, and was not always crowned. The woman who changed that was Emma, daughter of the Duke of Normandy, who in 1002 married Æthelred the Unready as part of an alliance against the Vikings. When the Danes conquered England, Emma did something that astonished contemporaries: after Æthelred's death she married the conqueror, Cnut, in 1017. She was thus queen to two rival kings and, eventually, mother to two more: Harthacnut, her son by Cnut, and Edward the Confessor, her son by Æthelred.

Emma was a political operator of the first rank. She controlled a vast fortune in land and treasure, backed Harthacnut over Edward when Cnut died, and commissioned a book, the *Encomium Emmae Reginae*, "In Praise of Queen Emma", that told the story of the period in a version flattering to herself. It is one of the earliest works of history written to order for an English queen. Its frontispiece shows Emma enthroned, receiving the book from its author, with her two sons peering in from the side.

It did not save her from her son Edward, who resented his mother's preference for his half-brother. In 1043, soon after his coronation, he rode to Winchester and confiscated her treasure. She died in 1052, still rich enough to be buried with honour in Winchester, and her marriage to Æthelred had one great unintended consequence: it gave her great-nephew, William of Normandy, a family link to the English throne that he would later exploit.

### Edith and the women of 1066

Edward the Confessor married Edith, daughter of the overmighty Earl Godwin, in 1045. It was a political match: Edward needed the Godwins, and the Godwins wanted a grandson on the throne. No child came. When Edward quarrelled with the Godwins in 1051 and drove them into exile, he sent his wife to a nunnery; when the Godwins returned in force the following year, she came back too. Edith sat by Edward's deathbed in January 1066 and commissioned a *Life of King Edward* that helped turn him into the saint of later legend. Her brother Harold became king.

Harold himself had two partners. His official queen was Ealdgyth, widow of a Welsh king, married for politics shortly before 1066. But for twenty years he had lived with Edith Swan-neck, his wife in a customary form of marriage not blessed by the Church, with whom he had several children. According to a tradition recorded at Waltham Abbey, where Harold was buried, it was Edith Swan-neck who was brought to the battlefield of Hastings and identified his mutilated body by marks known only to her.

### Matilda of Flanders: the conqueror's equal

William the Conqueror's wife, Matilda of Flanders, was the daughter of the Count of Flanders and a granddaughter of the King of France, which made her a far grander catch than the illegitimate Duke of Normandy. A later legend claimed that she initially refused him, and that he responded by riding to Bruges and dragging her around by her plaits until she changed her mind. The story first appears long after the event and should be treated with suspicion, though it says something about William's reputation. The Pope opposed the match, possibly because the couple were related, and the two later founded a pair of abbeys in Caen, one each, by way of penance.

Their marriage was, by the standards of the time, an unusually successful partnership. Matilda bore at least nine children, governed Normandy as regent while William was conquering England, and was crowned queen at Westminster in 1068, with a ceremony that stressed she shared in royal power. Her only serious clash with her husband came when she secretly sent money to their eldest son, Robert, who had rebelled against his father. She died in 1083 and was buried in her abbey in Caen. A persistent myth holds that she was barely 1.27 metres (4 feet 2 inches) tall; when her bones were examined in the twentieth century, they suggested she was a perfectly ordinary height for the period, about 1.5 metres.

### Two Matildas and a Margaret

Across the border, Malcolm III of Scotland married Margaret, an English princess of the old West Saxon royal line who had fled north after the Conquest. Margaret was famously pious; she reformed the customs of the Scottish Church, fed the poor at her own table, and paid for a ferry across the Firth of Forth for pilgrims, which is why the towns on either side are called North and South Queensferry. She died in 1093, days after her husband and eldest son were killed fighting the English, and was made a saint in 1250. Her small chapel in Edinburgh Castle is the oldest surviving building in the city.

![Dunfermline Abbey](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/margaret-scotland.jpg)

*Dunfermline Abbey in Fife. Margaret and Malcolm III founded a priory church here, and she was buried in it; the medieval nave still stands beside the nineteenth-century church.* Photo: conner395, [CC BY 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/91779914@N00/8659192706), via Flickr.

Her daughter, Edith, renamed Matilda, married Henry I of England in 1100. The match was a stroke of genius on Henry's part: it married the new Norman dynasty to the blood of Alfred the Great. Matilda served as regent when Henry was in Normandy, sat in on his councils, founded a leper hospital at St Giles in the Fields in London, and was remembered by the English as "Good Queen Maud". Every English monarch from her grandson Henry II onwards is descended from her, and through her from the Anglo-Saxon kings.

The third Matilda, Matilda of Boulogne, wife of King Stephen, deserves a medal. When Stephen was captured at the Battle of Lincoln in 1141, it was his wife who kept his cause alive. She raised an army, besieged the Empress Matilda's forces at Winchester, captured her chief general, Robert of Gloucester, and exchanged him for her husband. Rarely has a consort so directly saved a king's throne.

## The Plantagenet Queens: Duchesses, She-Wolves and Saints (1154 to 1399)

### Eleanor of Aquitaine: the most remarkable queen of the Middle Ages

If medieval queens had a hall of fame, Eleanor of Aquitaine would have the biggest statue. She inherited the Duchy of Aquitaine, a great swathe of south-western France richer than the lands of the French king, as a teenager in 1137, and was promptly married to the heir to the French throne, who became Louis VII within weeks. She went with him on the Second Crusade, quarrelled with him, gave him two daughters but no son, and in 1152 the marriage was annulled.

Eight weeks later she married Henry, Count of Anjou and Duke of Normandy, who was about eleven years younger than her. Two years later he was Henry II of England, and Eleanor was queen of a second kingdom. She brought him Aquitaine, the core of his vast continental empire, and bore him five sons and three daughters, including two future kings, Richard and John. She governed in his absence and kept a glittering court at Poitiers.

Her power over Henry ended in 1173, when she backed her sons' rebellion against their father, apparently furious at his refusal to give them real power and, possibly, at his mistresses. She was caught trying to reach her son's camp disguised as a man, and Henry kept her under guard, mostly in England, for some fifteen years. His death in 1189 set her free, and her second career began in her late sixties. With Richard away on crusade, she effectively ran the government. When he was captured on his way home, she raised his enormous ransom and travelled to Germany to deliver it in person. In 1200, in her late seventies, she crossed the Pyrenees to Castile to collect her granddaughter, Blanche, and bring her back as a bride for the French heir; Blanche became the mother of St Louis. Eleanor died in 1204 and was buried at the abbey of Fontevraud, alongside Henry and Richard. She had been queen of France, queen of England and mother of two kings, and had outlived almost all her children.

### Berengaria, Isabella and Eleanor of Provence

Richard I married Berengaria of Navarre in 1191 in Limassol, on Cyprus, on his way to the Holy Land. She went with him on crusade, saw very little of him thereafter, and had no children. She is often said to be the only English queen never to set foot in England, though it is possible she visited after Richard's death. She spent her long widowhood in Le Mans, where she founded an abbey, struggling for years to get her widow's pension paid by her brother-in-law.

That brother-in-law, King John, set the tone for his reign with his marriage. In 1200 he married Isabella of Angoulême, then about twelve years old and already engaged to a powerful French lord, Hugh de Lusignan. The jilted Lusignans appealed to the King of France, who used their complaint as grounds for declaring John's French lands forfeit, and in the war that followed John lost Normandy. Isabella had little political influence while John lived. After his death she returned to France and in 1220 married Hugh X de Lusignan, the son of the man she had been engaged to as a child. She had nine children by him, and sent several to England, where their uncle Henry III loaded them with lands and offices, to the fury of the English barons.

Henry III's own queen, Eleanor of Provence, was clever, cultured, fiercely loyal to her husband and deeply unpopular, chiefly because of the many relatives from Savoy and Provence who followed her to England and were rewarded with English wealth. In 1263, when she tried to travel by barge under London Bridge, Londoners pelted her with stones, mud and rotten eggs, shouting insults, and she had to turn back. During the Barons' War she went to France and raised a fleet and an army to rescue her husband, though storms kept it from sailing. After Henry's death she became a nun at Amesbury, where she died in 1291.

### Eleanor of Castile: the queen of the crosses

Edward I married Eleanor of Castile when he was fifteen and she was about thirteen. It became one of the great royal love matches. She went with him almost everywhere, including on crusade to Acre, where, according to a later legend with no contemporary support, she sucked the poison from a wound inflicted by an assassin's dagger. She bore him at least fourteen children and probably more, though most died young; their only son to survive became Edward II.

Eleanor was not universally loved. She built up a huge personal estate, often by acquiring land from Christian landowners indebted to Jewish moneylenders, and contemporaries regarded her as grasping; even the Archbishop of Canterbury warned her about it. But when she died in November 1290 at Harby, near Lincoln, Edward was devastated. He ordered a stone cross to be raised at each of the twelve places where her funeral procession rested overnight on its way to Westminster. Three of the originals survive, at Geddington, Hardingstone near Northampton, and Waltham Cross. The one at Charing Cross in London, outside the railway station, is a Victorian reconstruction, but it is why the area has its name.

### Isabella of France: the she-wolf

Isabella, daughter of King Philip IV of France, married Edward II in 1308 when she was about twelve. At their wedding feast, Edward reportedly paid more attention to his favourite, Piers Gaveston, than to his bride, and gave Gaveston some of the jewels that had been her wedding presents. For more than a decade, Isabella was a loyal and effective queen, who bore Edward four children and acted as a peacemaker between the king and his angry barons.

What broke her was Edward's new favourite, Hugh Despenser the Younger, who seized her estates and, she believed, came between her and her husband. In 1325 she went to France to negotiate a treaty, and refused to come back. At the French court she began a relationship with Roger Mortimer, an exiled English baron, and in 1326 the two invaded England with a small army. Edward's regime collapsed. The Despensers were executed, Edward was deposed in favour of their fourteen-year-old son, and in September 1327 he died in Berkeley Castle, almost certainly murdered.

For three years Isabella and Mortimer ruled England, and enriched themselves so blatantly that they soon became as unpopular as the Despensers. In 1330 her son, Edward III, seized power in a coup at Nottingham Castle and had Mortimer hanged. Isabella was not punished. She was retired, with a generous income, and lived comfortably, much of the time at Castle Rising in Norfolk, until her death in 1358. She was buried in the Franciscan church in London, reportedly with her husband's heart. The poet Thomas Gray gave her the nickname by which she is still known, "the she-wolf of France", some four centuries after her death. Her legacy was far bigger than her scandal: as the daughter of a French king, she gave her son a claim to the French throne, which led to the Hundred Years' War.

### Philippa of Hainault and Anne of Bohemia: the good queens

The next two queens were the kind that chroniclers liked. Philippa of Hainault married Edward III in 1328, bore him at least twelve children, and was by every account a kind and steady presence in a warlike court. Her most famous moment came in 1347, after the year-long siege of Calais. According to the chronicler Jean Froissart, Edward intended to execute six leading citizens who had surrendered the keys to the city, and Philippa, heavily pregnant, knelt before him and begged for their lives, which he granted. The scene inspired Auguste Rodin's sculpture *The Burghers of Calais*, a cast of which stands in Victoria Tower Gardens beside the Houses of Parliament. Her chaplain founded The Queen's College, Oxford, in 1341, and named it in her honour.

![Rodin's The Burghers of Calais](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/philippa.jpg)

*Auguste Rodin's The Burghers of Calais, commemorating the six citizens whose lives Philippa of Hainault is said to have saved.* Photo: Kate C Hopkins, [CC BY 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/54151899@N00/4757528908), via Flickr.

Anne of Bohemia, daughter of the Holy Roman Emperor, married Richard II in 1382. She was popular, and was credited with interceding for rebels and for the citizens of London when they fell out with the king. Richard adored her, and when she died of plague at Sheen Palace in 1394, aged 28, he was so grief-stricken that he ordered the palace demolished. They had no children, which mattered: Richard's lack of an heir helped make his deposition possible. Their joint tomb in Westminster Abbey was designed to show them holding hands.

His second wife, Isabella of Valois, was six years old at their wedding in 1396. After his deposition, she was sent home to France.

## Lancaster and York: Witches, Warriors and Widows (1399 to 1503)

### Joan of Navarre and Catherine of Valois

Henry IV's second wife, Joan of Navarre, the widowed Duchess of Brittany, had a peaceful time as queen. Her real drama came after his death. In 1419 her stepson, Henry V, short of money for his French wars, had her arrested on charges of using witchcraft to try to kill him. She was never tried, but her income and possessions were seized and she was kept in comfortable confinement for nearly three years, until Henry released her shortly before he died, apparently troubled by his conscience. The arrangement had saved the crown a great deal of money.

Henry V's own wife, Catherine of Valois, was the daughter of the French king, and their marriage in 1420 was the centrepiece of the Treaty of Troyes, which made Henry heir to France. Two years later Henry was dead and Catherine, at twenty, was the mother of the infant Henry VI. Parliament, worried about whom she might marry, passed a statute restricting the remarriage of queens dowager. Catherine got round it by secretly marrying, or at least living with, a Welsh squire of her household called Owen Tudor, with whom she had several children. She died in 1437. Their son Edmund Tudor was the father of Henry VII. The Tudor dynasty, in other words, began with a queen's secret love affair.

Catherine had one more strange afterlife. During rebuilding work at Westminster Abbey in the early sixteenth century her embalmed body was taken out of its tomb, and for more than two centuries it was left on display, in an open coffin, to anybody who tipped the vergers. On 23 February 1669, his 36th birthday, the diarist Samuel Pepys visited the Abbey and, by his own account, held up her upper body in his hands and kissed her on the mouth, reflecting that he had kissed a queen. She was finally reburied in the nineteenth century.

### Margaret of Anjou: the queen who went to war

Margaret of Anjou, a niece of the French queen, was fifteen when she married Henry VI in 1445, as part of a truce in which England secretly agreed to hand over the county of Maine. That was the first of many things for which the English blamed her. Henry was gentle, devout and incapable, and when he collapsed into mental illness in 1453, it was Margaret, not her husband, who became the real leader of the Lancastrian cause. She even asked parliament, unsuccessfully, to make her regent.

![Margaret of Anjou in stained glass at Mucklestone](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/margaret-anjou.jpg)

*Margaret of Anjou in a window at St Mary's church, Mucklestone, in Staffordshire, near the battlefield of Blore Heath (1459). Local tradition says she watched the battle from the church tower.* Photo: sps1955, [CC BY 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/40878011@N07/19898565668), via Flickr.

She was determined to protect the rights of her son, Edward, born in 1453, and for nearly twenty years she fought for them. She raised armies, negotiated with the Scots and the French, and was blamed by Yorkist propaganda for every Lancastrian atrocity. After the Lancastrian victory at Wakefield in 1460, the head of her enemy, Richard, Duke of York, was set on the gates of York wearing a paper crown. In 1470 she made an astonishing alliance with her former enemy, the Earl of Warwick, sealed by marrying her son to Warwick's daughter. It ended in disaster. In May 1471 her son was killed at Tewkesbury, her husband died in the Tower, and Margaret was captured. Louis XI of France ransomed her in 1475, and she died in poverty in Anjou in 1482. Shakespeare made her a vengeful prophetess. Her more peaceful legacy is Queens' College, Cambridge, which she founded in 1448. The apostrophe comes after the "s" because another queen refounded it: her Yorkist rival.

### Elizabeth Woodville: the widow who married a king

That rival was Elizabeth Woodville, the widow of a Lancastrian knight killed in battle, who married the young Yorkist king Edward IV in secret on 1 May 1464. According to later stories, she first met him while waiting under an oak tree to petition him about her sons' inheritance. The marriage was a political bombshell. Edward's chief adviser, the Earl of Warwick, had been negotiating a French royal bride for him and was humiliated, and the nobility were outraged that the king had married a commoner, by royal standards, with five brothers and seven sisters, all of whom needed good marriages. They got them. Her twenty-year-old brother John married the elderly and very wealthy Dowager Duchess of Norfolk, a match a chronicler called "diabolical".

![Portrait of Elizabeth Woodville](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/elizabeth-woodville.jpg)

*Elizabeth Woodville, in a portrait painted after her death. The Latin calls her the wife of Edward IV.* Image: lisby1, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/60861613@N00/3393191670), via Flickr.

The Woodvilles' rise helped drive Warwick into rebellion. In 1470, when Edward fled abroad, Elizabeth took refuge in sanctuary at Westminster Abbey, where she gave birth to her son, the future Edward V. After Edward IV's sudden death in 1483 she fled into sanctuary again, with her younger children, as her brother-in-law Richard of Gloucester seized power. Her marriage was declared invalid, her children illegitimate, her brother and a son by her first marriage were executed, and her two sons by Edward vanished in the Tower.

Yet Elizabeth survived. She plotted with Henry Tudor's mother, Margaret Beaufort, to marry her daughter to Henry, and after Bosworth the plan succeeded. She retired to Bermondsey Abbey in 1487, possibly under pressure from her son-in-law, and died there in 1492. Through her daughter, every monarch since Henry VIII is her descendant.

### Anne Neville and Elizabeth of York

Anne Neville, daughter of Warwick the Kingmaker, had one of the strangest careers of any English queen. She was first married, aged fourteen, to Margaret of Anjou's son, Edward of Lancaster, as part of her father's alliance with Margaret; he was killed at Tewkesbury months later. She then married Richard, Duke of Gloucester, who became Richard III. Their only son died in 1484, and Anne died in March 1485, probably of tuberculosis. Rumours spread that Richard had poisoned her to marry his niece, Elizabeth of York, and he took the extraordinary step of publicly denying it.

Elizabeth of York did marry a king, but not Richard. In 1486 she married Henry VII, uniting the houses of Lancaster and York. She was popular, gentle and generous, and the marriage seems to have been happy. She was the daughter, sister, niece and wife of English kings, and the mother of Henry VIII, but she never held real power herself. Her mother-in-law, the formidable Margaret Beaufort, dominated the royal household. Elizabeth died on her 37th birthday, 11 February 1503, a few days after giving birth to a daughter who also died; Henry, by all accounts, was grief-stricken.

## Divorced, Beheaded, Died: The Six Wives of Henry VIII

No English king did more to make the position of queen dangerous than Henry VIII, and no group of consorts is more famous than his wives. Between them, they show every source of a consort's power, and how quickly each could be lost.

### Catherine of Aragon: the queen who would not go quietly

Catherine, daughter of Ferdinand and Isabella of Spain, came to England in 1501 to marry Henry VII's eldest son, Arthur, who died five months later. She spent seven miserable, impoverished years as a widow in limbo before marrying Arthur's younger brother, Henry VIII, in 1509, weeks after he became king. She was clever, well educated, devout and popular, and in 1513, when Henry was fighting in France, she served as regent and organised the defence of the north against the Scots. After the English victory at Flodden, in which James IV was killed, she sent Henry a piece of the Scottish king's bloodstained coat.

![The tomb of Catherine of Aragon in Peterborough Cathedral](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/catherine-aragon.jpg)

*Catherine of Aragon's grave in Peterborough Cathedral, where visitors still leave flowers and pomegranates, her emblem. The banner above calls her what she always insisted she was: Queen of England.* Photo: Ben Sutherland, [CC BY 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/60179301@N00/4752909769), via Flickr.

Her problem was children. She had at least six pregnancies, but only one child survived infancy: a daughter, Mary, born in 1516. By the mid-1520s Henry, desperate for a son and in love with Anne Boleyn, had convinced himself that his marriage to his brother's widow was cursed. Catherine refused to agree to an annulment. At a church court at Blackfriars in 1529 she knelt before Henry and appealed to him directly, declaring that she had come to him a virgin, before walking out and refusing to return. She never accepted that she was not his wife or queen. She was separated from her daughter, moved from one damp house to another, and died at Kimbolton Castle in January 1536. Henry, it is said, celebrated by wearing yellow.

### Anne Boleyn: the queen who changed the religion

Anne Boleyn, who had spent her formative years at the sophisticated courts of the Netherlands and France, did something no previous royal mistress had done: she refused to sleep with the king unless he married her. She held out for some six years, and in that time she helped bring about one of the biggest changes in English history. She was sympathetic to religious reform, and introduced Henry to radical books, including William Tyndale's *The Obedience of a Christian Man*, which argued that kings, not popes, were answerable for the Church in their realms. Henry is said to have declared that it was a book for him and all kings to read.

In January 1533, pregnant, she secretly married Henry; the break with Rome followed, and she was crowned that June. But her daughter, Elizabeth, born in September 1533, was not the promised son, and two later pregnancies failed. Anne was sharp-tongued and quick-tempered, and had many enemies. In May 1536 she was arrested and charged with adultery with five men, one of them her own brother, and with plotting the king's death. The charges are almost universally regarded by historians as false. She was beheaded at the Tower of London on 19 May 1536 by a swordsman specially brought from Calais. Her legacy is enormous: the English Reformation, and Elizabeth I.

### Jane, Anne, Catherine and Catherine

Henry was engaged to Jane Seymour the day after Anne's execution and married her eleven days later. Jane, quiet and conservative, took as her motto "Bound to obey and serve". She is believed to have tried, cautiously, to reconcile Henry with his daughter Mary. On 12 October 1537 she gave birth to the longed-for son, Edward, and died twelve days later. She was the only one of his wives to be given a queen's funeral, and he was buried beside her at Windsor.

![Portrait of Jane Seymour after Holbein](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/jane-seymour.jpg)

*Jane Seymour after Hans Holbein the Younger. She gave Henry the son he wanted, and died doing so.* Image: lisby1, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/60861613@N00/3329976080), via Flickr.

Anne of Cleves, a German princess, was chosen in 1539 for diplomatic reasons, partly on the strength of a flattering portrait by Holbein. When Henry first met her, disguised, at Rochester, she failed to recognise him and he took offence. He married her in January 1540, complained that he could not consummate the marriage, and had it annulled in July. Anne agreed to everything, and was rewarded with a generous income, several houses, including Hever Castle, and the honorary status of "the king's beloved sister". She stayed in England, got on well with Henry and his children, and outlived all his other wives, dying in 1557. Of the six, she was arguably the one who played her hand best. The insult "Flanders mare" is often attributed to Henry, but it first appears in print more than a century later.

![Anne of Cleves by Hans Holbein](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/anne-cleves.jpg)

*Hans Holbein's portrait of Anne of Cleves, painted in 1539 to help Henry choose a bride. Holbein's reputation survived the marriage.* Image: lisby1, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/60861613@N00/3334339192), via Flickr.

On the very day in July 1540 that Henry's minister Thomas Cromwell, who had arranged the Cleves marriage, was executed, Henry married Catherine Howard, a teenage cousin of Anne Boleyn. She was lively and flirtatious, and Henry, by now obese and in pain from an ulcerated leg, called her his "rose without a thorn". But details emerged of her sexual relationships before her marriage and of secret meetings with a courtier, Thomas Culpeper, after it. She was beheaded in February 1542, still probably no more than about nineteen.

His last wife, Catherine Parr, a twice-widowed, intelligent and devout woman in her early thirties, married him in 1543. She was regent while he campaigned in France in 1544, helped reconcile him with his daughters, supervised the education of the younger children, and published devotional books in English under her own name, one of the first Englishwomen to do so. Her Protestant views nearly destroyed her in 1546, when religious conservatives persuaded Henry to sign a warrant for her arrest. Catherine, warned in time, went to Henry and told him she had only argued about religion to distract him from his pain and learn from his wisdom. He forgave her. When the Lord Chancellor arrived to arrest her the next day, Henry sent him away with a stream of abuse. After Henry's death she married her old love, Thomas Seymour, and died in 1548 after giving birth to a daughter.

## Husbands and Non-Husbands: When the Monarch Was a Woman

When the monarch was a woman, the problem of the consort was turned on its head. In a world where wives were legally subordinate to husbands, would a queen's husband become king?

### Philip of Spain: the king who was not quite a king

Mary I answered that question in 1554 by marrying Philip, son and heir of the Emperor Charles V, in Winchester Cathedral. Parliament, alarmed at the prospect of England becoming a Spanish province, passed an act which gave Philip the title of king but strictly limited his powers: he could not appoint foreigners to English offices, take the queen or her children abroad, or drag England into his wars. Coins were struck showing the two of them face to face. Mary loved him; he found her dull and spent most of the marriage abroad. He did, despite the terms of the treaty, involve England in a war with France, in which England lost Calais. Mary died childless in 1558, and Philip proposed to her sister, Elizabeth. She declined. Thirty years later he sent the Armada.

### Elizabeth I: the art of not marrying

Elizabeth never had a consort, and that was the point. She understood that a husband would either be a foreigner, who might subordinate England to his own country, or an Englishman, who would raise one faction above all the others. Her great love was Robert Dudley, later Earl of Leicester. In 1560 his wife, Amy Robsart, was found dead at the foot of a staircase at Cumnor Place, near Oxford, and although an inquest found it an accident, the scandal made a marriage to Dudley impossible. Elizabeth kept him close until his death in 1588, and when she died, a note from him was found in a box by her bed, on which she had written "his last letter".

### Mary, Queen of Scots, and her three husbands

Elizabeth's cousin, Mary, Queen of Scots, showed what could go wrong. Her first husband was Francis II of France, whom she married in 1558, and who died in 1560 after a reign of seventeen months. Back in Scotland, she married her cousin Henry Stewart, Lord Darnley, in 1565: tall, handsome, vain and violent. Darnley wanted the "crown matrimonial", which would have made him king in his own right, and Mary refused. In March 1566 he and a gang of conspirators burst into her supper room at Holyrood and stabbed her secretary, David Rizzio, to death in front of her while she was six months pregnant. In February 1567 the house at Kirk o' Field in Edinburgh where Darnley was staying was blown up with gunpowder, and he was found dead in the garden, apparently strangled. Three months later Mary married the man most people believed was responsible, James Hepburn, Earl of Bothwell. Scotland rebelled. Mary was forced to abdicate, Bothwell fled to Scandinavia, where he died insane in a Danish prison in 1578, and Mary fled to England, captivity and, eventually, the executioner's block.

### William III and George of Denmark

When parliament offered the crown to Mary II and her husband William of Orange in 1689, William made it clear that he would not be a mere consort. According to Bishop Gilbert Burnet, he said he would not be his wife's gentleman usher. He got his way, and they reigned jointly, the only true co-monarchs in British history.

Queen Anne's husband, Prince George of Denmark, was the opposite: the most self-effacing royal husband imaginable. Charles II is reported to have said of him, "I have tried him drunk and I have tried him sober, and there is nothing in him." Whenever news of another defection reached James II's court in 1688, George would exclaim "*Est-il possible?*", "Is it possible?"; when George himself deserted to William, James is said to have remarked, "So, *Est-il possible* is gone too." He was never made king, but Anne made him Lord High Admiral, and the marriage was devoted. She was pregnant at least seventeen times, and he nursed her through it all. When he died in 1708, she was distraught. Some consorts change history. Others simply make a lonely monarch less lonely.

*A statue of Prince George of Denmark, in Roman dress, on the Guildhall at Windsor.* Photo: ell brown, [CC BY 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/39415781@N06/3782087469), via Flickr.

## The Stuart Queens: Masques, Wars and Cups of Tea (1503 to 1714)

### Margaret Tudor: the marriage that made Great Britain

The marriage that eventually united the crowns of England and Scotland took place in 1503, when Henry VII's daughter Margaret Tudor married James IV of Scotland. Poets called it the union of the thistle and the rose. After James was killed at Flodden in 1513, Margaret served briefly as regent for her infant son, James V, before a disastrous second marriage cost her the regency. A century after her wedding, her great-grandson James VI inherited the English throne through her. No English queen consort had a greater long-term effect on the map of the British Isles, and she was not even an English queen.

### Anne of Denmark: a winter wedding in Oslo

James VI's own marriage began with a voyage. In 1589 his bride, Anne of Denmark, set sail for Scotland, but storms forced her ships to shelter in Norway, then part of the Danish kingdom. James, in a fit of romantic impatience most unlike him, sailed across the North Sea to fetch her. They were married in Oslo in November 1589 and spent the winter in Denmark. The storms were blamed on witchcraft, and the episode helped trigger the North Berwick witch trials in Scotland, in which James took a horrible personal interest.

![Portrait of Anne of Denmark](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/anne-denmark.jpg)

*Anne of Denmark, who married James VI in Oslo in 1589 and became the first queen consort of both Scotland and England.* Image: lisby1, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/60861613@N00/3791579399), via Flickr.

As queen of England from 1603, Anne was a great patron of the arts. She commissioned masques, lavish court entertainments written by Ben Jonson and designed by Inigo Jones, in which she and her ladies performed, scandalising some observers. She commissioned Jones to build the Queen's House at Greenwich, the first classical building in England, though she died in 1619 before it was finished. She and James lived largely separate lives, and she seems quietly to have converted to Catholicism. Her political influence was limited, but her children included Charles I and Elizabeth of Bohemia, through whom the Hanoverians would later inherit the throne.

### Henrietta Maria: the queen generalissima

Charles I's wife, Henrietta Maria, a daughter of Henry IV of France, was fifteen and a devout Catholic when she married him in 1625, and she refused to take part in a Protestant coronation. The marriage started badly, with Charles sending most of her French household home, but became one of the closest royal partnerships of the age. That was part of the problem. Many English Protestants believed she was leading the king towards Catholicism, and her open practice of her faith, with her own chapels and priests, fed the fears that led to civil war. The colony of Maryland, founded in 1632, was named after her.

![Portrait of Henrietta Maria](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/henrietta-maria.jpg)

*Henrietta Maria, whose faith and politics helped bring her husband's enemies together.* Image: Alexander Hatley, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/109137992@N02/55039992458), via Flickr.

When war came, she was a genuine political and military force. In 1642 she went to the Netherlands with some of the crown jewels and pawned them to buy weapons. In 1643 she landed at Bridlington with arms and troops, calling herself "her she-majesty generalissima", and led them to join the king. Parliament impeached her for treason. In 1644, pregnant and ill, she fled to France, and she never saw her husband again. After his execution she lived in poverty in Paris for a time, before returning to England as queen mother after the Restoration. She died in France in 1669.

### Catherine of Braganza and Mary of Modena

Catherine of Braganza, a Portuguese princess, married Charles II in 1662, bringing an enormous dowry that included the ports of Tangier and Bombay, which became the foundation of British power in western India. She is often credited with making tea drinking fashionable at the English court. Her husband's many mistresses humiliated her, and she was forced to accept one of them, Lady Castlemaine, as a lady of her bedchamber. She had no children, and politicians repeatedly urged Charles to divorce her. He refused, and when the conspiracy-monger Titus Oates accused her of plotting to poison the king in 1678, Charles defended her. After his death she returned to Portugal, where she served as regent for her brother.

![Engraving of Catherine of Braganza](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/catherine-braganza.jpg)

*Catherine of Braganza, whose dowry brought Bombay under the English crown.* Image: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, [CC0](https://www.rawpixel.com/image/8207162/catherine-braganza), via Rawpixel.

Mary of Modena, an Italian Catholic princess, was fifteen when she married the future James II in 1673. In June 1688, after many pregnancies but no surviving son, she gave birth to a boy. Protestant propaganda claimed the baby had been smuggled into the birth chamber in a warming pan, a ludicrous story that was nevertheless widely believed and helped justify the Glorious Revolution. That December she escaped to France with the baby, disguised as a laundress, and she spent the rest of her life in exile at Saint-Germain, near Paris. Her son, the "Old Pretender", spent his life trying to win back the throne.

## The Hanoverian Queens: Scholars, Scandals and a Very Long Engagement (1714 to 1861)

### Caroline of Ansbach: the power behind the throne

George I never brought a queen to England: his wife, Sophia Dorothea, spent the last thirty-two years of her life confined in a German castle after an affair. His son's wife was a very different proposition. Caroline of Ansbach, who married the future George II in 1705, was one of the cleverest women ever to be queen of Britain. She corresponded with the philosopher Gottfried Leibniz, took a keen interest in science and theology, and was a skilled politician.

![The Queen Caroline monument at Stowe](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/caroline-ansbach.jpg)

*The Queen Caroline monument in the landscape gardens at Stowe, Buckinghamshire.* Photo: Graham Horn, [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/2588056), via Geographorguk.

She also championed medicine. Smallpox inoculation, the practice of deliberately infecting someone with a mild case to give them immunity, had been brought to Britain from Constantinople by Lady Mary Wortley Montagu. In 1721 Caroline backed an experiment in which condemned prisoners at Newgate were inoculated in exchange for their freedom; they survived, and in 1722 she had two of her own daughters inoculated, which did a great deal to make the practice respectable.

Politically, Caroline was the real power behind George II. She formed a close alliance with the prime minister, Robert Walpole, who reportedly said that he had taken "the right sow by the ear", and she steered the king towards Walpole's policies while letting George believe they were his own. A popular rhyme mocked the king: "You may strut, dapper George, but 'twill all be in vain; We know 'tis Queen Caroline, not you, that reign." She acted as regent during his frequent trips to Hanover, and she reshaped Kensington Gardens, including the creation of the Serpentine. When she was dying in 1737, she urged George to remarry. Weeping, he replied that he would not: he would have mistresses instead. He never did remarry, and when he died in 1760 he was buried beside her, with the sides of their coffins removed so that their remains could mingle.

### Charlotte of Mecklenburg-Strelitz: the queen of botany

George III chose his bride from a list of German Protestant princesses, and married Charlotte of Mecklenburg-Strelitz in September 1761 on the day they first met. She was seventeen. The marriage was a happy one, and produced fifteen children, thirteen of whom survived to adulthood. George bought Buckingham House as a private home for her, and it became known as the Queen's House before it grew into Buckingham Palace.

![Strelitzia reginae, the bird of paradise flower](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/charlotte.jpg)

*Strelitzia reginae, the bird of paradise flower, in a botanical plate published between 1805 and 1816, during Queen Charlotte's lifetime. The plant was named in her honour; she was born a princess of Mecklenburg-Strelitz.* Image: Swallowtail Garden Seeds, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/97123293@N07/16558820423), via Flickr.

Charlotte was a keen amateur botanist who helped to develop the gardens at Kew. The South African bird of paradise flower, *Strelitzia reginae*, was named in her honour. She was a patron of music; the eight-year-old Mozart dedicated a set of sonatas to her in 1765. She is also credited with setting up one of the first Christmas trees in England, at Windsor in 1800. The city of Charlotte in North Carolina was named after her. Her later years were overshadowed by her husband's mental illness. Under the Regency Act of 1811, she was given custody of the king's person, a heavy and miserable responsibility. She died in 1818, two years before him.

### Caroline of Brunswick: the queen who was locked out

The marriage of the future George IV to his cousin Caroline of Brunswick in 1795 is perhaps the worst in royal history. George was already secretly married to Maria Fitzherbert, a Catholic widow, though that marriage was invalid under the Royal Marriages Act of 1772. He agreed to marry Caroline only because parliament promised to pay his enormous debts. When he first saw her, according to the diplomat who brought her to England, he turned away and called for a glass of brandy. He was reportedly drunk at the wedding. They lived together long enough to conceive a daughter, Princess Charlotte, born in 1796, and then separated.

Caroline lived an increasingly unconventional life, much of it travelling in Europe, while George had her investigated for adultery. When he became king in 1820 she returned to claim her place as queen. George had his government introduce a bill into the House of Lords to strip her of her title and dissolve the marriage. The public, who hated George, took Caroline's side with riotous enthusiasm, and the bill was dropped. At the coronation in July 1821 she was turned away from the doors of Westminster Abbey. She died three weeks later. Her only legacy of lasting importance came through her daughter, Charlotte, the heir to the throne, whose death in childbirth in 1817 set off a scramble among George's middle-aged brothers to marry and produce an heir.

### Adelaide: a queen and a city

One of those brothers was William, Duke of Clarence, who married Adelaide of Saxe-Meiningen in 1818. He was 52 and had ten illegitimate children with the actress Dorothea Jordan; she was 25. The marriage was, unexpectedly, happy. Adelaide was kind to William's illegitimate children and to her niece, the young Victoria. Her own two daughters died in infancy, and her later pregnancies ended in miscarriage or stillbirth. As queen from 1830, she was thought to oppose the Great Reform Act, which made her briefly unpopular, but she was later much admired for her charity and her dignified widowhood. The city of Adelaide in South Australia, founded in 1836, was named after her.

### Prince Albert: the husband who did the job

Victoria, as reigning queen, had to propose to her own husband, which she did in October 1839. Her cousin, Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, was handsome, earnest and very well educated, and she fell head over heels in love. The British establishment was less impressed. Parliament cut the allowance proposed for him from £50,000 a year to £30,000, refused to give him a peerage, and argued about his precedence. He was not formally styled Prince Consort until 1857.

![The Royal Albert Hall](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/albert.jpg)

*The Royal Albert Hall, opened in 1871 and named by Victoria in memory of her husband, on land bought with the profits of his Great Exhibition.* Photo: Loco Steve, [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/36989019@N08/9582656496), via Flickr.

Albert's power over Victoria grew steadily. At first she excluded him from state business. Within a few years, and especially during her pregnancies (there were nine), he had become her private secretary and closest political adviser, reading state papers, drafting her letters and attending her meetings with ministers. He reorganised the royal household and finances, which had been chaotic, and he pushed the monarchy towards political neutrality, so that it would be above party. His great public project was the Great Exhibition of 1851, whose profits paid for the museums and colleges of South Kensington. He promoted science, education and public health, and helped popularise the German custom of the decorated Christmas tree when the *Illustrated London News* published a picture of the royal family around one in 1848.

His last important act came in late 1861, during the *Trent* affair, when Britain and the United States came close to war after a Union warship seized two Confederate envoys from a British ship. Albert, already very ill, softened the wording of the government's message to Washington, giving the Americans room to back down, which they did. He died on 14 December 1861, aged 42. Victoria wore black for the rest of her life, had his clothes laid out each day, and filled the country with memorials, including the Royal Albert Hall and the Albert Memorial in Kensington Gardens. The obvious question about Albert is how much power he had. The answer is that, for nearly twenty years, he was in practice a joint head of the monarchy, and its modern, dutiful, politically neutral style was largely his creation.

## The Windsor Consorts: From Duty to Divorce, and Back (1863 to the Present)

### Alexandra of Denmark: beauty, deafness and patience

Edward VII's wife, Alexandra, a Danish princess, married him in 1863 and was hailed as the most beautiful princess in Europe. Her family had an extraordinary reach. Her father became King of Denmark, her brother King of Greece, her sister Empress of Russia, and her daughter Maud became Queen of Norway in 1905, when Maud's husband, Alexandra's nephew, was chosen as King Haakon VII.

![Princess Alexandra with her two eldest sons](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/alexandra.jpg)

*Alexandra, then Princess of Wales, with her two eldest sons: Prince Albert Victor, to whom Mary of Teck was first engaged, and the future George V, whom she married instead.* Photo: whatsthatpicture, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/24469639@N00/5462645556), via Flickr.

Alexandra suffered from hereditary deafness, which grew worse with age, and was left with a limp after a serious illness in 1867. Fashionable women copied that too, along with her high necklines and choker necklaces. She had to tolerate her husband's long list of mistresses, and did so with remarkable composure; when Edward was dying in 1910, she allowed his mistress, Alice Keppel, to visit him. Her political influence was limited, though she never forgave Prussia for its war against Denmark in 1864 and did not hide her views. Her lasting legacy is in charity: Alexandra Rose Day, which she launched in 1912 and which still raises money, and the military nursing service that still bears her name.

### Mary of Teck: the queen who held it together

Princess Mary of Teck was engaged in 1891 to Prince Albert Victor, the eldest son of the future Edward VII. Six weeks later he died of influenza, and in 1893 she married his younger brother, the future George V. It sounds unromantic, and to some extent it was, but the marriage was solid and devoted.

![Queen Mary](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/mary-teck.jpg)

*Queen Mary, who lived through six reigns and set the tone of royal duty for the twentieth century.* Photo: unknown photographer, [CC0](https://www.rawpixel.com/image/13964142/photo-image-face-person-crown), via Rawpixel.

Queen Mary was dignified, upright and intensely dutiful, and she did more than anyone except her husband to make the House of Windsor a symbol of family virtue and national service. She was a passionate collector of art and antiques, and acquired a reputation, partly deserved, for admiring objects in other people's houses so persistently that hosts felt obliged to give them to her. During the Second World War she stayed at Badminton House in Gloucestershire, where she led parties of staff in clearing the estate of ivy, which she detested. She was implacably opposed to her son Edward VIII's relationship with Wallis Simpson, and after the abdication she never received the Duchess of Windsor. She lived to see her granddaughter become queen, but died in March 1953, ten weeks before the coronation, having asked that it should not be postponed on her account.

### Wallis Simpson: the consort who never was

The woman for whom Edward VIII gave up the throne was never a consort at all. When Edward married Wallis Simpson in 1937, after the abdication, she became Duchess of Windsor, but George VI's government ruled that she was not entitled to be called "Her Royal Highness", a slight that the couple never forgave. She remains the most important royal partner who never sat in the other chair: her influence over Edward was so great that it cost him the crown, and changed the line of succession.

![Dining room at the Château de Candé](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/wallis.jpg)

*The dining room at the Château de Candé in France, where the Duke of Windsor and Wallis Simpson held their wedding meal on 3 June 1937. No member of his family attended.* Photo: DCHNwam, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/57179180@N07/52216438834), via Flickr.

### Elizabeth Bowes-Lyon: the queen who never stopped

Lady Elizabeth Bowes-Lyon, the daughter of a Scottish earl, turned down two proposals from George V's second son, Albert, Duke of York, before accepting the third in 1923. She was not of royal birth, and her marriage seemed to put her into the quieter side of royal life. Then her brother-in-law abdicated, and in 1936 she became queen.

![Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother in Brisbane, 1958](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/queen-mother.jpg)

*Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother at a state reception in Brisbane during her tour of Australia in February 1958.* Photo: Queensland State Archives, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/60455048@N02/5948385093), via Flickr.

She was the making of her shy, stammering husband, giving him confidence and support and helping him through the speech therapy that made his public role possible. During the Second World War she refused to send her daughters to safety in Canada, declaring, in a remark widely reported at the time, that the children would not leave without her, she would not leave without the king, and the king would never leave. When Buckingham Palace was bombed in 1940 she said she was glad, because now she could look the East End in the face. At her wedding she had laid her bouquet on the Tomb of the Unknown Warrior in Westminster Abbey, in memory of her brother killed in the First World War, and royal brides have followed her example ever since.

After the king's death in 1952 she became Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother, and for fifty years she remained among the most popular members of the royal family, famous for her hats, her horse racing and her gin. She never forgave the Duke and Duchess of Windsor, whom she blamed for placing a burden on her husband that she believed shortened his life. She died in 2002, aged 101.

### Prince Philip: the man two steps behind

Prince Philip of Greece and Denmark was born on Corfu in 1921. When he was eighteen months old, his family was forced into exile after a revolution in Greece, and he left the island, according to family tradition, in a cot made from a fruit box. He grew up in France, Germany and Britain, largely separated from his parents, joined the Royal Navy and had a distinguished war, being mentioned in dispatches. He gave up his Greek titles, took the surname Mountbatten, and married Princess Elizabeth on 20 November 1947, when he was created Duke of Edinburgh.

![Elizabeth II and Prince Philip](/assets/essays/behind-the-throne/images/philip.jpg)

*Elizabeth II seated in front of Prince Philip, in an official portrait now held by Library and Archives Canada. He was consort for 69 years, longer than anyone else in British history.* Photo: Library and Archives Canada, [CC BY 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/28853433@N02/7195938514), via Flickr.

His promising naval career ended when Elizabeth became queen in 1952. His position was awkward in the extreme: he was the husband of the sovereign in an age when husbands were expected to lead, and the role had almost no defined functions. When it was decided that the royal house would continue to be called Windsor, not Mountbatten, he reportedly complained that he was the only man in the country not allowed to give his name to his children. (In 1960 it was agreed that some of their descendants would use the surname Mountbatten-Windsor.)

He made the job his own. He modernised the running of the royal household, took a keen interest in science, technology and industry, was an early champion of conservation as president of the World Wildlife Fund in Britain, and founded the Duke of Edinburgh's Award in 1956, which has since been taken up by millions of young people around the world. He was famous for his blunt, sometimes offensive remarks, and for his unswerving support of his wife: the queen once described him as her "strength and stay". He retired from public duties in 2017, having carried out more than 22,000 solo engagements, and died on 9 April 2021, two months short of his hundredth birthday. He had been consort for 69 years, longer than anyone else in British history.

### Camilla: from villain to queen

Camilla Shand met Prince Charles in the early 1970s. They became close, but she married the army officer Andrew Parker Bowles in 1973, and Charles married Lady Diana Spencer in 1981. The collapse of the Wales marriage, Diana's popularity and her tragic death in 1997 made Camilla, for a time, the most unpopular woman in Britain, cast as the "other woman" in a royal soap opera watched around the world.

Her rehabilitation was slow and carefully managed. She and Charles married in a civil ceremony at the Windsor Guildhall in April 2005, and she took the title Duchess of Cornwall rather than Princess of Wales, out of sensitivity to Diana's memory. It was said at the time that she would be known as Princess Consort when Charles became king. But years of hard work for charities, especially in literacy and in support for victims of domestic abuse and sexual violence, steadily changed public opinion. In February 2022, in a message marking 70 years on the throne, Elizabeth II said it was her sincere wish that Camilla be known as Queen Consort when the time came. She was crowned alongside Charles on 6 May 2023, as Queen Camilla.

## The Scorecard: How Consorts Got There, and What Became of Them

Stepping back, some patterns emerge.

**How did they get there?** Overwhelmingly, through diplomacy. Most medieval and early modern consorts were foreign princesses, married to seal alliances: Matilda of Flanders, Eleanor of Aquitaine, Isabella of France, Catherine of Aragon, Anne of Denmark, Catherine of Braganza. Their marriages could bring enormous assets, such as Aquitaine, Bombay or a claim to the French throne, and enormous risks, like the suspicion that a Catholic queen would lead a Protestant king astray. The exceptions, the English-born commoners and gentry who married for love or lust, were often the most controversial: Elizabeth Woodville, Anne Boleyn, Jane Seymour, Catherine Howard and, in a different way, Wallis Simpson. Only in the twentieth century did marrying a British subject become normal and accepted, with Elizabeth Bowes-Lyon.

**What power did they have?** None in law, and a great deal in practice. The most powerful consorts were those who governed in their spouse's absence (Matilda of Flanders, Eleanor of Aquitaine, Catherine of Aragon, Catherine Parr, Caroline of Ansbach), or who effectively shared the job (Albert, and in a quieter way Philip). Several led political factions or armies: Matilda of Boulogne, Margaret of Anjou, Henrietta Maria. One, Isabella of France, overthrew her husband. And one, Anne Boleyn, helped change the national religion.

**How did their time end?** Mostly with widowhood or their own death in childbirth or illness. But the list of unhappy endings is long.

| Consort | How it ended |
|---|---|
| Emma of Normandy | Stripped of her treasure by her own son |
| Eleanor of Aquitaine | Imprisoned by her husband for fifteen years |
| Isabella of France | Deposed her husband; retired after her son's coup |
| Joan of Navarre | Accused of witchcraft by her stepson |
| Margaret of Anjou | Son killed, husband murdered, died in poverty |
| Elizabeth Woodville | Marriage declared invalid; sons vanished |
| Catherine of Aragon | Marriage annulled; died in exile from court |
| Anne Boleyn | Beheaded, 1536 |
| Catherine Howard | Beheaded, 1542 |
| Henrietta Maria | Impeached; fled abroad; husband executed |
| Caroline of Brunswick | Locked out of her own coronation |

**What did they leave behind?** Children, above all. The single most consequential acts of many consorts were the births of heirs, or the failure to produce them. Catherine of Aragon's failure to have a son led to the English Reformation; Mary of Modena's success led to the Glorious Revolution. But consorts also left colleges (Queen's College, Oxford, and Queens' College, Cambridge), buildings (the Queen's House at Greenwich, the Royal Albert Hall), place names (Maryland, Charlotte, Adelaide and Queensferry), customs (the Christmas tree, the bride's bouquet laid at the Tomb of the Unknown Warrior), charities and a great deal of art. And they left a long line of descendants. Every monarch since Henry II descends from Matilda of Scotland, and through her from Alfred the Great; every one since Henry VIII descends from Elizabeth Woodville; every one since James I descends from Margaret Tudor.

## Conclusion: The Weight of the Other Chair

Back at Westminster Abbey, the coffee break is over, and the tour guide is gathering her flock.

"So, who had the hardest job," asks the man in the rugby shirt, "the king or the queen?"

"Depends on the king," says the guide. "And on the queen. Henry VIII's wives would say the queen. Prince Philip might have said the same, though he'd have put it more colourfully."

"And who had the most power?"

She thinks about it. "Isabella of France deposed a king. Eleanor of Aquitaine ran an empire. Albert more or less ran the monarchy. But if you mean who changed things most, I'd say Anne Boleyn. Without her, no Church of England, no Elizabeth I. Not bad for a woman who was queen for three years."

The consort's position has always been a paradox. It carries no constitutional power, but it sits closer to the centre of power than any other. It depends wholly on a marriage, but marriages, especially royal ones, can fail, and when they did, the consort usually paid the price. For most of history, a queen consort's first duty was to produce an heir, and her standing rose and fell with every pregnancy. Those who succeeded could become formidable: regents, patrons, kingmakers and, now and then, rulers in all but name. Those who failed could be set aside, locked up or, under one particularly memorable king, beheaded.

The modern consort's role is gentler, but no easier. Prince Philip described his job as making sure the queen could reign. Queen Camilla, it seems, sees hers in much the same way. They are the last in a long line of people who learnt that the weight of the crown is not borne only by the head that wears it. Some of it is always borne by the person in the other chair.
