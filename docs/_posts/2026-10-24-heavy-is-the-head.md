> *"Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown."*
> William Shakespeare, *Henry IV, Part 2* (c. 1597)

## The Question at the Pub Quiz

It is the final round of a pub quiz somewhere in the Midlands, and the quizmaster, a retired geography teacher with a fondness for trick questions, leans into the microphone.

"Question ten. Who was the first King of England?"

Table four confers in urgent whispers.

"Alfred the Great. Obviously. He burnt the cakes."

"He was King of *Wessex*. And then King of the Anglo-Saxons. Not England."

"William the Conqueror, then. 1066. Everybody knows 1066."

"There were kings before 1066. What about Harold? He got an arrow in the eye."

"Possibly got an arrow in the eye. The tapestry is ambiguous."

"Fine. Who was king before Harold?"

"Edward the Confessor. And before him a Dane called Cnut, who sat on the beach telling the tide to go back."

"He sat on the beach to *prove he couldn't* make the tide go back. That's the whole point of the story."

The quizmaster, who has been enjoying this, finally reads out the answer on his card: Æthelstan, crowned in 925, who in 927 became the first man to rule all of England. Table four, which had written "William I", files a formal complaint. It is rejected.

This essay is a very long answer to that question, and to the more interesting ones behind it. Over more than a thousand years the British crown has been a warlord's prize, a sacred office, a family business, a constitutional rubber stamp and, latterly, a national symbol with an excellent wardrobe department. It has been won in battle, grabbed in a hurry, inherited by toddlers, handed over by parliament, invited in from abroad and, once, given up for love.

For each monarch we ask the same questions. How did they get the crown? What did they do with it, for the country and for themselves? And how did they lose it? For most, the answer to the last is dull: they died in bed. But since 1066 alone, England and Britain have had one king killed in battle, one executed by his subjects, at least three deposed and almost certainly murdered, one boy-king who vanished, one who fled, one who abdicated, one shot by an arrow while hunting, and one very probably helped on his way by his doctor so that his death could make the morning papers.

Strictly, "British monarchs" is a short list: the Kingdom of Great Britain dates only from 1707, and its first monarch was Queen Anne. But the crown Anne wore was the product of centuries of English, Scottish and Welsh history, so we will start with the first kings of all England, keep an eye on Scotland and Wales, and follow the line to the present day. We will travel quickly. There are a great many of them, and some, frankly, did not do very much.

## Before England: Warlords, Bretwaldas and a Man Called Alfred

When the Romans left Britain in the early fifth century, the island split into a patchwork of British and, increasingly, Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. By the eighth century the future England was dominated by Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Kent and Wessex, whose kings were first and foremost war leaders: they held power by rewarding followers with land and plunder, and kept it as long as they kept winning. The Mercian king Offa (757 to 796) was mighty enough to build the dyke along the Welsh border that still bears his name, but his supremacy died with him.

What changed everything was the Vikings. In 865 a Danish army, the "great heathen army" of the chroniclers, arrived to conquer rather than raid, and within a decade it had destroyed every English kingdom except Wessex.

![Statue of Alfred the Great in Winchester](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/alfred.jpg)

*Hamo Thornycroft's statue of Alfred the Great in Winchester, unveiled in 1901 to mark what was then believed to be the thousandth anniversary of his death.* Photo: ell brown, [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/39415781@N06/9383032405), via Flickr.

Its king from 871 was Alfred, the only English monarch to be called "the Great". In January 878 a surprise attack drove him into hiding in the Somerset marshes, the setting for the story of the burnt cakes (first recorded a century later, so take it with a pinch of salt, or possibly currants). He regrouped, won a decisive victory at Edington that May, and forced the Danish leader Guthrum to accept baptism and a treaty dividing the country. Alfred then did what the best monarchs in this story tend to do: he built institutions that outlasted him. He ringed Wessex with fortified towns, called *burhs*, reorganised the army, built ships, issued a law code, learnt Latin as an adult and had important books translated into English. By the end he was styling himself "King of the Anglo-Saxons". He died in 899, in bed, which was an achievement in itself.

## The First Kings of England (927 to 1066)

### Æthelstan: the answer to the quiz question

Alfred's son Edward the Elder and daughter Æthelflæd, the formidable "Lady of the Mercians", spent the next quarter-century pushing back into the Danelaw. The prize went to Edward's son Æthelstan. In 927 he took York, the last Viking stronghold, and that summer the rulers of Scotland, Strathclyde and parts of Wales met him at Eamont Bridge, near Penrith, and acknowledged his overlordship. That is the moment historians usually treat as the birth of a single kingdom of England.

![The Coronation Stone at Kingston upon Thames](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/aethelstan.jpg)

*The Coronation Stone at Kingston upon Thames, where Æthelstan was crowned in 925. Local tradition holds that several Anglo-Saxon kings were crowned on it.* Photo: tpholland, [CC BY 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/18964106@N00/5207592484), via Flickr.

Æthelstan was no mere conqueror. He issued laws, standardised the coinage so that his coins bore the title *Rex totius Britanniae*, "king of all Britain", and gathered churchmen and nobles from all over the country to his councils. He is also the first English king of whom a contemporary portrait survives: a manuscript illustration, made around 934, of him offering a book to the shrine of St Cuthbert. In 937 he crushed an alliance of Scots, Strathclyde Britons and Dublin Vikings at the Battle of Brunanburh, a battle so famous at the time that it inspired an Old English poem, and so poorly located today that historians still argue about where it was. He died in 939, unmarried and childless, and was buried at Malmesbury Abbey.

**How he got power:** inheritance, plus conquest. **What he did with it:** created England. **How he lost it:** death from natural causes, aged about 45.

### A crowded century

The century after Æthelstan shows how fragile a new kingdom can be. His half-brother Edmund I was stabbed to death at a feast at Pucklechurch in 946 by an exiled thief he had tried to throw out. Edmund's son Edgar (959 to 975) presided over peace and church reform, and in 973 was crowned in a magnificent ceremony at Bath; the order of service drawn up for it, with its anointing with holy oil, is the backbone of every coronation since, including Charles III's in 2023.

Edgar's son Edward was murdered at Corfe in 978, apparently by followers of his half-brother, and was soon venerated as Edward the Martyr. The beneficiary, Æthelred II, is remembered as "the Unready", a mistranslation of *Unræd*, "ill-advised", a pun on his name, which means "noble counsel". His long reign (978 to 1016) was dominated by renewed Viking attacks, which he tried to buy off with ever-larger payments later called Danegeld. In 1013 the Danish king Sweyn Forkbeard conquered England and Æthelred fled to Normandy. When Sweyn died a few weeks later, the English invited Æthelred back on condition that he governed better: an early example of the English setting terms for their monarch.

### Cnut and the tide

Æthelred died in 1016, and his warrior son Edmund Ironside soon after, leaving Sweyn's son Cnut king of all England, and later of Denmark and Norway too. Cnut married Æthelred's widow, Emma of Normandy, governed through English institutions and proved effective, pious and reasonably popular. The story of him commanding the tide to retreat first appears a century later, and in that version the point is humility: he gets his feet wet to show flattering courtiers that earthly power is nothing beside God's. His sons Harold Harefoot and Harthacnut both died young; Harthacnut collapsed at a wedding feast in 1042 "as he stood at his drink", in the words of the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*.

### Edward the Confessor and the year of three kings

The crown passed back to the old English line in the shape of Edward the Confessor, Æthelred's son by Emma, who had spent most of his life in exile in Normandy. He was devout, keen on hunting, and overshadowed for much of his reign by the mighty Godwin family. His great project was a new abbey church at Westminster, consecrated in December 1065, days before he died on 5 January 1066. He had no children, which turned out to be one of the most consequential facts in English history.

Three men believed they had a claim. Harold Godwinson, Earl of Wessex, said Edward had named him on his deathbed, and was crowned the next day. William, Duke of Normandy, said Edward had promised him the crown years earlier, and that Harold himself had sworn to support him. Harald Hardrada, King of Norway and the most famous warrior in Europe, claimed it through an old agreement between Danish and Norwegian kings.

Harold II had a busy year. In September Hardrada landed in Yorkshire; Harold marched north at astonishing speed and destroyed the Norwegian army at Stamford Bridge on 25 September. Three days later William landed at Pevensey. Harold marched south again, and on 14 October 1066, near Hastings, he was killed, and with him most of the English leadership.

![The Battle of Hastings in the Bayeux Tapestry](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/bayeux.jpg)

*"Here fell Leofwine and Gyrth, brothers of King Harold": Norman knights and English infantry clash at Hastings in the Bayeux Tapestry, which was probably made in England within a few years of the battle.* Photo: archer10 (Dennis), [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/22490717@N02/14994810851), via Flickr.

Whether he died from an arrow in the eye is still argued over. The tapestry shows one figure clutching an arrow near his face beneath the words "Harold Rex" and another cut down by a horseman, and it was restored in the nineteenth century, so the arrow may have been touched up. Earlier written accounts mostly describe him being hacked down. What is certain is that he was the last Anglo-Saxon king of England, and the last English king to die in battle until Richard III, more than four centuries later.

## The Normans (1066 to 1154): Conquest by Committee of One

### William I: the bastard who became the Conqueror

William had been born illegitimate, to the Duke of Normandy and a tanner's daughter from Falaise, and became duke as a child amid violence so constant that several of his guardians were murdered. It was a tough school, and it produced a tough man. He was crowned in Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day 1066, and even that went badly: when the congregation shouted their acclamation, the Norman guards outside thought a riot had broken out and set fire to nearby houses.

He governed England as a conquered country. Northern rebellions in 1069 and 1070 were met with the "Harrying of the North", a campaign of deliberate destruction that even Norman chroniclers condemned. Within a generation almost the entire English aristocracy had been replaced by Normans, castles sprang up everywhere (the White Tower of the Tower of London among them), and French became the language of the elite for three centuries, which is why English has "pork" and "beef" on the table but "pig" and "cow" in the field. His most enduring legacy is paperwork: Domesday Book, the great survey of 1086 of who held what land and what it was worth, so thorough that a chronicler complained not one ox, cow or pig was left out. It is still at the National Archives in Kew.

In 1087, while burning the French town of Mantes, William was injured, reportedly thrown against the pommel of his saddle, and died at Rouen. His funeral at Caen was a disaster even by Norman standards. A local man interrupted to claim the church stood on land stolen from his father and had to be paid off; then, as monks forced the corpulent body into a stone coffin that was too small, it burst, and the mourners fled from the smell.

### William II and Henry I

William left Normandy to his eldest son, Robert, and England to William II, known as Rufus, probably for his ruddy face. Rufus was a capable soldier and a famously irreligious man who left church posts vacant to pocket their revenues; his monument is Westminster Hall, built in the 1090s and still standing. On 2 August 1100 he was killed by an arrow while hunting in the New Forest, traditionally fired by a nobleman called Walter Tirel, who promptly fled to France. Accident or murder has been argued ever since.

Beyond argument is that his younger brother Henry, who was in the hunting party, rode straight to Winchester, seized the treasury and was crowned three days later, while Robert was still on his way home from crusade. Henry I issued a Charter of Liberties promising to end his brother's abuses, a document later cited by the barons who confronted King John. He was a hard, efficient ruler; his reign saw the birth of the Exchequer, named after the chequered cloth on which royal sums were calculated, and he fathered more than twenty illegitimate children, a record for an English king. But in 1120 his only legitimate son drowned when the *White Ship* struck a rock off Barfleur, reportedly with a drunken crew. Henry made his barons swear to accept his daughter Matilda as heir, and in 1135 died in Normandy, according to one chronicler after eating "a surfeit of lampreys" against his doctor's advice.

### Stephen and Matilda: the Anarchy

The barons' oath lasted about as long as it took Henry's nephew, Stephen of Blois, to cross the Channel. Stephen, like Henry before him, moved fast, seized the treasury and was crowned. Matilda, who as the widow of the Holy Roman Emperor still styled herself "Empress", was not the kind of woman to accept this, and the result was nearly twenty years of civil war that later historians called the Anarchy. A chronicler at Peterborough wrote that people said openly that Christ and his saints were asleep.

The war produced some extraordinary moments. In 1141 Stephen was captured at the Battle of Lincoln, and Matilda reached London, but was driven out by the citizens before she could be crowned; she was never formally crowned queen, though she was styled "Lady of the English". In the winter of 1142, besieged in Oxford Castle, she escaped by being let down from a tower and crossing the frozen Thames on foot, reportedly in white to blend in with the snow. Neither side could win. In 1153, after Stephen's eldest son died, a settlement was reached: Stephen would remain king for life, and Matilda's son Henry would succeed him. Stephen died the following year.

## The Plantagenets (1154 to 1399): Empire, Charters and Parliaments

### Henry II: lawgiver and family disaster

Henry II came to the throne aged 21, already ruling Normandy, Anjou and, through his marriage to Eleanor of Aquitaine, the former wife of the King of France, much of south-western France. His lands ran from the Scottish border to the Pyrenees, and he was a man of terrifying energy whose court could barely keep up with him.

For England, his great achievement was law. He sent royal justices round the country on regular circuits, encouraged juries of local men to settle land disputes, and created standard legal procedures, called writs, that anyone who could pay could use to bring a case to the king's courts. Their decisions hardened into a law common to the whole kingdom: the common law, now the foundation of legal systems from the United States to Australia.

His attempt to bring clergy under royal justice set him against his former friend Thomas Becket, whom he had made Archbishop of Canterbury. His exact outburst is not known (the famous "turbulent priest" line is a later paraphrase), but four knights took it as an order and murdered Becket in Canterbury Cathedral on 29 December 1170. Becket was a saint within three years, and Henry did penance at his tomb, flogged by monks. His family was his undoing. His sons rebelled repeatedly, encouraged at times by their mother, whom Henry kept under house arrest for some fifteen years, and he died at Chinon in 1189, defeated by his son Richard and reportedly broken by the news that his favourite, John, had joined the rebels.

### Richard I: the absentee hero

Richard the Lionheart is one of the most famous English kings and one of the least English. He spent perhaps six months of his ten-year reign in England, and saw the country chiefly as a source of money for his crusade. He sold offices, lands and privileges, and is said to have joked that he would sell London if he could find a buyer. On the Third Crusade he won a great victory at Arsuf in 1191 but did not retake Jerusalem. On his way home he was captured by Duke Leopold of Austria, whom he had insulted, and handed over to the Holy Roman Emperor, who demanded a ransom of 150,000 marks: roughly two or three years of the English crown's income. It was raised by heavy taxation. Richard died in 1199 from an infected crossbow wound received while besieging the castle of Châlus-Chabrol in France. His statue outside the Houses of Parliament is a Victorian tribute to a king who barely visited.

### John: the king who lost almost everything except his place in history

John was clever and a hands-on administrator, but suspicious, cruel and untrustworthy. In 1204 he lost Normandy to the French king Philip Augustus, ending the union that had begun in 1066 and earning the nickname "Softsword". His nephew and rival Arthur of Brittany disappeared in his custody and was almost certainly murdered. A quarrel with the Pope got England placed under an interdict and John excommunicated. No English or British monarch since has been christened John, and it is not hard to see why.

To pay for his attempts to win back France, he squeezed his barons with ever-heavier taxes and fines. After his allies were defeated at Bouvines in 1214, the barons rebelled, seized London and forced him to meet them at Runnymede, beside the Thames, where on 15 June 1215 he agreed to Magna Carta.

![The Magna Carta Memorial at Runnymede](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/magna-carta.jpg)

*The memorial at Runnymede, built by the American Bar Association in 1957, which commemorates Magna Carta as a symbol of freedom under law. Four original copies of the 1215 charter survive, two in the British Library and one each at Lincoln and Salisbury cathedrals.* Photo: ttwff, [CC BY 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/37140164@N06/11909741145), via Flickr.

Much of it concerns feudal technicalities, fish weirs and the rights of widows. But clause 39 promised that no free man would be imprisoned, stripped of his property or outlawed except by the lawful judgement of his peers or the law of the land, and clause 40 that justice would not be sold, denied or delayed. The radical idea was that the king himself was subject to the law. As a peace treaty it failed at once: the Pope annulled it within ten weeks, war resumed, and the rebels invited the French king's son, Louis, to take the throne. In October 1216 John lost part of his baggage train to the tides of the Wash (the legend that the crown jewels went with it has never been proved), fell ill with dysentery and died at Newark.

### Henry III: the long minority and the first parliaments

John's son Henry III became king at nine, with much of the country in French hands. His regent, the elderly knight William Marshal, rallied the royalists, reissued Magna Carta in the boy's name to win back the barons, and drove Louis out. That reissue was the charter's real victory: confirmed again and again, it became part of the fabric of English law, and three of its clauses are still on the statute book.

Henry reigned for 56 years, a record until George III. He was pious, artistic and politically inept. His monument is Westminster Abbey, which he rebuilt in the Gothic style; most of what we see today is his. But his foreign favourites and broken promises drove the barons to impose the Provisions of Oxford in 1258, placing government under a baronial council, and when he tried to escape them they rose under his brother-in-law Simon de Montfort, who captured the king at Lewes in 1264. In January 1265 de Montfort summoned a parliament that included, for the first time, representatives of the towns as well as the shires, often seen as the ancestor of the House of Commons. He was killed at Evesham later that year by Henry's son Edward. But the idea that the king must consult the wider community, especially when he wanted money, had taken root.

### Edward I: castles, conquest and the Hammer of the Scots

Edward I (1272 to 1307) was tall, which earned him the nickname "Longshanks", and a formidable lawgiver, soldier and administrator. He passed a string of major statutes reforming land law and royal justice, and summoned regular parliaments that included representatives of the counties and towns, most famously the so-called "Model Parliament" of 1295.

He also used his power ruthlessly. In 1282 and 1283 he conquered the Welsh principality of Gwynedd, whose prince, Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, had been recognised as Prince of Wales by Henry III in the Treaty of Montgomery of 1267, and was killed in a skirmish near Cilmeri in December 1282. Edward ringed north Wales with some of the most magnificent castles in Europe, at Conwy, Caernarfon, Harlech and Beaumaris, now a World Heritage Site. His son Edward was born at Caernarfon in 1284 and created Prince of Wales in 1301, the start of a tradition by which the title is given to the monarch's heir. In 1290 he expelled the entire Jewish population of England, perhaps three thousand people, having first taxed them heavily; Jews were not formally allowed to return until the 1650s.

His attempt to do to Scotland what he had done to Wales is part of the Scottish story, to which we will come shortly. He died in 1307 at Burgh by Sands, near Carlisle, on his way north to fight Robert the Bruce. The Latin inscription on his tomb in Westminster Abbey, *Scottorum malleus*, "Hammer of the Scots", was added two centuries later.

### Edward II: favourites, failure and a dark end

Edward II was in almost every respect the opposite of his father. He disliked war, enjoyed rowing, swimming and thatching, and lavished favour on Piers Gaveston, a Gascon knight whose relationship with the king historians still debate, and who mocked the great barons with nicknames. The barons had Gaveston murdered in 1312. In 1314 Edward led a great army into Scotland and was disastrously defeated by Robert the Bruce at Bannockburn. He then fell under the influence of a new favourite, Hugh Despenser the Younger, and the regime became so rapacious that his own queen, Isabella of France, turned against him.

In 1326 Isabella and her lover Roger Mortimer invaded from France with a small force. Edward's support evaporated. In January 1327 he was forced to give up his crown in favour of his fourteen-year-old son: the first deposition of an English king since the Conquest, and one that set a dangerous precedent. He died in Berkeley Castle in September 1327, almost certainly murdered. The lurid story of a red-hot poker comes from later chroniclers and should be treated as legend; the reality, though less gruesome in the telling, was probably no less cold-blooded.

### Edward III: glory, plague and the Garter

The young Edward III spent three years as a figurehead for his mother and Mortimer. Then, in October 1330, aged seventeen, he led a band of friends through a secret passage into Nottingham Castle, seized Mortimer and had him hanged.

Edward claimed the French throne through his mother, starting what became the Hundred Years' War, which in fact ran, on and off, from 1337 to 1453. His longbowmen won stunning victories at Crécy in 1346 and, under his son Edward the Black Prince, at Poitiers in 1356, where the French king was captured. Around 1348 he founded the Order of the Garter, which still meets every June at Windsor. Because he constantly needed parliament's consent for war taxes, the Commons became a regular, separate part of parliament, and in 1362 a statute required lawsuits to be pleaded in English rather than French. His reign also saw the Black Death of 1348, which killed perhaps a third to a half of the population. His last years were sad: the Black Prince died in 1376, the French conquests slipped away, and his mistress, Alice Perrers, was said to have pulled the rings from his fingers as he died in 1377.

### Richard II: the boy who faced down a revolt

The Black Prince's son, Richard II, became king at ten. At fourteen he faced the Peasants' Revolt of 1381, sparked by a poll tax, in which rebels burnt London's grandest palace and beheaded the Archbishop of Canterbury. At Smithfield, after the Mayor of London killed the rebel leader Wat Tyler, the boy rode forward, declared himself their leader and persuaded the crowds to go home. It was real courage. His promises to them were promptly broken.

He grew into a cultured king with an exalted idea of his office. He commissioned the exquisite Wilton Diptych, now in the National Gallery, rebuilt Westminster Hall with its great hammerbeam roof, and his portrait in Westminster Abbey is the earliest surviving painted portrait of an English monarch.

![The coronation of Richard II in a fifteenth-century manuscript](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/richard-ii.jpg)

*The coronation of the ten-year-old Richard II, as imagined in a fifteenth-century manuscript of Jean de Wavrin's chronicles of England.* Image: Walters Art Museum, Manuscript W.201, [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/39699193@N03/24213150343), via Flickr.

He was also vindictive. In 1397 and 1398 he took revenge on nobles who had humiliated him a decade earlier, exiled his cousin Henry Bolingbroke and then seized Bolingbroke's vast Lancastrian inheritance. In 1399, with Richard in Ireland, Bolingbroke landed in Yorkshire claiming only to want his lands back. Support flocked to him; Richard was captured, forced to abdicate and died in Pontefract Castle early in 1400, probably starved. His fall raised a question that would haunt the next century: if one king could be removed, why not another?

## Interlude: Meanwhile, in Scotland and Wales

England was not the only kingdom on the island. Scotland's kings traced their line to Kenneth MacAlpin, who united Scots and Picts in the mid-ninth century. Its most famous medieval king bears little resemblance to Shakespeare's tormented murderer. The real Macbeth killed Duncan I in battle in 1040, not in bed, reigned for seventeen years, was secure enough to go on pilgrimage to Rome in 1050, and was killed by Duncan's son Malcolm III in 1057.

In 1286 Alexander III rode out on a stormy night, fell from his horse and was found dead on the shore at Kinghorn. His heir, his three-year-old granddaughter Margaret, the "Maid of Norway", died in Orkney in 1290 on her way to Scotland. The Scots asked Edward I of England to choose between the claimants; he picked John Balliol, treated him as a vassal, and in 1296 invaded, deposed him and carried the Stone of Destiny, on which Scottish kings were inaugurated, to Westminster.

It was Robert the Bruce who won independence, though his route was not pretty: in 1306 he stabbed his rival John Comyn before the high altar of a church in Dumfries, then had himself crowned at Scone. After years as a fugitive he crushed Edward II at Bannockburn in 1314. In 1320 Scottish nobles sent the Pope the Declaration of Arbroath, warning that if Bruce ever betrayed them to the English they would choose another king, because they fought not for glory or riches but for freedom. England recognised Scottish independence in 1328.

The Stewarts, descended from Bruce's daughter, took the throne in 1371 and showed a remarkable talent for violent ends. James I was murdered at Perth in 1437, trapped in a drain whose outlet he had recently blocked to stop tennis balls going down it. James II was killed when one of his own cannons exploded in 1460. James III died after losing a battle to rebels fronted by his own son. That son, James IV, a gifted Renaissance prince, was killed at Flodden in 1513, the last monarch from anywhere in Britain to die in battle. James V died in 1542, a week after the birth of his only legitimate child, Mary.

Wales was rarely united under one native ruler for long, although Hywel Dda, remembered for codifying Welsh law, came close in the tenth century. Llywelyn ap Gruffudd was recognised as Prince of Wales by Henry III in 1267, but was killed in 1282 as Edward I conquered his principality. The last great bid for independence, the rising of Owain Glyndŵr, began in 1400 and held much of Wales for years before fading; nobody knows where or when he died. Under Henry VIII, the Laws in Wales Acts of 1535 and 1542 annexed Wales fully to England. By then, as we shall see, England's royal family was itself Welsh.

## Lancaster and York (1399 to 1485): The Wars of the Roses

### Henry IV and Henry V

Henry IV had taken the crown by force, and spent most of his reign trying to keep it. He faced Glyndŵr's rebellion in Wales, and a rising by the Percy family of Northumberland, whose famous son Harry "Hotspur" was killed at the Battle of Shrewsbury in 1403. He fell seriously ill, possibly with a skin disease that contemporaries thought was leprosy, a judgement for his sins. He died in 1413 in the Jerusalem Chamber at Westminster Abbey, fulfilling, it was said, a prophecy that he would die in Jerusalem.

His son Henry V is the hero of Shakespeare's play, and he remains the closest thing English history has to a warrior saint. He renewed the war with France, and on 25 October 1415 won a famous victory at Agincourt against a much larger French army, thanks largely to his archers and to French ground so sodden that heavily armoured knights struggled to move. He went on to conquer Normandy, and in 1420, by the Treaty of Troyes, he was recognised as heir to the French throne and married Catherine of Valois, the French king's daughter. He was the first king since the Conquest to write letters in English as a matter of policy.

![Statue of Henry V on the Shire Hall at Monmouth](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/henry-v.jpg)

*Henry V in his niche on the Shire Hall at Monmouth, the town of his birth. The statue in front, holding a model aeroplane, is of Charles Rolls, co-founder of Rolls-Royce.* Photo: ell brown, [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/39415781@N06/18942233139), via Flickr.

He was well on his way to becoming King of France when he died of dysentery at Vincennes in August 1422, aged about 35. The French king, Charles VI, died two months later. Had Henry lived a few months more, he would have worn both crowns.

### Henry VI: the infant king who lost everything

Instead, both crowns passed to his son, Henry VI, who was nine months old. He was crowned King of France in Paris in 1431, the only English king to be so. But he grew up into a gentle, pious, unworldly man, utterly unsuited to leading a medieval kingdom at war. His lasting legacies are educational: he founded Eton College in 1440 and King's College, Cambridge, in 1441. Meanwhile, the French, inspired at first by Joan of Arc, slowly drove the English out. By 1453 only Calais was left.

That same year, Henry suffered a complete mental collapse and for more than a year did not recognise anyone, even his newborn son. Into the power vacuum stepped Richard, Duke of York, who had a strong claim to the throne himself, descended from Edward III through two lines. The rivalry between York and the king's Lancastrian supporters, led by the fierce Queen Margaret of Anjou, broke into open war in 1455: the Wars of the Roses, a name invented much later, after the white rose of York and a red rose of Lancaster that was largely a Tudor invention. Richard of York was killed in 1460, but his son Edward won the throne the next year.

### Edward IV, Edward V and Richard III

Edward IV was six feet four inches tall, handsome, charming and a brilliant general who never lost a battle he commanded. He secured the throne at Towton in March 1461, fought in a snowstorm and often called the bloodiest battle on English soil, then astonished everyone by secretly marrying Elizabeth Woodville, a widow from a minor gentry family. That infuriated his chief backer, the Earl of Warwick, "the Kingmaker", who in 1470 switched sides and briefly restored Henry VI. Edward fled abroad, returned the next year, killed Warwick at Barnet and destroyed the Lancastrians at Tewkesbury. Henry VI died in the Tower soon afterwards, almost certainly murdered. Edward's second reign was peaceful and prosperous; he took a large pension from the French king in return for not invading France, and was a patron of William Caxton, who set up England's first printing press at Westminster in 1476. He died suddenly in 1483, aged 40.

His son, Edward V, was twelve. His uncle Richard, Duke of Gloucester, named Protector, intercepted him and lodged him and his younger brother in the Tower. In June the boys were declared illegitimate, on the grounds that their father had been contracted to marry another woman before their mother, and Richard took the throne as Richard III. The princes were never seen again. Most historians believe they were killed in 1483, probably on Richard's orders, though it has never been proved.

![Portrait of Richard III](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/richard-iii.jpg)

*Richard III, in the earliest surviving portrait of him, painted some decades after his death.* Image: lisby1, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/60861613@N00/3473317431), via Flickr.

Richard was a capable administrator, and his only parliament protected suspects' right to bail. But he never escaped the stain of his nephews' disappearance. In August 1485 an exile called Henry Tudor landed in Wales, and on 22 August at Bosworth, after the powerful Stanley family switched sides, Richard led a desperate charge at Henry and was cut down: the last English king to die in battle. In 2012 archaeologists digging in a Leicester car park found his skeleton, its spine twisted by scoliosis and its skull marked by battle wounds; DNA from his sister's descendants confirmed the match, and he was reburied in Leicester Cathedral in 2015.

## The Tudors (1485 to 1603): Wives, Reformations and an Armada

### Henry VII: the accountant king

Henry VII's claim to the throne was flimsy. It came through his mother, Margaret Beaufort, descended from an illegitimate (later legitimised) son of John of Gaunt, and his Welsh grandfather, Owen Tudor, had secretly married Henry V's widow. His real claim was victory. He strengthened it by marrying Elizabeth of York, the elder sister of the princes in the Tower, uniting the warring houses; the Tudor rose, red and white, was a piece of brilliant branding.

Henry faced pretenders. Lambert Simnel was crowned in Dublin in 1487 as "Edward VI", defeated at Stoke Field, and then pardoned and given a job in the royal kitchens, which tells you a good deal about Henry's sense of humour. Perkin Warbeck, who claimed to be the younger prince in the Tower, caused trouble for years before he was executed in 1499. Henry's real achievement, though, was financial. He governed through able officials, used bonds to keep the nobility in check, collected every penny owed to the crown, and avoided expensive wars. He was the first English king in a long while to die solvent, in 1509, and he handed his son a full treasury and, for the first time in decades, an undisputed succession.

### Henry VIII: six wives and a revolution

Henry VIII was eighteen when he became king: tall, athletic, musical and learned. The bloated tyrant of later portraits was the product of the following decades, including a serious jousting fall in 1536.

![Portrait of Henry VIII after Hans Holbein](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/henry-viii.jpg)

*Henry VIII after Hans Holbein the Younger. The pose was designed to fill the viewer with awe, and largely succeeded.* Image: lisby1, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/60861613@N00/3472368670), via Flickr.

Schoolchildren remember his six wives as "divorced, beheaded, died; divorced, beheaded, survived". Catherine of Aragon, his elder brother's widow, gave him a daughter, Mary, but no surviving son. Convinced that God was punishing him, and in love with Anne Boleyn, Henry sought an annulment; when the Pope refused, largely because Catherine's nephew, the Emperor Charles V, controlled Rome, Henry broke with Rome altogether, and the Act of Supremacy of 1534 made him Supreme Head of the Church of England. Anne gave him a daughter, Elizabeth, and was beheaded in 1536 on charges most historians think false. Jane Seymour gave him a son, Edward, and died days later. Anne of Cleves, chosen from a Holbein portrait, disappointed him in person and was pensioned off as a contented ex-wife. Catherine Howard was beheaded for adultery; Catherine Parr outlived him.

The consequences were enormous. The break with Rome began the English Reformation. Between 1536 and 1541 some eight hundred religious houses were dissolved and their vast estates seized and sold, creating a class of landowners with a stake in the new order. Henry built up the navy (his flagship, the *Mary Rose*, sank in 1545 and was raised in 1982) and made himself King of Ireland. He also wasted his father's fortune on French wars and debased the coinage so badly that the silver wore off the copper at the nose on his shillings, earning him the name "Old Coppernose". He died in 1547, having executed two wives, two chief ministers and a long list of others.

### Edward VI, Jane and Mary I

Henry's son Edward VI was nine when he became king, and the country was governed by his uncle, the Duke of Somerset, and then by the Duke of Northumberland. Both pushed England in a firmly Protestant direction. The Book of Common Prayer, drafted largely by Archbishop Thomas Cranmer and first issued in 1549, put church services into English, and its language still echoes through English: "till death us do part" and "ashes to ashes" both come from it. Edward was precocious, devout and a firm Protestant, and when he fell fatally ill at fifteen, he and Northumberland drew up a "devise" excluding his Catholic half-sister Mary from the throne in favour of his Protestant cousin, Lady Jane Grey, who happened to be married to Northumberland's son.

Jane was proclaimed queen on 10 July 1553. Within nine days the country had rallied to Mary, Northumberland's support collapsed, and Jane was in the Tower. She was executed in February 1554, aged about sixteen. She is usually left out of official lists of monarchs, because she was never crowned and her accession is regarded as unlawful.

Mary I, England's first undisputed queen regnant, set about restoring the Catholic Church. She married Philip of Spain, son of the Emperor Charles V, in 1554, which provoked a rebellion, and she revived the heresy laws. Around 280 Protestants were burned at the stake, including Cranmer, which earned her the name "Bloody Mary", fixed in the national memory by John Foxe's *Book of Martyrs*. She longed for a child; two pregnancies turned out to be phantom ones. In January 1558 England lost Calais, its last possession in France, and Mary is said to have declared that, when she died, "Calais" would be found written on her heart. She died in November 1558, aged 42.

### Elizabeth I: Gloriana

Elizabeth was twenty-five when she became queen. She had been declared illegitimate as a child, had seen her mother executed and had spent part of her sister's reign in the Tower. It was an education in survival.

She restored a moderate Protestant church in 1559, designed to keep as many people in it as possible, and practised ambiguity as a political art, dangling the prospect of marriage before foreign princes for decades without ever committing. She never married, and cultivated the image of the Virgin Queen wedded to her kingdom.

![The Armada Portrait of Elizabeth I](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/elizabeth-i.jpg)

*The Armada Portrait, painted to celebrate the defeat of the Spanish fleet in 1588. Note the queen's hand resting on the globe.* Image: lisby1, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/60861613@N00/3473475412), via Flickr.

Her reign was dangerous. In 1570 the Pope excommunicated her and released her subjects from obedience. Her Catholic cousin and heir, Mary, Queen of Scots, who had fled to England in 1568 after being forced to abdicate, became the focus of plot after plot, and was executed at Fotheringhay in February 1587. The following year Philip II of Spain sent the Armada, which was harried up the Channel, scattered by fireships off Calais and wrecked by storms on its way home round Scotland and Ireland. At Tilbury, Elizabeth told her troops she had "the heart and stomach of a king, and of a king of England too".

Her reign saw Shakespeare and Marlowe, Drake's voyage round the world, the first attempts at English colonies in America and, on the last day of 1600, the charter of the East India Company. Her final years brought war in Ireland, bad harvests and the rebellion of her old favourite, the Earl of Essex, but in her "Golden Speech" of 1601 she told parliament that she counted the glory of her crown to be that she had reigned with her people's love. She died in March 1603, the last of the Tudors.

## The Stuarts (1603 to 1714): Divine Right, Civil War and Revolution

### James VI and I: one king, two kingdoms

Elizabeth's heir was the son of the woman she had executed: James VI, King of Scots since he was thirteen months old. When the news reached Edinburgh, James set off south so quickly that he left most of his court behind. He was the first monarch to rule both England and Scotland, though the two remained separate kingdoms with separate parliaments, churches and laws. He styled himself "King of Great Britain" by proclamation in 1604, and in 1606 the first version of the Union Flag was devised, combining the crosses of St George and St Andrew.

![James I enthroned among his family](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/james-i.jpg)

*James I enthroned among his family, in an engraving after a seventeenth-century print. He was, in a quip usually attributed to the French king Henri IV, the wisest fool in Christendom.* Image: lisby1, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/60861613@N00/3828443796), via Flickr.

James was clever, bookish and garrulous. He wrote books on kingship, on witchcraft (*Daemonologie*, 1597) and against tobacco (*A Counterblaste to Tobacco*, 1604), which he called loathsome to the eye, hateful to the nose and harmful to the brain. He believed firmly in the divine right of kings, the idea that monarchs were appointed by God and answerable only to him. His greatest legacy is the translation of the Bible he commissioned in 1604 and that was published in 1611, the King James Version, which shaped the English language for centuries. He survived the Gunpowder Plot of 1605, in which Catholic conspirators planned to blow up the king in parliament, an event still marked every 5 November with fireworks. He was also extravagant and lavished money and titles on handsome favourites, above all George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham. He died in 1625.

### Charles I: the king who lost his head

Charles I was shy, stammering, devout, a superb patron of the arts (he employed Anthony van Dyck and amassed one of Europe's great picture collections) and absolutely convinced of his divine right. He was also inflexible and not entirely trustworthy, a fatal combination.

After quarrels with parliament over money and religion, he ruled without it from 1629 to 1640, raising funds by dubious means such as Ship Money, a wartime tax on coastal counties that he extended inland in peacetime. Then in 1637 he tried to impose an English-style prayer book on Presbyterian Scotland, provoking a riot in St Giles' Cathedral, a national covenant and a Scottish invasion. To pay for an army he had to recall parliament, which promptly began dismantling his powers. In January 1642 he marched into the House of Commons with soldiers to arrest five members, who had been warned and fled. No monarch has entered the chamber since. Civil war followed.

The wars across Charles's three kingdoms killed a larger share of the population than the First World War. Parliament's New Model Army, in which Oliver Cromwell rose to prominence, beat the king decisively at Naseby in 1645. Charles surrendered, negotiated, escaped, made a secret deal with the Scots and restarted the war, which convinced the army that he could never be trusted. In January 1649, before a purged parliament's court whose authority he refused to recognise, he was tried for treason against his own people.

On 30 January 1649 he was beheaded outside the Banqueting House in Whitehall, beneath a ceiling Rubens had painted in glorification of his father's divine kingship. It was cold, and he is said to have worn two shirts so that he would not shiver and be thought afraid. When the head was held up, one witness recalled, the crowd gave a groan such as he had never heard before.

![Dutch print of the execution of Charles I](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/charles-i-execution.jpg)

*"Englands Memorial": a Dutch broadsheet published in Amsterdam in 1649, showing the execution of Charles I before the Banqueting House at Whitehall.* Image: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, [CC0](https://www.rawpixel.com/image/9329617/photo-image-art-vintage-book), via Rawpixel.

### The kingless decade

For eleven years England had no king. The monarchy and the House of Lords were abolished, and the country became a Commonwealth. Cromwell conquered Ireland with notorious brutality and then Scotland, uniting the three countries under a single government for the first time. In 1653 he dissolved what was left of parliament and became Lord Protector, a king in all but name. He was offered the crown in 1657, and refused it. He died in 1658, and his son Richard, nicknamed "Tumbledown Dick", proved unable to hold the regime together. In 1660, General George Monck marched his army from Scotland to London, and a new parliament invited Charles I's son home.

![Gold coin showing Oliver Cromwell](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/cromwell.jpg)

*Cromwell, laurelled like a Roman emperor, on a gold broad engraved by Thomas Simon. The Latin calls him Lord Protector of England, Scotland and Ireland: a king in all but name. He is said to have asked to be painted "warts and all".* Image: Cleveland Museum of Art, [CC0](https://www.rawpixel.com/image/9690158/broad-oliver-cromwell-lord-protector-obverse-thomas-simon), via Rawpixel.

### Charles II: the merry monarch

Charles II entered London on 29 May 1660, his thirtieth birthday, to wildly cheering crowds. He was witty, charming, cynical and lazy about anything that bored him, and determined above all, as he is supposed to have said, never to go on his travels again. He took his revenge only on those who had signed his father's death warrant; Cromwell's body was dug up and hanged at Tyburn.

His reign saw the Great Plague of 1665 and the Great Fire of London in 1666, after which Christopher Wren rebuilt St Paul's Cathedral and dozens of city churches. Charles was genuinely interested in science, granted a royal charter to the Royal Society in 1662 and founded the Royal Observatory at Greenwich in 1675. Theatres reopened and, for the first time, women appeared on the English stage. He had at least twelve acknowledged illegitimate children by a string of mistresses, including the actress Nell Gwyn, but no legitimate ones. His brother and heir, James, was openly Catholic, and the attempt by parliament to exclude him from the succession between 1679 and 1681 gave rise to England's first political parties: the Whigs, who wanted to exclude him, and the Tories, who supported the hereditary succession. Both names began as insults. Charles was received into the Catholic Church on his deathbed in 1685, and is said to have apologised to his courtiers for being "an unconscionable time a-dying".

### James II: the king who ran away

James II came to the throne with plenty of goodwill. He lost it all in three years. He crushed a rebellion by Charles II's illegitimate son, the Duke of Monmouth, in 1685, after which hundreds of rebels were hanged or transported in the "Bloody Assizes". He then used his powers to suspend the laws against Catholics and appointed Catholics to the army, the universities and the government, and when seven bishops refused to read his declaration of indulgence from their pulpits he put them on trial. They were acquitted to public rejoicing.

The final straw was the birth of a son in June 1688, which raised the prospect of a permanent Catholic dynasty. Seven leading politicians invited the Protestant William of Orange, Stadtholder of the Dutch Republic and husband of James's Protestant daughter Mary, to intervene. William landed at Torbay in Devon on 5 November 1688 with a large army. James's support melted away, including that of his younger daughter, Anne, and of his best general, John Churchill. In December James fled, reportedly throwing the Great Seal of the realm into the Thames as he went. He was caught by fishermen, brought back to London, and then allowed to escape to France, which suited everybody.

### William III and Mary II: monarchs by contract

Parliament decided that James had abdicated by fleeing, and offered the crown jointly to William and Mary, the only joint reign in British history. With it came the Bill of Rights of 1689: no suspending laws, raising taxes or keeping a peacetime army without parliament's consent, free elections, free speech in parliamentary debate, and no Catholics on the throne. The "Glorious Revolution" was glorious, in English eyes, because it was nearly bloodless in England. It was not bloodless in Ireland or Scotland, where supporters of James, the Jacobites, fought on; William beat James at the Boyne in 1690, a battle still marked by Protestant marches in Northern Ireland each July.

![Gilded statue of William III at Portsmouth](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/william-mary.jpg)

*A gilded statue of William III, dressed as a Roman general, in Portsmouth Historic Dockyard.* Photo: ell brown, [CC BY 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/39415781@N06/9330793362), via Flickr.

William, a Dutch Calvinist and asthmatic, was chiefly interested in using England's resources against Louis XIV of France. To pay for the war his ministers created the Bank of England in 1694 and the beginnings of the national debt, institutions that would help make Britain the richest country in the world. Mary, who ran the government while William was at war, died of smallpox in 1694, aged 32. William died in 1702 after his horse stumbled on a molehill at Hampton Court, and Jacobites thereafter toasted "the little gentleman in the black velvet waistcoat".

### Anne: the first Queen of Great Britain

Anne, Mary's younger sister, was pregnant at least seventeen times, but only one child survived infancy, and he died at eleven. Plagued by illness, she had to be carried to her coronation. Yet her reign was one of the most consequential in British history. Her armies under John Churchill, Duke of Marlborough, beat the French at Blenheim in 1704, and the nation built him Blenheim Palace in thanks. At home, the Acts of Union of 1707 merged England and Scotland into a single Kingdom of Great Britain with one parliament, while Scotland kept its own church and law. Anne thus became the first monarch of Great Britain. Her reign also produced the world's first copyright law, the Statute of Anne of 1710. She was the last monarch to veto a bill, in 1708, and the last to "touch for the king's evil", the belief that the royal touch could cure scrofula; one of the last she touched, in 1712, was a toddler called Samuel Johnson.

With no surviving children, the succession had already been settled. The Act of Settlement of 1701 passed the crown to the nearest Protestant heir, Sophia of Hanover, a granddaughter of James I, and her descendants, bypassing more than fifty Catholics with stronger hereditary claims. Sophia died weeks before Anne, who died in August 1714. Her doctor wrote that sleep was never more welcome to a weary traveller than death was to her.

## The Hanoverians (1714 to 1901): The Crown Steps Back

### George I and George II: the German Georges

George I, Sophia's son, was 54, spoke little English (he talked to his ministers mostly in French) and much preferred Hanover. He arrived without his wife, Sophia Dorothea, whom he had divorced and kept confined in a German castle for over thirty years after her affair with a Swedish count, who vanished, almost certainly murdered.

His reign's most important development happened almost by accident. Because George took little interest in the details of British politics and attended cabinet less and less, power drifted to his ministers. After the South Sea Bubble burst in 1720, Robert Walpole rose to dominate government for twenty years, and is regarded as Britain's first prime minister.

His son George II, with whom he had a famously poisonous relationship, was hot-tempered and brave. In 1743 he led his troops at Dettingen, the last British monarch to command in battle. In 1745 Charles Edward Stuart, "Bonnie Prince Charlie", grandson of James II, led a Jacobite army from Scotland as far as Derby before turning back; the rising ended at Culloden in April 1746, the last pitched battle on British soil, followed by brutal repression in the Highlands. George's reign also saw victory in the Seven Years' War, which won Canada and British dominance in India. The story that he began the custom of standing for Handel's Hallelujah Chorus is charming but unsupported. He died in 1760, shortly after getting up from his close stool.

### George III: Farmer George and the lost colonies

George III was the first Hanoverian born in Britain and to speak English as his first language, and he declared that he gloried in the name of Britain. He reigned for nearly sixty years, was devoted to his wife, Charlotte, with whom he had fifteen children, and earned the nickname "Farmer George" for his enthusiasm for agriculture. He collected scientific instruments, helped found the Royal Academy of Arts in 1768, and amassed a library that is now a foundation collection of the British Library.

![Coronation portrait of George III by Allan Ramsay](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/george-iii.jpg)

*George III in his coronation robes, by Allan Ramsay. The studio produced dozens of copies for embassies and colonial governors.* Image: studio of Allan Ramsay, [CC0](https://www.rawpixel.com/image/8924710/george-iii-england), via Rawpixel.

He also lost America. He was not quite the tyrant of the Declaration of Independence, since policy was by then made by ministers answerable to parliament, but he backed the hard line and was reluctant to give up even after Yorktown in 1781. In 1801 the Act of Union created the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland, and the Union Flag gained the cross of St Patrick; George then blocked Pitt's plan for Catholic equality as a breach of his coronation oath, and Pitt resigned. He suffered repeated bouts of severe mental illness. The once-fashionable diagnosis of porphyria is now doubted, and some suggest bipolar disorder; the honest answer is that we do not know. From 1811 his son ruled as Prince Regent, and the old king spent his last decade blind and confined at Windsor, dying in 1820.

### George IV: the Prince of Pleasure

George IV had been a figure of national satire for decades before he became king in 1820. He was self-indulgent, extravagant and enormously fat, and he ran up colossal debts. He had secretly and illegally married a Catholic widow, Maria Fitzherbert, in 1785, then married his cousin Caroline of Brunswick in 1795 in return for parliament paying off his debts. They loathed each other on sight. At his coronation in 1821 Caroline was barred from Westminster Abbey and turned away at the door. He also had genuinely good taste: he was a great patron of architecture and art, transformed Buckingham House into Buckingham Palace, remodelled Windsor Castle, and built the exuberant Royal Pavilion at Brighton. In 1822 he became the first reigning monarch to visit Scotland since Charles II, in a visit stage-managed by the novelist Walter Scott, wearing a kilt and flesh-coloured tights. In 1829 he reluctantly accepted Catholic emancipation, which allowed Catholics to sit in parliament. When he died in 1830, *The Times* wrote that there had never been an individual less regretted by his fellow creatures.

### William IV: the sailor king

His brother William IV had served in the Royal Navy as a young man, and became king at 64. He was bluff, eccentric and much more popular than his brother, and he had ten illegitimate children with the actress Dorothea Jordan. His reign saw the Great Reform Act of 1832, which overhauled the electoral system and abolished "rotten boroughs", constituencies with few or no voters. When the House of Lords blocked it, William reluctantly agreed to create enough new peers to force it through if necessary, and the Lords backed down. The following year parliament passed the act abolishing slavery in most of the British Empire. In 1834 William dismissed his prime minister, Lord Melbourne, and appointed the Tories, but they could not command a majority in the Commons and Melbourne was back within months. He was the last monarch to appoint a prime minister against the will of parliament. When he died in 1837 without surviving legitimate children, the crown of Hanover, which could not pass to a woman, went to his brother, and the crown of Britain to his eighteen-year-old niece.

### Victoria: the grandmother of Europe

Victoria was woken on 20 June 1837 and told she was queen. She wrote in her diary that she was young and inexperienced, but that few had more real desire to do what was right. Her first act was to have her bed moved out of her mother's room.

In 1840 she married her German cousin, Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha. Their nine children married into the royal houses of Europe, so that by 1914 her grandchildren sat on the thrones of Britain, Germany and Russia. Albert, serious and progressive, drove the Great Exhibition of 1851, whose profits built the museums of South Kensington. When he died in December 1861, Victoria went into a mourning that lasted, in some form, for the rest of her life, and her long withdrawal from public life fuelled a burst of republicanism in the 1860s.

Her reign saw Britain become the world's leading industrial power and the centre of the largest empire in history; in 1876 Disraeli made her Empress of India. She popularised the white wedding dress, made chloroform in childbirth respectable by using it in 1853, and survived at least seven assassination attempts. Her Golden and Diamond Jubilees, in 1887 and 1897, helped invent the modern monarch as the focus of national celebration.

![Queen Victoria photographed by Alexander Bassano](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/victoria.jpg)

*Queen Victoria, from Alexander Bassano's photograph of 1882, which became the official portrait for her Golden Jubilee.* Photo: WRGreens, [Public domain](https://www.flickr.com/photos/158138415@N04/24964285128), via Flickr.

Meanwhile real power drained away. Victoria had strong views, adoring some prime ministers (Melbourne, Disraeli) and loathing others (Gladstone, who she complained addressed her as though she were a public meeting). But as the electorate widened in 1832, 1867 and 1884, it was voters who chose governments. In 1867 Walter Bagehot's *The English Constitution* distinguished the "dignified" parts of the constitution, which win loyalty, from the "efficient" parts, which govern, and gave the monarch three rights: to be consulted, to encourage and to warn. It remains the best short description of the job. Victoria died in January 1901, after 63 years and seven months on the throne.

## Saxe-Coburg and Windsor (1901 to the present): The Family Firm

### Edward VII: the uncle of Europe

Victoria's eldest son, Edward VII, had waited nearly sixty years for the throne, and had filled the time with racing, shooting, gambling, eating and a long string of mistresses; his mother blamed his escapades for his father's death. He turned out to be a surprisingly successful king. Charming and sociable, he travelled widely and his state visit to Paris in 1903 helped pave the way for the Entente Cordiale with France the following year. He revived royal pageantry, and gave his name to an era. He died in May 1910, in the middle of a constitutional crisis over the House of Lords' rejection of the Liberal government's "People's Budget".

### George V: the man who invented the Windsors

His son George V resolved the crisis by privately agreeing to create enough Liberal peers to pass the Parliament Act of 1911, which ended the Lords' veto over most legislation. A dutiful, conservative former naval officer with a passion for stamp collecting, he steered the monarchy through its most dangerous decades.

In July 1917, amid fierce anti-German feeling, he changed the royal house's name from Saxe-Coburg and Gotha to Windsor; his cousin the Kaiser joked that he was off to see *The Merry Wives of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha*. That same year George withdrew an offer of asylum to another cousin, the deposed Tsar Nicholas II, fearing it would damage the monarchy at home; the Tsar and his family were murdered in 1918. The fall of the Russian, German and Austrian thrones taught George a lesson his successors never forgot: monarchies that lose touch with their people can vanish. So he adapted. In 1924 he invited Ramsay MacDonald to form the first Labour government, and in 1932 made the first royal Christmas broadcast, with a script by Rudyard Kipling. He died at Sandringham in January 1936; decades later his doctor's notes revealed that Lord Dawson had hastened his death with morphine and cocaine, partly so that it could be announced in the morning edition of *The Times* rather than the evening papers.

### Edward VIII: the king who left

His son Edward VIII was glamorous, modern and popular, the first royal celebrity of the age of mass media. He was also bored by paperwork, and was in love with Wallis Simpson, an American who had been divorced once and was in the process of divorcing her second husband. As Supreme Governor of the Church of England, which then did not allow divorced people to remarry while a former spouse was alive, Edward could not marry her and remain king, at least not with the support of his government or of the prime ministers of the Dominions. Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin made it clear that the government would resign if he went ahead.

Edward chose Mrs Simpson. He signed the Instrument of Abdication on 10 December 1936, and parliament's act made it effective the next day, after a reign of less than eleven months; he was never crowned. In a radio broadcast he told the nation he had found it impossible to carry the heavy burden of responsibility without the help and support of the woman he loved. He became Duke of Windsor, married Wallis in France in 1937, and lived out his life in exile, mostly in Paris. His visit to Germany in 1937, where he met Hitler, and his later wartime conduct, did lasting damage to his reputation.

### George VI: the reluctant king

Edward's younger brother, Albert, Duke of York, had never expected to be king, and did not want to be. He was shy, suffered from a severe stammer, which he worked hard to overcome with the help of the Australian speech therapist Lionel Logue, and took the name George VI to signal continuity with his father. With his wife, Queen Elizabeth, and his daughters, Elizabeth and Margaret, he restored the monarchy's image as a dutiful family.

During the Second World War, the king and queen stayed in London and toured bombed cities. When Buckingham Palace was bombed in September 1940, the queen said she was glad, because now she could look the East End in the face. The king created the George Cross for civilian gallantry and, in 1942, awarded it to the whole island of Malta. After the war, India became independent in 1947 and George ceased to be Emperor of India. He also became the first Head of the Commonwealth, as the former empire turned into a voluntary association of independent states, many of them republics. A heavy smoker, he died of lung cancer and its complications in his sleep at Sandringham on 6 February 1952, aged 56.

### Elizabeth II: seventy years

His daughter Elizabeth was in Kenya when she became queen, staying at a royal lodge after a night at the Treetops hotel, hence the line that she went up a tree a princess and came down a queen. She was 25. Her coronation on 2 June 1953 was the first to be televised, and sent hundreds of thousands of families out to buy or rent their first set.

![Elizabeth II in New Zealand, 1954](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/elizabeth-ii.jpg)

*The young queen with the mayor, Harry Barker, at Gisborne, New Zealand, on 6 January 1954, during her six-month tour of the Commonwealth after her coronation.* Photo: Archives New Zealand, [CC BY 2.0](https://www.flickr.com/photos/35759981@N08/17172528575), via Flickr.

She reigned for 70 years and 214 days, the longest of any British monarch, and the second-longest verifiable reign of any sovereign, after Louis XIV. She oversaw the turning of empire into Commonwealth, which she plainly cared for deeply, travelled more widely than any predecessor and worked with fifteen prime ministers, from Winston Churchill, born in 1874, to Liz Truss, born in 1975, whom she appointed two days before she died. Almost nothing from their weekly audiences ever leaked. There were crises: 1992, her *annus horribilis*, brought three collapsing royal marriages and a fire at Windsor Castle, after which she agreed to pay income tax; the death of Diana, Princess of Wales, in 1997 and the family's muted first response caused the sharpest public anger of her reign. But by her Platinum Jubilee in 2022 she was perhaps the most familiar woman in the world. In 2013 parliament ended male preference in the succession for anyone born after 28 October 2011. She died at Balmoral on 8 September 2022, aged 96.

### Charles III: the king who waited

Her son Charles III was the longest-serving heir apparent in British history, and at 73 the oldest person ever to accede to the British throne. He was crowned in Westminster Abbey on 6 May 2023, in a ceremony that combined the ancient rites, including the anointing that goes back to Edgar at Bath in 973, with a shorter service and a role for faiths other than Christianity. His wife, Camilla, was crowned as queen.

![King Charles III](/assets/essays/heavy-is-the-head/images/charles-iii.jpg)

As Prince of Wales, Charles was a more outspoken figure than his mother. He championed environmentalism, organic farming and traditional architecture decades before they were mainstream concerns, founded the Prince's Trust in 1976, which has helped more than a million young people into work and training, and lobbied ministers so energetically through letters, which became known as the "black spider memos" after his handwriting, that their publication was the subject of a long legal battle. As king, he has largely kept his opinions to himself, as constitutional practice demands. In February 2024 the palace announced that he had been diagnosed with cancer, and he has continued to carry out a reduced but still extensive programme of duties. His heir is his elder son, William, Prince of Wales.

## The Scorecard: How They Got the Crown, and How They Lost It

Having run through more than a millennium at breakneck speed, it is worth stepping back to look at patterns. Leaving aside the disputed cases of Matilda and Lady Jane Grey, and counting William and Mary separately, England and Britain have had 42 monarchs since 1066.

How did they get the crown? Most inherited it, but that covers a multitude of sins. William I won it by conquest; Henry I and Stephen by getting to the treasury first; Henry IV, Edward IV, Richard III and Henry VII with armies. Several were children controlled by regents or uncles, which often ended badly for someone. And a striking number owed their crowns to parliament: Henry IV, whose usurpation it ratified; William and Mary, invited in on conditions; and George I and every monarch since, whose right rests on the Act of Settlement of 1701. Charles III is descended from both Alfred the Great and William the Conqueror, but his title rests on an act of parliament.

How did they lose it? The table below summarises the more eventful exits since the Conquest.

| Monarch | How the reign ended |
|---|---|
| William II (1100) | Killed by an arrow while hunting |
| Richard I (1199) | Died of a crossbow wound at a siege |
| Edward II (1327) | Deposed; almost certainly murdered |
| Richard II (1399) | Deposed; probably starved to death |
| Henry VI (1461, 1471) | Deposed twice; murdered in the Tower |
| Edward V (1483) | Declared illegitimate; disappeared |
| Richard III (1485) | Killed in battle at Bosworth |
| Charles I (1649) | Tried and executed |
| James II (1688) | Fled; parliament declared he had abdicated |
| Edward VIII (1936) | Abdicated |

The trend is striking. Before 1500, holding the English crown was dangerous; five of the eighteen kings between 1066 and 1485 were deposed or killed in battle, not counting the three who died of wounds, accidents or suspicious hunting incidents. After 1688, no British monarch has been removed by force. The reason is the same reason the crown survived at all: power steadily moved from the monarch to parliament, and the monarch moved from being the government to being above it. A king who rules can be blamed and overthrown. A king who reigns, but does not rule, is much harder to get rid of, because there is less reason to.

## What Is the Crown For Now?

Formally, the king still does a great deal. He is head of state of the United Kingdom and of more than a dozen other Commonwealth countries, including Canada, Australia and New Zealand. He opens parliament with the King's Speech (written by the government), gives royal assent to laws, appoints the prime minister, and can in theory dissolve parliament, declare war and grant pardons. In practice, these prerogative powers are exercised on ministers' advice. No monarch has refused royal assent since Queen Anne in 1708, and the prime minister is whoever can command the confidence of the Commons. His real influence lies where Bagehot put it: in the weekly private audience with the prime minister, and the right to be consulted, to encourage and to warn.

The royal family also carries out thousands of engagements a year, supports hundreds of charities and hosts state visits. The Sovereign Grant that funds official duties is set as a share of the profits of the Crown Estate, whose income has gone to the Treasury since George III surrendered it in 1760 in return for a fixed allowance. Critics say the system is expensive and undemocratic; supporters say a head of state above politics, who never has to be elected or bought, provides continuity no president could. Polls have consistently shown most Britons in favour of keeping it, though support is much weaker among the young.

## Conclusion: The Crown That Learnt to Bend

Back at the pub quiz, table four has conceded the point about Æthelstan and moved on to whether Lady Jane Grey counts.

"She was proclaimed queen."

"She was never crowned. Neither was Edward VIII."

"Edward VIII definitely counts. He's on the stamps."

"He's on *some* stamps."

The quizmaster has gone home.

Looking back over all of them, what stands out is how much the monarchy changed, and how much of that change was forced upon it. The early kings were warlords. Then came the builders of the state: Æthelstan's councils, William's Domesday Book, Henry II's courts. And the people who paid for royal ambitions, first the barons and later the Commons, kept demanding limits in return: Magna Carta, the first parliaments, the Bill of Rights. Monarchs who refused those limits, such as John, Richard II, Charles I and James II, tended to come to bad ends. Those who accepted them, such as Edward III, Elizabeth I, Victoria and Elizabeth II, tend to be remembered fondly.

The country got a great deal from them: a united kingdom, the common law, cathedrals and castles, the King James Bible, the Bank of England and a mostly peaceful passage from personal rule to parliamentary democracy. It also got civil wars, crushing taxes, religious persecution, the expulsion of the Jews, the conquest of Wales and Ireland, and an empire whose legacy is still argued over. The monarchs themselves got wealth, glory and splendid tombs, or exile, prison and an early grave, and every one of them got the thing no monarch escapes: the judgement of history.

The British crown's greatest trick was to give power away without giving itself away. It survived Vikings, Normans, the Wars of the Roses, the Reformation, civil war, a regicide, a revolution, a German dynasty, two world wars and the end of an empire by bending rather than breaking. Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown, as Shakespeare's Henry IV complained. But for three centuries the heads that have worn this one have rested rather more easily, precisely because the crown itself has become so much lighter.
