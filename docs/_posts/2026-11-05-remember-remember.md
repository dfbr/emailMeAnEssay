> *Remember, remember the fifth of November,*
> *Gunpowder treason and plot.*

*A note before we begin. This is a story. Tom Ashdown, his family and his employers do not exist, and nobody knows what the next twenty years hold; the future in these pages is a guess, made as carefully as I could make it. But everything presented as history, or as fact about the world in 2026, is true to the best of my knowledge, and where the story leans on research, the research is real. Think of it as a novel with footnotes that have been allowed out into the main text.*

## 2026: The Undercroft

The bangers started at teatime, as they always do in Lewes, and by seven o'clock the town smelled of paraffin and cordite and frying onions.

Tom Ashdown, aged forty-seven, stood on a wall in Cliffe High Street with his son Ollie on his shoulders and his daughter Maisie pressed against his hip, and tried not to look at his phone. It buzzed anyway. He looked anyway.

*P2 incident auto-contained. Credential stuffing against customer portal. 1,412 attempts blocked. Summary attached. No action required.*

No action required. He read the summary, which was good, better than he would have written at the end of a twelve-hour shift, and put the phone away.

"Was that work?" said Priya, his wife, who could tell by the set of his shoulders.

"It was the robot telling me it had done my job for me."

"Well, that's nice of it."

"Is it?"

Below them the first procession was coming down the hill: hundreds of people in striped smugglers' jerseys, Tudor ruffs and monks' habits, carrying flaming torches, dragging burning tar barrels, beating drums. Behind them came seventeen burning crosses, one for each of the Protestant martyrs burned in Lewes in the reign of Mary I. The heat came off the torches in waves. Ollie, who was seven, gripped Tom's ears like the handlebars of a bicycle.

"Dad," he shouted, "why are we burning a man?"

"We're not burning a man. We're burning a guy. It's a sort of scarecrow."

"Why?"

And so, standing on a wall with a seven-year-old on his head, Tom Ashdown, Head of Security Operations at a mid-sized insurance company in the Midlands that he had visited in person perhaps four times, told the story of the Gunpowder Plot. He told it, because he could not help himself, as an incident report.

![Engraving of eight of the Gunpowder Plot conspirators](/assets/essays/remember-remember/images/conspirators.jpg)

*Eight of the thirteen Gunpowder Plot conspirators, with Guy Fawkes third from the right, in a contemporary engraving.* Engraving: Crispijn van de Passe the Elder, c. 1605, [Public domain](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gunpowder_Plot_conspirators.jpg), via Wikimedia Commons.

### The first incident report

The facts, which are not in dispute, are these. In 1605 a small group of English Catholics, furious that James I had not eased the persecution of their faith, decided to blow up the House of Lords at the State Opening of Parliament, killing the king, much of the aristocracy and most of the government in one go. The leader was Robert Catesby. The man left in charge of the explosives was a Yorkshireman and veteran of the Spanish army in the Low Countries called Guy Fawkes, who was going by the name of John Johnson.

"That's identity fraud," said Tom. "Fake name. He was pretending to be a servant."

The plotters had rented an undercroft, a ground-floor cellar, directly beneath the House of Lords' chamber. Into it they moved thirty-six barrels of gunpowder, hidden under firewood and coal.

"That's an insider threat," said Tom. "Somebody on the inside renting them the space. Well, sort of."

"Dad," said Maisie, who was ten and had heard this before, "nobody else talks like this."

Then, on 26 October, ten days before Parliament was due to open, an anonymous letter was delivered to a Catholic peer, Lord Monteagle, warning him to find an excuse not to attend, because Parliament would "receive a terrible blow". Nobody knows for certain who wrote it. Monteagle took it straight to Robert Cecil, the king's chief minister.

"That," said Tom, with real enthusiasm, "is a threat intelligence tip-off. Somebody sees something, says something. Classic."

The authorities waited. On the night of 4 November they searched the cellars beneath Parliament, and shortly before midnight a party led by Sir Thomas Knyvet found Fawkes guarding the firewood, with matches and fuses on him. He was taken to the king. He was taken to the Tower. On the king's written authority he was tortured, and his signature on his confession, wavering and barely legible, still survives in the National Archives, next to a confident one written days before. He died on the scaffold in January 1606.

"That's the bit where you'd say lessons learned," said Maisie.

"Lessons learned," said Tom. "Search your cellars. They still do, you know. Before every State Opening of Parliament, the Yeomen of the Guard go down with lanterns and search the basement. It's a ceremony now. But they still do it."

"Because of the man?"

"Because of the man. Four hundred and twenty years later. That's good security, that is. Belt and braces."

Parliament promptly passed an act requiring an annual thanksgiving for the king's deliverance on 5 November, and people lit bonfires, and they have been lighting them ever since, long after the act was repealed in 1859. Lewes, which has its own reasons involving the martyrs and a fierce Protestant memory, does it louder than anywhere else in Britain, with seven bonfire societies and tens of thousands of visitors, and effigies of whichever public figures have most annoyed the townsfolk that year.

"So the bonfire," said Ollie, slowly, from on top of his father's head, "is because of the security guards."

"That's one way of putting it."

"Is that what you do? Find the man in the basement?"

"Sort of. I find the people trying to get into the basement. On computers."

"Does the robot find them now?"

Tom thought about the message on his phone. *No action required.*

"Some of them," he said. "Not all of them."

"Will the robot take your job?"

Maisie looked up at him. Priya looked at him too, more carefully than either child.

"No," said Tom. "There's always going to be bad guys, and they've got robots too. Somebody's got to be in charge of the robots. Look, here comes the big one."

The Cliffe society's tableau was coming down the street: an enormous papier-mâché effigy of a grinning chrome robot holding a P45. The crowd roared and jeered. Somebody set off a rook scarer. Ollie screamed with delight and Tom's ears rang, and the question went up the chimney of the night with the sparks.

He was right about the bad guys. He was half-right about the rest.

## The view from 2026

It is worth pausing to describe the world Tom lived in, because it is our world, and because the story only works if we are honest about it.

By the autumn of 2026, artificial intelligence systems could write working software, draft legal contracts, summarise medical notes, translate between dozens of languages, produce convincing images and video, and hold fluent conversations about almost anything. The most capable of them could be set a task in the morning, such as investigating a suspicious login, writing a report, fixing a bug or researching a market, and left to get on with it, using tools much as a junior employee would. Nobody serious claimed they were flawless. They made mistakes, sometimes confidently. But they were cheap, fast, tireless and getting better on a timescale of months rather than decades.

In security operations, Tom's trade, the effect was already visible. A security operations centre, or SOC, is in essence a room (these days often a virtual one) full of people watching alerts. Every organisation of any size generates thousands of security alerts a day: a login from an odd country, a file that looks like malware, a server talking to an address it shouldn't. Most are noise. A few are disasters in the making. The traditional SOC has tiers: junior "tier-one" analysts triage the flood, escalating anything interesting to more experienced "tier-two" and "tier-three" staff, who investigate and respond. Tier one was always the entry point to the profession, the place where you learned what normal looked like so that you could spot abnormal. It was also, unfortunately, exactly the kind of work that AI systems turned out to be good at: high volume, pattern-heavy, documented, and checkable.

There was a well-known paradox here. For years the industry had complained of a skills shortage. ISC2, a professional body, estimated in 2024 that the world was short of some 4.8m cybersecurity workers. Surely, people said, a field that short of humans could absorb any amount of automation? Perhaps. But a shortage of experienced people is not the same thing as a demand for inexperienced ones, and the work that inexperienced people did was the work being automated first.

Economists were watching for exactly this. In August 2025 researchers at Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, led by Erik Brynjolfsson, published a paper using payroll data from millions of American workers. They found that since late 2022, when generative AI arrived in earnest, employment for 22- to 25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI, such as software development and customer service, had fallen by around 13% relative to less-exposed jobs, while employment for older workers in the same occupations had held up or grown. They called the paper "Canaries in the Coal Mine". The canaries were the young.

This is the first thing to understand about how automation tends to work, and it is the thread that runs through the rest of Tom's story. Machines rarely take jobs whole. They take *tasks*. A job is a bundle of tasks, and when a machine can do some of them, three things can happen. The job can shrink, as fewer people are needed to do what is left. The job can change, as people spend their time on the tasks the machine cannot do. Or the job can grow, because the machine has made the whole thing so much cheaper that demand explodes. Which of these happens depends on details: how much of the bundle the machine can do, how elastic the demand is, and who owns the machine.

Tom, standing on a wall in Lewes, believed his job would change. He was correct for about eighteen months.

## 2027: The Agent Supervisor

The restructuring was announced in March, in a video call, by a chief operating officer whose camera was angled slightly too low.

The security operations team, which had been twelve people, would be five. The tier-one function would be "fully agentic". The remaining staff would be retitled. Tom's new job title was Agent Supervisor and Escalation Lead, which Maisie, now eleven, pronounced "Agent Supervisor and Escalator Lead" and would not stop.

"You supervise escalators," she said, at dinner.

"I supervise agents. AI agents. Little programs that go off and do things."

"Like spies?"

"Like very fast, very literal interns."

The thing about the agents, which Tom tried and failed to explain to anyone who did not work with them, was that they were extraordinarily good and occasionally alarming. In an average week they closed several thousand alerts, wrote clean incident summaries, opened tickets, isolated infected laptops and wrote, overnight, the kind of threat report that used to take a human analyst three days. Their error rate on routine triage was lower than the junior analysts' had been. Tom had checked, partly from professional duty and partly in the faint hope of finding something damning.

What they lacked was harder to put into a spreadsheet. They did not know that the finance director always logged in from Portugal in August because he had a villa there. Or rather, they did, once you told them, but nobody had thought to tell them, because it was the sort of thing that lived in someone's head. They did not get a prickle on the back of the neck. And twice that year an agent did something perfectly reasonable that was also wrong: once it locked out the entire claims department because a new piece of software looked, to its eye, like an intruder; once it accepted, as genuine, an urgent request that had been very cleverly forged.

"Who gets blamed?" Priya asked, after the claims department incident, which had made the local paper.

"Me," said Tom. "That's the job now. I'm the person who gets blamed."

He said it as a joke, but it was the truest description of his new role he would ever come up with. In 2027 the machines could do much of the work. What they could not do was *carry responsibility*. A regulator could not fine an agent. A board could not sack one. When the insurers who insured the insurer asked who was accountable for security decisions, the answer had to be a person with a name. Tom was that name.

### Meanwhile, at the kitchen table

The children's lives were changing faster than his, and with less fuss.

Maisie had started secondary school in Lewes that September, and her homework now arrived through a platform with an AI tutor built in. It would not do her homework for her. Her school had made sure of that, after a term in which a startling number of Year 8 history essays had used the word "tapestry". But it would explain, patiently and at midnight, why the Corn Laws mattered or how to factorise a quadratic, and then ask her questions until it was satisfied she understood. Her maths improved noticeably. Her handwriting, which she now used only in exams, deteriorated.

Ollie, eight, had stopped asking adults questions. He asked the speaker in the kitchen. He asked it why the sky was blue, what the biggest dinosaur was and whether sharks could smell blood from a mile away (an exaggeration, the speaker told him, though they are very good at it). He asked it to tell him stories in which he was the hero. He asked it, once, while Tom was in the room, whether his dad's job was safe.

"I can't know that," said the speaker, which Tom thought was the most sensible thing anyone had said about it all year.

## The Luddites were not stupid

On Bonfire Night 2027 the Cliffe society burned an effigy of a tech billionaire riding a robot horse, and Tom found himself in the pub afterwards, talking to a retired history teacher called Graham who wore a hat with a pheasant feather in it.

"You know what they'd have called you two hundred years ago?" said Graham. "A Luddite."

"I'm not against technology. I work in technology."

"Neither were they. That's the thing nobody understands about the Luddites."

Graham was right, and it is worth a detour to explain why, because the Luddites are the ghosts who haunt every argument about automation, usually summoned by people who do not know much about them.

![The Leader of the Luddites, a coloured print of 1812](/assets/essays/remember-remember/images/luddites.jpg)

*"The Leader of the Luddites", a satirical print published in 1812, at the height of the machine-breaking.* Print: artist unknown, 1812, [Public domain](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Luddite.jpg), via Wikimedia Commons.

The Luddite rebellion began in Nottinghamshire in 1811 and spread through the textile districts of the Midlands and the north of England until about 1816. Its members were skilled workers: framework knitters, croppers who finished woollen cloth, hand-loom weavers. They took their name from a mythical leader, "Ned Ludd" or "General Ludd", who supposedly lived in Sherwood Forest, and they wrote threatening letters in his name. At night, in disciplined, masked bands, they broke into workshops and smashed machines.

But they did not smash all machines. In the hosiery trade they targeted the frames of masters who cut wages, who hired unapprenticed workers, or who used wide frames to make cheap, shoddy "cut-ups", stockings sewn from cut pieces of fabric that fell apart and gave the trade a bad name. Their complaints were about pay, quality, skill and the breaking of customs that had protected workers for generations. The machines were the target because the machines were what you could reach.

The government responded with enormous force. The Frame Breaking Act of 1812 made machine-breaking a capital crime. Thousands of soldiers, by some counts around 12,000, were deployed to the disturbed districts. In January 1813, after a mill owner was murdered, more than a dozen men were hanged at York. The movement was crushed.

"So they lost," said Tom.

"In the short run, completely," said Graham. "And here's the bit that keeps me up at night. In the short run, they were *right*."

This is the uncomfortable truth economists have spent the past few decades reconstructing. The early Industrial Revolution was an astonishing machine for producing output. It was a much worse machine for raising ordinary people's wages. The economic historian Robert Allen has called the period from roughly the 1780s to about 1840 "Engels' pause", after Friedrich Engels, who documented the conditions of the Manchester working class in 1845. During those decades, output per worker in Britain rose substantially, while real wages for ordinary workers barely moved. The gains went into profits, which were reinvested in more factories. The hand-loom weavers, perhaps a quarter of a million of them at their peak, saw their earnings collapse over a generation as power looms spread, and their trade did not so much end as starve.

Only from the 1840s did wages begin to rise steadily, and when they did, they rose for a very long time. The grandchildren of the Luddites lived longer, ate better and earned more than their grandparents could have dreamt of. But the Luddites themselves did not get to be their grandchildren.

"So which is it?" said Tom. "Does technology make everyone richer or doesn't it?"

"Both," said Graham. "Eventually. It depends what you mean by eventually. And it depends who's holding the machine."

"That's not very helpful."

"I'm a historian," said Graham. "We're not paid to be helpful. We're paid to be right after the event."

## 2029: Redundant

The email came on a Monday in September, which Tom later thought was at least efficient: a whole week to get used to it.

The company had been bought. The new parent, a much larger insurer, ran its security operations from a single centre with a handful of human staff, and the handful did not need a supervisor of escalators in Sussex. Tom was offered a decent redundancy payment, an outplacement service (which was, inevitably, an AI coach) and a warm, slightly embarrassed call from his manager, who would herself be gone by the spring.

He was fifty. Ollie was ten, Maisie thirteen.

He told the children at the kitchen table. He had rehearsed it, with the AI coach as it happens, which had been very good about tone.

"It's not because I did anything wrong. Lots of people's jobs are changing. We'll be fine. Mum's still working. I've got savings and the redundancy. And I'll find something else."

"Is it because of the robots?" said Ollie.

"Partly. Partly because the company got bought."

"I *told* you," said Ollie, who had not told him, but felt he had.

Maisie, who was thirteen and had developed a teenager's appetite for injustice, was outraged. "That's so unfair. You trained the robots. You literally taught them. And then they sacked you."

There was no answer to that, because it was true. Tom had spent two years feeding his knowledge into the agents: the finance director's villa, the prickle on the back of the neck turned into rules and examples and corrections. He had been, as an article he read that winter put it, the last generation of experts asked to teach their replacements.

### What the numbers said

Tom spent that autumn doing what unemployed people with spreadsheets do: reading. He read about the economics of automation, and he discovered that economists had been arguing about his situation for a long time.

He found the famous 2013 paper by Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael Osborne of Oxford, which had estimated that 47% of American jobs were at "high risk" of computerisation over the following decade or two. It had been enormously influential and widely misunderstood: the authors were estimating which jobs were technically automatable, not predicting how many would actually vanish, and in the decade after it was published, employment in rich countries rose to record highs. The robots, it seemed, had come later and differently than predicted, and for different people. Frey and Osborne had thought the safest jobs were those requiring creativity and social intelligence. Generative AI, inconveniently, turned out to be rather good at the first and passable at imitating the second.

He found a 2024 paper by James Feigenbaum and Daniel Gross that he found more useful than any prediction, because it was about something that had already happened. In the early 20th century, being a telephone operator was one of the commonest jobs for young American women. Every call was connected by hand: a voice said "Number, please", and a woman plugged a cable into a switchboard. From the 1920s, the Bell System began converting cities to mechanical dial switching, town by town. Because the conversions happened at different times in different places, the economists could compare what happened to similar women in converted and unconverted towns.

![Women operating a telephone switchboard](/assets/essays/remember-remember/images/switchboard.jpg)

*Women at work on the switchboard of the US Capitol. For decades, connecting calls by hand was one of the commonest jobs for young women in America and Britain.* Photo: Marion S. Trikosko, U.S. News & World Report collection, Library of Congress, [Public domain](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Women_working_at_the_U.S._Capitol_switchboard,_Washington,_D.C._04925v.jpg), via Wikimedia Commons.

What they found was two stories at once. For the next generation of young women, automation of operators made remarkably little difference: they simply went into other jobs, notably secretarial work and the expanding service sector, and their employment held up. But the women *already working* as operators when their exchange was converted did worse. Years later they were more likely to be out of the labour force, or in lower-paid work. The labour market adjusted beautifully for those entering it. It adjusted badly for those already in it, especially older workers.

"That's me," said Tom, to nobody, at two in the morning. "I'm an incumbent."

He found out about the human computers, too. For much of the 20th century "computer" was a job title, held largely by women doing calculations by hand or with mechanical calculators. At the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics, NASA's predecessor, rooms full of them worked out the aerodynamics of wing designs. When electronic computers arrived in the 1950s and 1960s, the job title disappeared. Some of the human computers became the first programmers, Katherine Johnson famously among them; others did not. The word survived. The job became the name of the thing that replaced it.

And he found, in the evenings, a strange comfort in all of it. Not because history said everything would be fine. It didn't. But because it said that what was happening to him was neither unprecedented nor personal. It had happened to weavers and croppers and switchboard operators and lamplighters. It had a shape. And a shape, he thought, was something you could work with.

## 2029, continued: Penny for the guy

Bonfire Night that year fell on a Monday. Tom, who had nowhere to be on Tuesday morning, stayed out late.

Ollie, ten, had built a guy for the first time, out of Tom's old work trousers and a hoodie stuffed with newspaper, with a face Ollie had drawn and then, after some thought, given a lanyard reading AGENT SUPERVISOR. He had wanted to sit outside the Co-op with it and collect a penny for the guy, the way children did in the old photographs, until Priya pointed out that nobody had used pennies since about 2021.

"Can people tap?"

"People can tap," said Tom, "but I'm not having you set up a card reader outside the Co-op."

They burned it in the back garden in a metal bin instead, with sparklers. Ollie was enormously proud of it. Tom watched his old trousers catch and found, slightly to his surprise, that he felt fine about it.

"Are you sad?" said Maisie, who had come out to watch.

"About the trousers?"

"About your job."

He considered lying, and didn't.

"Some days. Not today. I keep thinking about the guards in the cellar. The Yeomen. They search the basement every year, and they've found nothing for four hundred years. And they still do it, because if they didn't, how would you know?"

"That's not about your job."

"It is a bit. I think the robots can do most of the searching now. But somebody's still got to decide whether to trust the search. And somebody's got to carry the lantern, so everyone knows someone's looked."

Maisie thought about this. "So you're going to be a Yeoman of the Guard?"

"In a manner of speaking."

![Children in Wales collecting money with a guy](/assets/essays/remember-remember/images/guy-collecting.jpg)

*Children in Bontnewydd, near Caernarfon, collecting for their guy. The sign reads, in Welsh, "a penny for the old guy".* Photo: Geoff Charles, National Library of Wales, [CC0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Children_from_Bontnewydd,_Caernarfon,_collecting_for_the_%E2%80%9CGuy%E2%80%9D_(15730938785).jpg), via Wikimedia Commons.

He meant it. Over the next six months, Tom did something he would later describe, in a talk to a room full of anxious middle-aged IT people, as "becoming the lantern". He set up on his own as a consultant, specialising in exactly the things he had learned that the agents could not do. Small firms, charities, GP practices and local councils were adopting AI systems faster than they understood them, and they needed someone to tell them what their agents were allowed to do, check that they had done it, test whether they could be tricked, and sign a piece of paper saying so. He became, in effect, a professional sceptic.

It paid about two-thirds of what his old job had. There were months when it paid less. Priya's salary, as practice manager at a GP surgery, carried them through several of them. But it was work, and it was his, and he discovered that after twenty-five years of being employed he rather liked it.

## 2030: Keynes's deadline

On Bonfire Night 2030 Tom gave a talk at the Lewes town library about a famous essay, because it had been exactly a century since it was published and someone had asked him to.

He had been invited because he had become, locally, The Man Who Knows About AI, which mostly meant that people stopped him in Waitrose to ask whether they should let their parents use chatbots for company (sometimes) or whether a video of their MP was real (usually not). The library had a projector and about thirty plastic chairs, and about forty people came, which meant some of them stood and the librarian was thrilled.

"In 1930," Tom began, "in the depths of the Great Depression, with unemployment soaring, the economist John Maynard Keynes wrote an essay called 'Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren'."

He clicked to a slide of Keynes, all moustache and long legs.

"Everyone around him was gloomy. Keynes was not. He said the world was suffering from what he called 'technological unemployment', a phrase he more or less made famous: we were discovering ways of saving labour faster than we could find new uses for labour. But he said this was a temporary phase of maladjustment. In the long run, he predicted, the standard of living in progressive countries would be between four and eight times higher in a hundred years. That is, by 2030."

He clicked again.

"And on that, he was right. In both Britain and America, real income per head has risen by something like that much, depending a bit on how you count. Keynes's grandchildren, which is to say us, are rich beyond the imagination of 1930."

A man at the back raised his hand. "Doesn't feel like it."

"Hold on to that thought," said Tom. "Because Keynes made a second prediction. He said that with all this wealth, the economic problem, the struggle for subsistence, would be essentially solved, and that people would work far less. He suggested three-hour shifts. A fifteen-hour week."

There was laughter.

"So what went wrong?" said the man at the back.

This was the part of the talk Tom had thought about most.

"Partly, we chose more stuff over more time. We wanted bigger houses, foreign holidays, better medicine, phones, things Keynes had never imagined. Partly the gains weren't shared evenly; some people work far less than in 1930, through longer education, retirement and holidays, while plenty of others work two jobs. And partly, I think, Keynes underestimated how much people get from work besides money. Status. Company. A reason to get up. He knew this, actually. He worried about it. He wrote that for the first time since creation, man would be faced with his real, permanent problem: how to use his freedom. He thought it might cause a 'general nervous breakdown'."

"Sounds about right," said someone, and people laughed again, more nervously.

"Here's why I'm telling you this," said Tom. "We're having this argument again, about AI. Some people say it'll be like every other technology: painful for some, a boom for most, and new jobs we can't imagine yet. Some say this time is different, because for the first time we've built a machine that does thinking, not just lifting, and there isn't another category of work for people to move into. Keynes's essay is a reminder that both things can be true at once. The total can go up enormously, and you can still have the problem of who gets it, and what everyone's supposed to do all day."

Afterwards, when people had drifted out towards the bonfire smoke, a woman in her twenties came up to him. She had been a junior copywriter at an agency in Brighton until the previous year.

"What would you tell your own kids to do?" she asked. "Honestly."

Tom thought about Maisie, who was fourteen and had announced that week that she wanted to be either a vet or a famous DJ, and Ollie, who was eleven and wanted to be a professional gamer.

"Honestly? I don't know. I'd tell them to be good at something with their hands, something with people, and something with machines, and to never stop learning. And I'd tell them that the person who checks the work is going to be more valuable than the person who does it."

"That's what everyone says."

"I know," said Tom. "That's why I'm worried it's wrong."

## 2032: The Mask

On the afternoon of 5 November 2032, Tom got a call from Ollie's school.

Ollie was thirteen, in Year 9, and had not, the head of year was keen to stress, done anything wrong. But a video had gone round the year group that morning, and Ollie was in it, and he had gone home at lunchtime and locked himself in his room.

The video showed Ollie, or what looked exactly like Ollie, in his school uniform, sobbing and begging another boy not to tell anyone something. It was twelve seconds long. It was entirely fake. It had been made, the school thought, by a boy in the year above, with a free app, from photos on a group chat, in about four minutes.

Tom knocked on his son's door for some time before it opened.

"Everyone's seen it," said Ollie. "Everyone. It doesn't matter that it's fake. They've all seen me crying."

"You weren't crying."

"They don't care."

Tom sat on the end of the bed. He had spent the past three years, professionally, explaining deepfakes to other people. He had told a parish council that, in early 2024, an employee in the Hong Kong office of the engineering firm Arup had been tricked into transferring around $25m after a video call in which the company's chief financial officer, and several colleagues, had all been convincing fakes. He had told GP practices how to verify a request that appeared to come from their own partners. He had a slide that said TRUST NOTHING, VERIFY EVERYTHING in red. None of it was any use to a thirteen-year-old boy whose humiliation had been manufactured from nothing and distributed to everyone he knew.

"You know what I do for work?" he said eventually.

"Check robots."

"I check whether things are true. That's what it's turned into, really. People pay me to tell them whether something's real. And the main thing I've learned is that the fakes aren't the problem. The fakes are just a tool. The problem is that people *want* to believe the fake, because it's funnier, or worse, or it fits what they already thought. The boy who made that video didn't need it to be convincing. He just needed it to exist."

"That makes it worse."

"I know. Sorry. I'm better with incident reports."

Ollie almost laughed.

They went to the bonfire that night anyway, because Priya said that not going would be letting the boy in Year 10 win. It was the first year Ollie wore a mask: a white, smiling Guy Fawkes mask with a thin moustache and pointed beard, bought from a stall on the high street. Tom watched him put it on, and understood exactly why.

### The face of anonymity

The mask Ollie wore has a curious history, and Tom told it to him on the walk down School Hill, because it was the sort of thing that helped.

For most of the four centuries since the plot, Guy Fawkes was the villain of the story: the guy on the bonfire, burned in effigy with gusto. Children paraded stuffed figures with grotesque painted faces through the streets. The stylised, smiling mask everyone now recognises was drawn by the illustrator David Lloyd for *V for Vendetta*, a graphic novel written by Alan Moore and first published in the early 1980s, in which an anarchist in a Fawkes mask fights a fascist British government. The 2005 film adaptation made the mask famous worldwide. In 2008 members of the online collective Anonymous wore it to street protests against the Church of Scientology, and from then on it became the global face of hackers, protesters and people who wanted to be seen without being identified. One of the odder footnotes is that the rights to the design belong to Warner Bros, which has reportedly received a licensing fee on official masks sold to anti-corporate protesters ever since.

"So the bad guy turned into the good guy," said Ollie, through the mask.

"Depends who you ask. He wanted to blow up the government. He'd have killed hundreds of people. But four hundred years later, he's a symbol for people who want to hide their faces from people more powerful than them."

"Like me."

"Like you. For tonight."

They walked on in the torchlight. Around them, perhaps a third of the teenagers in Lewes wore the same mask. It was, Tom reflected, the perfect symbol of the age they were entering: a world in which anyone's face could be borrowed, and the only defence was to have a face nobody could see.

The next year, the school introduced a policy under which every piece of media created on school devices carried an invisible provenance watermark, and Ollie's year sat through a two-hour assembly on synthetic media. It helped a little. What helped more was that by the spring, fake videos of almost every child in the school had circulated at some point, and they had become, like everything that becomes universal, boring. Nobody believed anything they saw any more. Tom was not sure that this was a victory.

## 2034: The Saturday bonfire

In 2034, 5 November fell on a Sunday, so Lewes, which has a rule about these things, held its bonfire on the Saturday, the 4th.

Maisie was eighteen and had, to her father's mild astonishment and her mother's great relief, decided not to go to university. She had started an apprenticeship as an electrician.

"It's the one thing the robots can't do," she had said, the previous spring, laying out her case at the kitchen table with a precision Tom recognised as his own. "Have you seen them try? There's videos. They've got humanoid robots that can fold laundry and sort parcels in warehouses, fine. But put one in a 1930s semi in Newhaven with a fuse box behind the boiler and asbestos in the ceiling and a dog, and it just stands there."

She was overstating it, but not by much, and she had put her finger on one of the stranger facts about artificial intelligence, one that roboticists had noticed long before Maisie was born.

In 1988 the roboticist Hans Moravec observed that it was comparatively easy to make computers perform at adult level on intelligence tests or at draughts, and very hard to give them the perception and mobility of a one-year-old. This became known as Moravec's paradox. The things humans find hard, like chess, calculus or legal drafting, turn out to be relatively easy for machines, because they are recent, narrow skills we learned deliberately. The things we find effortless, like walking across a cluttered room, picking up an egg or recognising a face in bad light, are fiendishly hard, because evolution spent hundreds of millions of years perfecting them and we have no idea how we do them.

By 2034, in Tom's story, AI had made remarkable progress on the second category as well. Robots worked in warehouses, on farms and in some hospitals, lifting patients and fetching supplies. But the physical world was messy, varied, and above all *expensive*. A robot that could rewire an old house safely would have to cost less than an electrician, be insured, be certified, be trusted around children and pets, and be able to cope with whatever the Edwardians had done to the wiring. Software could be copied for nothing. A robot had to be built, shipped, charged and repaired.

"So you're going to be a Luddite," said Ollie, who was fifteen and had heard about Graham.

"No, I'm going to be the person the Luddites' grandchildren paid to fix things. There's a difference."

Her apprenticeship was full of AI, of course. She wore glasses that overlaid wiring diagrams on to walls and an earpiece that could identify a 1960s consumer unit from its photo and talk her through its quirks. A senior electrician in Brighton told Tom, at a party, that apprentices now learned in two years what used to take four, because they had an expert on their shoulder at all times. The work hadn't been automated. It had been *amplified*.

"That's the bit people miss," Tom said to Priya that night, at the bonfire, shouting over the drums. "Everyone talks about AI replacing people. But in some jobs, it makes people more valuable, because one electrician can now do the work of two."

"Doesn't that mean you need fewer electricians?"

"Not if there's more work than electricians. The whole country's trying to rewire for heat pumps and car chargers and batteries. There's ten years of work."

Economists had a name for what Tom was describing, although he did not know it at the time. Whether a technology destroys or creates jobs in a given trade depends heavily on how demand responds when the work gets cheaper. The economist James Bessen had shown that when cash machines spread through American banks from the 1970s onwards, the number of tellers per branch fell sharply, but branches became so much cheaper to run that banks opened many more of them, and the total number of tellers held up for decades. It was only when people stopped needing branches at all, with mobile banking, that teller numbers fell. If demand is hungry enough, automation of part of a job can increase the number of people doing it. If demand is already satisfied, it simply shrinks the job.

### Priya's surgery

The trade nobody in the family had expected to be transformed was Priya's.

General practice in Britain had been short of doctors and drowning in paperwork for years. By the mid-2030s, in Tom's story, much of the paperwork had gone. AI systems listened to consultations and wrote the notes. They triaged online requests, sorted prescriptions, chased test results and answered the telephone in a calm voice at eight in the morning when the whole town rang at once. Priya's practice, which had employed six receptionists and administrators in 2026, employed two by 2034.

But it employed more nurses, two more physician associates and a social prescriber, and the doctors saw more patients for longer. The administrative savings had gone, as Priya put it, "into the bit with the people in it".

"The robots are better at the forms than we ever were," she said. "But Mrs Hennessy doesn't want a robot to tell her about her husband's scan results. She wants a person in a room."

This is an old idea in economics, older than computers. In the 1960s the economists William Baumol and William Bowen noticed that it takes as many musicians just as long to play a Beethoven string quartet as it did in Beethoven's day; no productivity improvement has ever been made to it. Yet musicians' wages rise along with everybody else's, because they could otherwise leave and do something else. The result, which came to be called "Baumol's cost disease", is that services where the human presence *is* the product, such as performance, teaching, nursing and care, get relatively more expensive over time as everything else gets cheaper. In a world of extremely cheap AI, the human parts of work were, if anything, becoming more valuable. The question was who would pay for them.

## 2036: The argument about the money

By 2036 the argument about AI and jobs had stopped being an argument about technology and become, as Graham the historian had predicted from his favourite chair in the pub, an argument about money.

This is the part of Tom's story where it is most important to remember that it is a story. Nobody knows how fast AI will spread through the economy, or how governments will respond. History suggests that the adoption of a powerful general technology is slower than the enthusiasts think and faster than the sceptics think. In a famous 1990 paper, "The Dynamo and the Computer", the economic historian Paul David pointed out that factories took decades to get the full benefit of electric motors, because they first had to be completely redesigned around them; for years, factory owners simply replaced their big steam engine with a big electric motor and wondered why nothing much had changed. Organisations change slowly. People change jobs slowly. Laws change slowest of all.

But in Tom's version of the 2030s, the change was fast enough to be politically explosive. Unemployment in Britain, after a long period of surprising resilience, had risen; it was not catastrophic, but it was concentrated among exactly the people who used to feel safe: graduates, office workers, middle managers, people in their forties and fifties with mortgages. Meanwhile the profits of the companies that owned the most capable AI systems were enormous, and much of the economy's output now flowed through a few of them. The 1830s had come back, with better broadband.

Politicians reached for old ideas. There were proposals to tax robots, an idea that Bill Gates had floated as far back as 2017, and proposals to tax the computing power AI systems used. There were arguments about a universal basic income: a regular, unconditional payment to every citizen. Both sides cited the same real-world experiment. Between 2017 and 2018 Finland had paid 2,000 randomly selected unemployed people aged 25 to 58 a tax-free €560 a month, with no conditions and no reduction if they found work. The results showed a small improvement in employment and a marked improvement in wellbeing, trust and confidence about the future. Supporters said this proved basic income worked. Opponents said a two-year trial for 2,000 unemployed Finns told you very little about paying every adult in Britain for ever. Both had a point.

There were arguments, too, about shorter working weeks. Tom liked to cite a real trial: in the second half of 2022, 61 British companies with around 2,900 staff had moved to a four-day week with no loss of pay. Of the 61, 56 kept going with it afterwards, and 18 made it permanent. Keynes's fifteen-hour week, it turned out, could be approached gradually, one day at a time.

Tom found himself, to his surprise, in demand as a speaker. Not because he had answers, but because he was a recognisable type, the mid-career professional whose job had been automated and who had come out the other side, and people liked hearing that it could be survived. He told the truth, which was that it could, if you had savings, a partner in work, a mortgage that was nearly paid off and some skills that happened to be in demand, and that he had been lucky to have all four.

"What about people who don't?" a woman asked him, at a conference in Birmingham.

"Then it's a political problem," said Tom, "not a personal one. And I'm a security consultant, not a chancellor."

That night, Bonfire Night 2036, a Wednesday, the Lewes procession included an effigy that the newspapers photographed and a lot of people shared: a giant figure of a Victorian mill owner in a top hat, his waistcoat buttons straining, sitting on a server rack and counting coins. The banner beneath it said REMEMBER, REMEMBER. Somebody had added, in smaller letters, WHO OWNS THE LOOM.

### The missing rung

That same autumn, the problem the Stanford economists had spotted a decade earlier came home to roost in Tom's own profession.

The security industry had automated away its entry-level jobs, and for ten years it had got away with it, because it still had people like Tom: seniors who had learned the trade the hard way, by spending years staring at alerts. But Tom's generation was retiring, and there was nobody behind them. You cannot hire a fifteen-year veteran who was never a one-year rookie.

Firms tried everything. Some created "synthetic apprenticeships", in which trainees learned on simulated attacks generated by AI. Some paid seniors like Tom to mentor juniors who would never, on paper, be economically necessary. Some simply let the AI make the senior decisions too, which was cheaper until it wasn't.

Tom took on two apprentices of his own, through a local college. One of them, a nineteen-year-old from Peacehaven called Jade, asked him on her first day why she should bother learning to read logs by hand when the agents could do it in a millisecond.

"Same reason a pilot still learns to land the plane," Tom said. "Autopilot does it nearly every time. You're there for the time it doesn't. And you can't recognise that time unless you've done it yourself."

He was rather pleased with that. Jade wrote it down. Two years later, she became the youngest person in the south-east to hold a certification in AI system assurance, and shortly afterwards she was earning more than he was.

## 2039: The one-person company

Ollie was twenty in 2039, and running a company.

He had gone to university in Manchester to study computer science and philosophy, an unusual combination that he had chosen on the grounds that, as he explained at Christmas, "one tells you how the machines work and the other tells you whether you should let them". He had lasted eighteen months. Then he dropped out, to the dismay of his mother, and started a business with two friends and, by his own count, about four hundred AI agents.

The business made personalised learning games for children with dyslexia. The agents wrote the code, designed the graphics, composed the music, tested the games, handled customer service in eleven languages, managed the marketing, did the accounts and filed the tax return. The three humans decided what to build, talked to parents and teachers, and argued.

For about a year it was spectacular. They had a hundred thousand users. A newspaper wrote a piece about "the twenty-year-olds running companies that used to need two hundred staff". Ollie appeared on a podcast and was insufferable about it at Sunday lunch.

Then it collapsed, for a reason that would have been obvious to any nineteenth-century economist. If three twenty-year-olds and four hundred agents could build a brilliant dyslexia app in six months, so could anyone. Within a year there were dozens of rivals, several of them free. Prices fell to nothing. The thing that had made Ollie's company possible, the fact that the work had become nearly costless, was the thing that destroyed its profits. When anything can be made cheaply by everyone, the money flows to whatever remains scarce: trust, reputation, distribution, physical presence, and ownership of the machines themselves.

Ollie came home to Lewes for Bonfire Night that year, a Saturday, slightly deflated and wearing an expensive coat he could no longer afford.

"So what was scarce, in the end?" asked Tom, as they walked down to watch the Cliffe procession.

"Teachers," said Ollie. "That's the stupid thing. Every school that used our app, the ones where it worked, it was because there was a teacher who knew the kid and made them use it and sat with them. The app was the cheap bit. The teacher was the bit that mattered."

Tom said nothing, which was one of the things he had got better at.

"I've applied for teacher training," said Ollie. "Primary. Don't make a thing of it."

"I would never make a thing of it."

"You're making a thing of it with your face."

## 2039, continued: Sixty

Tom turned sixty that year.

He had not expected, at forty-seven, standing on a wall with a seven-year-old on his head, that sixty would look like this. He worked three days a week, at his own rate, for clients he chose. Most of his work consisted of being the human who looked at what AI systems had done and said whether it could be trusted, a job that did not exist when he started his career and that would not, he suspected, exist in the same form when he retired. He earned less, in real terms, than he had at his peak. He was, by most measures, happier.

He knew he was fortunate. Several of the people from his old team had not landed so softly. One had retrained as a plumber and loved it. One had become a sort of travelling digital handyman for elderly people, setting up their devices and protecting them from scams, and made a modest living. One had not worked properly since 2029, and Tom met him for a pint twice a year and felt, each time, the particular guilt of the survivor.

And the town had changed. The high street had more cafés and fewer shops, more tattoo parlours and craft workshops and physiotherapists, more places where the point was the person doing the thing. The bank had closed. The library, oddly, was busier than it had been in 2026, full of people who were not sure what to do with their days and liked to do it among other people. The bonfire societies were thriving; membership was up, and the procession took longer every year. Graham the historian, now in his late eighties, said it reminded him of the 1930s, when people who had been thrown out of work by the Depression had taken up rambling, choirs and allotments in enormous numbers.

"Keynes was right about one thing," Graham said, in the pub, with the pheasant feather drooping from a newer hat. "When people stop being needed for work, they don't sit still. They find something to belong to."

"And if they can't?"

"Then they find somebody to burn," said Graham, and nodded at the window, through which, at that moment, the effigies of the year's most unpopular billionaires were passing in flames.

## 2042: The letter in the dark

The worst incident of Tom's career happened when he was sixty-three and supposed to be winding down.

It began, in his story, on the evening of 4 November 2042, four hundred and thirty-seven years to the night since Sir Thomas Knyvet went down into the undercroft with a lantern. A regional water company in the south of England, which used AI agents to run much of its treatment network, was hit by an attack that had itself been planned and executed largely by AI. The attackers' systems had spent months studying the company: its staff, its suppliers, its software, the voices of its managers. At six in the evening, the night-shift operators at two treatment works received calls from their duty manager, in his own voice, with his own turns of phrase, instructing them to accept an emergency software update. At the same time, the company's own monitoring agents were fed a carefully constructed stream of false data indicating that everything was normal.

It almost worked. What stopped it was not a machine. It was a sixty-one-year-old process operator called Bernie, who had worked at the plant for thirty-four years, who took the call, listened to his manager's voice telling him to hurry, and said: "Hang on, Steve, isn't it your daughter's wedding today?"

There was a pause on the line. A very slightly too-long pause.

Bernie put the phone down, walked to the control room, and physically disconnected the network cable from the system that would have received the update. Then he rang Steve on his personal mobile, and Steve, who was indeed at his daughter's wedding reception in a marquee in Kent, said a word that was later redacted from the official report.

Tom was one of the people called in that night to help work out what had happened, and he spent Bonfire Night 2042 not in Lewes but in a portable building in a car park, surrounded by screens and empty coffee cups, reading the attack logs. At about two in the morning, with fireworks still crackling faintly in the distance, he realised what he was looking at.

"It's the Monteagle letter," he said to the young analyst beside him, who had not heard of the Monteagle letter.

"The what?"

"The Gunpowder Plot wasn't stopped by a search. Well, it was, in the end. But the search only happened because somebody noticed something that didn't fit and took it to someone who'd listen. A letter that was a bit odd. That's what saved the day. Not the guards. The person who noticed."

"Bernie," said the analyst.

"Bernie," said Tom. "Bernie's the letter."

### The value of the person who notices

The water company incident, in the world of the story, changed the law. A year later, Parliament passed rules requiring that certain critical decisions in essential services, such as water, power, hospitals and the payment system, could not be authorised by an AI system alone, and that a named, trained, accountable human must be in the loop, with the right and the duty to stop the machine. Critics called it a job-creation scheme for the middle-aged. Supporters called it a fire door. Tom thought it was both, and that there was nothing wrong with either.

It is worth saying plainly that this is invention. But the principle behind it is not. Safety-critical industries have long insisted on human accountability even where machines do most of the work. Commercial aircraft can largely fly themselves, and yet nobody has seriously proposed removing the pilots, partly because pilots handle the rare moments that automation cannot, and partly because passengers want to know that somebody on board shares their fate. A machine that is right 99.99% of the time is a marvel. In a system that runs millions of times a day, it is also a guarantee of disaster somewhere, unless something catches the other 0.01%.

The deeper lesson, which Tom repeated in every talk he gave for the rest of his life, was that the most valuable thing Bernie brought was not knowledge. The AI systems knew far more about water treatment than he did. It was *context*: thirty-four years of knowing who people were, what they would and would not do, whose daughter was getting married. It was the kind of knowledge that lives in the gaps between documents. It was, in short, the finance director's villa in Portugal, and it was precisely the kind of knowledge that disappears from an organisation when you stop employing the people who carry it.

Maisie, who was twenty-six that year and ran her own small electrical firm with three employees and a great many AI assistants, put it more bluntly when he told her about it over Sunday lunch.

"So the robots were cleverer than Bernie, but Bernie knew Steve."

"That's about it."

"Everyone was so worried about whether the machines would be clever," she said. "Nobody worried about whether there'd be anyone left who knew Steve."

## 2046: Remember, remember

Bonfire Night 2046 fell on a Monday, and it rained, which is traditional.

Tom Ashdown was sixty-seven. Under the law as it stood back in 2026, someone born in 1979 would not draw the state pension until sixty-eight, so he was, technically, still a working man. He had three clients left, all of whom he liked, and an honorary role at the local college, where he taught a course called "How to Be Sceptical About Machines", which was always full.

He stood on the same wall in Cliffe High Street. His knees told him about it.

Beside him stood Maisie, thirty, an electrical contractor whose firm now employed eleven people, four of them apprentices she had insisted on hiring whether or not the numbers strictly required it. "Somebody hired Dad's Jade," she had said when her accountant, an AI, had queried it. "Somebody has to hire mine." On Tom's shoulders, where Ollie had once sat, was Maisie's daughter Rosa, aged five, gripping his ears like handlebars. And on the other side of the street, in a high-visibility vest, was Ollie, twenty-seven, now a Year 3 teacher at a primary school in the town, marshalling a group of his pupils from the junior bonfire society, who were carrying lanterns and looking at the torches with exactly the expression Ollie had worn twenty years earlier.

The procession came down the hill: the smugglers' jerseys, the drums, the seventeen burning crosses. The smell of paraffin and cordite and frying onions. Some things had not changed at all. The tar barrels were still dragged by hand; the bonfire societies had debated using robots to do it, briefly and with great hilarity, and voted the idea down unanimously.

Rosa leaned down over Tom's head.

"Grandad, why are we burning a man?"

"It's a guy. It's a sort of scarecrow."

"Why?"

And so he told it again, the story of the cellar and the barrels and the letter, but he told it differently from the way he had told it in 2026. He did not call it an incident report. He told her about Lord Monteagle opening a strange letter, and about the people who went down into the dark with lanterns to look.

"Did a robot find the bad man?"

"No. There weren't any robots then. People found him."

"Do robots find the bad men now?"

"Mostly," said Tom. "They're very good at it. But somebody still has to go down with the lantern."

"Why?"

He thought about that for a while. The procession roared past. The effigy that year, made by the Cliffe society, was a towering figure of a smiling, featureless android in a business suit, holding a placard that read I'M SURE IT'S FINE. The crowd booed it with enormous affection.

"Because the robots don't mind what happens," said Tom eventually. "We do. You need somebody there who minds."

Rosa considered this. She was five, and had grown up in a world in which machines answered every question, drove the bus to nursery, read her stories in any voice she liked and could draw her a picture of a unicorn in a spacesuit before she'd finished asking.

"Will the robots take my job?" she said.

Maisie laughed out loud. Ollie, across the street, saw them laughing and waved a glowstick.

"What do you want to be?" said Tom.

"A dragon," said Rosa.

"Then no," said Tom. "I think you're quite safe."

## Afterword: What the story is for

Tom Ashdown's story is one guess among many. The future could be much faster than his, with AI systems doing almost all intellectual work within a decade and robots following soon after; in that world, the questions of who owns the machines and how the proceeds are shared would dominate everything else. It could be much slower, with AI proving more like electricity, transforming the economy over half a century, rather than more like a bomb in the cellar. It could be stranger than any of us can imagine. Anyone who tells you confidently which it will be is selling something, possibly an AI product.

But some features of Tom's story are not guesses. They are patterns that have recurred every time a powerful technology has changed how work is done, and they are worth carrying with you whatever happens.

**Machines take tasks before they take jobs.** Almost no occupation disappears overnight. Jobs are bundles of tasks; technology unpicks the bundle, and what is left over can shrink, grow or change beyond recognition. Bank tellers lasted decades after the cash machine. Local switchboard operators largely vanished wherever automatic exchanges arrived. The difference lay in how much of the job the machine could do, and how much more of the service people wanted once it became cheaper.

**The young adapt; the incumbents pay.** The clearest finding from the telephone operators, and arguably from the Stanford study of the 2020s, is that labour markets adjust fairly well for people entering them, and badly for people already in them. A society that wants to handle this well has to think hard about the forty-seven-year-olds, not just the seven-year-olds.

**The ladder needs a bottom rung.** When the entry-level tasks of a profession are automated, the profession must invent new ways to train beginners, or it will run out of experts within a generation. This is a problem for security, law, accountancy, medicine, journalism and many more, and it is one of the most important and least discussed consequences of the AI revolution.

**Output and wages can part company for a long time.** Engels' pause lasted roughly half a century. The Industrial Revolution eventually made ordinary Britons rich, but not those who lived through its first decades. Whether the gains from AI are widely shared will depend less on the technology than on politics: on taxation, competition, education, and on who owns the machines. The Luddites, in the end, were asking the right question about the wrong object.

**Human presence becomes more valuable, not less.** Baumol's cost disease suggests that as AI makes many things very cheap, the things that need a person in the room, such as care, teaching, nursing, craft, performance and judgement, will become relatively more precious. Whether those jobs are well paid is, again, a political choice.

**Trust is the scarce good.** When anything can be faked, the ability to say "this is real", and to be believed, becomes enormously valuable. That is true for security professionals and for teachers, journalists, doctors and electricians. In a world full of synthetic everything, accountability, reputation and provenance are what money will chase.

**Somebody has to carry the lantern.** Every year, before the State Opening of Parliament, the Yeomen of the Guard still search the cellars of the Palace of Westminster by lantern light. Strictly speaking it is a ceremony; modern security is done very differently. But the tradition carries a meaning that is worth keeping. A system is safe not just when nothing goes wrong, but when someone whose job it is to care has looked, and is answerable for what they found.

Keynes, in 1930, ended his essay about his grandchildren with a hope that economists might one day manage to get themselves thought of as humble, competent people, like dentists. Perhaps the right ambition for the age of AI is similar. Not to predict the future, which no one can do, but to be the kind of people, and the kind of society, that keeps a lantern lit in the cellar: curious, sceptical, attentive to the people the change is happening to, and willing, when something does not fit, to open the strange letter and take it to someone who will listen.

Remember, remember. It is, after all, what the bonfires are for.
