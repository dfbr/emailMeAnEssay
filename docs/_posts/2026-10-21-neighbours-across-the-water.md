## A Very Small Sea

Every year, in the first week of December, a large spruce tree is lowered onto a plinth in Trafalgar Square. It has travelled from the forests outside Oslo by lorry and ship, it is roughly twenty metres tall, and it is decorated in the Norwegian manner, which is to say with vertical strings of plain white lights and absolutely no tinsel. Londoners walk past it on their way to the shops. Some of them stop and read the plaque. A smaller number, usually after a mulled wine, announce to their friends that it looks a bit thin this year. At least one newspaper will agree with them. The tree does not mind. It has been coming since 1947, and it knows why it is there.

The plaque explains that the tree is a gift from the people of Oslo to the people of London, as thanks for Britain's help during the Second World War. That is true, and we shall come to it. But the tree is also the latest chapter in a relationship that is more than twelve hundred years old, and which began, it must be said, rather less politely. The first well-known Norwegian visitors to Britain did not bring trees. They brought axes, and they took the church silver.

Between those two moments lies one of the oddest, warmest and most consistently entertaining friendships in European history. Britain and Norway have raided each other, married each other, traded with each other, sheltered each other's kings, argued about fish, drilled the same seabed, raced each other to the bottom of the world, and repeatedly beaten each other at football in ways that the losing side remembers for decades. They have also quietly agreed, over and over again, about the things that matter: that kings should be answerable to parliaments, that people should be allowed to walk in the hills, that bullies should be stood up to, and that there is no such thing as bad weather, only the wrong coat.

The North Sea between them is not large. From Lerwick in Shetland to Bergen is a shorter journey than from Lerwick to London, and for long stretches of history it felt like it. The sea was not a wall; it was a road. Longships, herring boats, timber barges, salmon-fishing aristocrats, polar explorers, wartime fishing boats and oil platforms have all used it. It has been, at different times, a battleground, a marketplace, an escape route and a shared back garden with a gas pipeline running through it.

This essay is a cheerful history of that relationship. It is not a complete one: twelve centuries do not fit tidily into ten thousand words, and there are whole libraries on the Vikings alone. Instead, we shall wander through the highlights, paying particular attention to the moments when the two countries competed, collaborated or simply misunderstood each other in instructive ways. There will be kings and saints, chessmen and skis, a race to the South Pole, a penguin with a military rank, a football commentator who shouted at Margaret Thatcher, and a great deal of oil. Along the way we shall try to work out why two nations that began by fighting have ended up, by most measures, among the closest friends either of them has.

We begin, as all good stories about Norway and Britain must, on a beach, with somebody making a terrible mistake.

## Visitors Who Forgot to Knock

Around the year 789, three ships arrived at Portland on the Dorset coast. According to the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, the king's reeve, a man named Beaduheard, rode down to meet them. He seems to have assumed that they were traders, which was a reasonable assumption, because most people arriving on ships were traders, and he wanted them to come to the royal estate so that the proper taxes could be collected. Some versions of the Chronicle say that the men came from Hordaland, the region around modern Bergen. They killed him. The Chronicle notes, with the weary air of a writer who already knows how the next two centuries will go, that these were the first ships of Danish men to come to the land of the English. The chronicler was using "Danish" loosely, as the English would for a long time: anyone from Scandinavia with a boat and a bad attitude qualified.

Poor Beaduheard was the first British official to underestimate a Norwegian, and he would not be the last. But the event that really announced the Viking Age came four years later, on 8 June 793, when raiders fell upon the monastery at Lindisfarne, off the coast of Northumberland. Lindisfarne was one of the holiest places in Britain, the home of Saint Cuthbert and the workshop that had produced the Lindisfarne Gospels. The raiders killed monks, carried others off as slaves, and helped themselves to anything that glittered. The scholar Alcuin, writing from the court of Charlemagne, was appalled. Never before, he wrote, had such a terror appeared in Britain, and nobody had thought such a voyage from the sea was even possible. He then did what clergymen have done in every century since and suggested that the people of Northumbria had probably brought it on themselves by wearing fashionable haircuts.

It is worth pausing to appreciate the technology involved, because it is the first of many technological contests in this story. The Viking longship was one of the great engineering achievements of the early medieval world. It was clinker-built, meaning that its planks overlapped like the slats of a fence, which made it light and flexible enough to flex with the waves rather than fight them. It had a shallow draught, so it could cross the open sea and then row straight up a river or onto a beach. It had a sail and oars, so it was not at the mercy of the wind. In practical terms, this meant that a crew from western Norway could appear off the coast of Northumberland with very little warning, and then disappear again before anyone had found a horse. The Anglo-Saxons had excellent metalwork, beautiful manuscripts and a sophisticated church. What they did not have, in 793, was a navy that could do anything about it.

The raids that followed are often lumped together, but it helps to know that the Scandinavians who came to Britain were not one people with one plan. Broadly speaking, the Danes concentrated on eastern and southern England, where they eventually settled in large numbers and established the Danelaw. The Norwegians went north and west: to Shetland, Orkney, the Hebrides, the Isle of Man, the coasts of Ireland, and the Lake District and Lancashire coast. There was a good deal of overlap, and the Vikings themselves would have been puzzled by modern arguments over who counted as what. But it is fair to say that if you want to find Norwegian fingerprints on Britain, you should look north and west, and you should look at the islands.

In Orkney and Shetland, the Norwegians did not merely raid. They moved in, and they stayed for six hundred years. The islands became the Earldom of Orkney, a Norse lordship nominally under the Norwegian crown, and its rulers appear in the *Orkneyinga Saga*, a thirteenth-century Icelandic work that reads like a family drama written by a committee of very bloodthirsty novelists. The earls burned each other in halls, married strategically, went on crusade, and occasionally became saints. Earl Magnus Erlendsson was murdered by his cousin around 1117 and later canonised; his nephew, Earl Rognvald, built St Magnus Cathedral in Kirkwall in his honour in 1137. It still stands, made of red and yellow sandstone, and it is one of the finest medieval buildings in Scotland. It was, for most of its early life, a Norwegian cathedral.

The language of the islands was Norn, a descendant of Old Norse, and it survived in Shetland until the eighteenth century. It has left behind thousands of place names, and a dialect in which a great deal of the everyday vocabulary would make a modern Norwegian sit up and listen. Even on the British mainland, the Norse gave English an unusually generous present. Sky, egg, knife, skirt, window (from *vindauga*, the "wind eye"), law, husband, ugly, die, take and get all came into English from Old Norse. So did the pronouns *they*, *them* and *their*, which means that every modern argument about English pronouns is, at some level, conducted in borrowed Norwegian. In northern England and Scotland, people still say *bairn* for child and *kirk* for church, and they *ken* things. A farm is a *by*, which is why so much of Yorkshire ends in it, and a stream is a *beck*.

The Viking Age is usually said to end in 1066, and its last great moment is one of the most dramatic episodes in British history, though it is often forgotten because of what happened three weeks later. In September 1066, Harald Hardrada, King of Norway, landed in Yorkshire with a large army and a claim to the English throne. Hardrada was the closest thing the eleventh century had to an action hero. He had fought at the battle where his half-brother, Olaf, was killed; he had served in the Varangian Guard of the Byzantine emperor in Constantinople; he had campaigned in Sicily and Bulgaria; and he had come home, become king, and spent years fighting the Danes. He was, by all accounts, extraordinarily tall. He was joined by Tostig Godwinson, the exiled brother of the new English king, Harold, who had a grievance and a working knowledge of the local geography.

Hardrada won a battle at Fulford, outside York, and then waited at Stamford Bridge for hostages. What he got instead was Harold Godwinson and the English army, who had marched up from the south at astonishing speed and caught the Norwegians by surprise on 25 September, many of them without their armour. Snorri Sturluson's saga tells a lovely story about the moment before the battle. An English horseman rides up and offers Tostig his earldom back if he will change sides. Tostig asks what Hardrada will get. "Seven feet of English ground," comes the reply, "or as much more as he is taller than other men." Tostig declines. Afterwards, Hardrada asks who the eloquent little man was, and is told it was King Harold himself.

The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* adds another legend: a single, gigantic Norwegian held the narrow bridge over the River Derwent against the whole English army until an Englishman floated underneath it in a barrel and stabbed him from below. This is almost certainly too good to be true, but it does suggest that the English already had a healthy respect for large Norwegians standing in narrow places, which would prove useful during the football of the 1990s.

Hardrada was killed by an arrow in the throat, and the Norwegian army was destroyed. The survivors needed only a couple of dozen ships to go home, out of the hundreds in which they had come. It was, in a sense, the last great Viking invasion of England. It was also, for the English, a pyrrhic victory. Three days later, William of Normandy landed on the south coast, and Harold had to march his tired army all the way back down the country to meet him at Hastings. We all know how that went. It is just possible, if you enjoy counterfactuals, that the Norwegians decided the history of England after all: not by winning, but by making the English army walk to Yorkshire and back at the worst possible moment. There is a strong case that William the Conqueror owed Harald Hardrada a thank-you card.

The Normans themselves, of course, were descended from Scandinavian settlers in northern France. Their name is simply a softening of "Northmen". So the Norman Conquest, at one remove, was the last Viking invasion too. Britain, it seems, could not get away from them.

## Kings, Saints, Chessmen and the Longest Unpaid Bill in Europe

After 1066, the relationship between Britain and Norway became less about raiding and more about politics, which is to say it became less violent but more complicated. It also acquired some of its most charming artefacts and some of its most enduring loose ends.

Start with London Bridge. In 1014, according to the Norse sagas, a young Norwegian warrior named Olaf Haraldsson helped the English king, Æthelred the Unready, recapture London from the Danes. The Danes held the bridge across the Thames, so Olaf's men are said to have roofed their ships with planks for protection, rowed underneath it, tied ropes to its wooden piles, and then rowed hard downstream until the bridge collapsed. A skaldic poem about the battle, quoted by Snorri Sturluson, begins with a line usually translated as "London Bridge is broken down". Some people have argued that this is the origin of the nursery rhyme. Most historians think that the connection is too neat to be proven, which is what historians always say about the best stories. Olaf went home, became king of Norway, tried with great enthusiasm and very little gentleness to convert his countrymen to Christianity, was killed at the Battle of Stiklestad in 1030, and was swiftly declared a saint.

St Olaf became enormously popular in Britain, especially among sailors and merchants trading with Scandinavia. Several English churches were dedicated to him. One of them, St Olave's in Hart Street, survived the Great Fire of London and is where the diarist Samuel Pepys worshipped and is buried. Another gave its name, through centuries of Londoners mumbling "St Olave's Street", to Tooley Street near London Bridge. A Norwegian king who may or may not have pulled down London Bridge is now commemorated, more or less, by a street that runs past it. History has a sense of humour.

Further north, the Norwegian crown remained a serious player in Scottish affairs for another two centuries. King Magnus III of Norway campaigned across the Hebrides, the Isle of Man and the Irish Sea around 1098 and again in the early 1100s. He earned the nickname *Berrføtt*, "Barefoot", apparently because he and his men adopted the local Gaelic fashion of going about with bare legs. One saga story tells how, having agreed with the Scottish king that he could have any island he could sail around, he had his ship dragged across the narrow neck of land at Tarbert with himself sitting at the helm, and so claimed the Kintyre peninsula as an island. It is the kind of legal argument that would get you laughed out of a modern court, but it has a certain Viking logic.

The final showdown came in 1263, when King Haakon IV of Norway sailed to the Firth of Clyde with a large fleet to defend his authority over the western isles. The resulting Battle of Largs, on 2 October, was not so much a great battle as a confused skirmish on a beach during bad weather, in which storms did as much damage to the Norwegian fleet as the Scots did. Both sides claimed victory. The Scots built a monument shaped like a round tower in the nineteenth century and still hold a Viking festival there. Haakon retreated to Orkney, fell ill, and died in the Bishop's Palace in Kirkwall that December. Three years later, the Treaty of Perth handed the Hebrides and the Isle of Man to Scotland in return for a payment, while Norway kept Orkney and Shetland. It was a remarkably civilised way to end centuries of conflict, and it set a pattern: in the long run, Britain and Norway tend to settle their disputes with a treaty and a cheque.

If you want to see what this world looked like, go to the British Museum, or to the National Museum of Scotland in Edinburgh, and look at the Lewis chessmen. They were found in 1831 on the Isle of Lewis in the Outer Hebrides, buried in a sand dune, where they had lain for perhaps six hundred years. There are more than ninety pieces, carved mostly from walrus ivory in the late twelfth or early thirteenth century, and most experts think they were made in Norway, quite possibly in Trondheim, which was then a great centre of ivory carving. The kings look solemn. The queens rest their chins on their hands with an expression of profound weariness, as if they have just been told that the board game will go on for another four hours. The bishops look anxious. And among the rooks are several warriors biting the tops of their shields: berserkers, working themselves into a frenzy. They are the most delightful objects in the whole story, and they appear, in replica, in the first Harry Potter film, which means that millions of children have watched a Norwegian chess set played in a fictional Scottish castle without realising it. They are a perfect symbol of the medieval North Sea world, in which a Norwegian craftsman's work could end up in a Hebridean sand dune and, eventually, in a London glass case.

Meanwhile, the royal families of Scotland and Norway kept marrying each other, with mixed results. The saddest example is Margaret, the Maid of Norway. She was the daughter of King Eric II of Norway and Margaret of Scotland, and when her grandfather, Alexander III of Scotland, fell off his horse in the dark and died in 1286, she became heir to the Scottish throne at the age of three. In 1290, she set sail for Scotland to take up her crown and to marry the future Edward II of England, a plan that might have united the crowns of Scotland and England three centuries early. She fell ill on the voyage and died in Orkney, aged about seven. The succession crisis that followed brought Edward I north to "arbitrate" and, in due course, led to the Wars of Scottish Independence, William Wallace, Robert the Bruce and a fairly significant amount of Mel Gibson. A little girl's seasickness, in other words, shaped the history of the British Isles.

Which brings us to the unpaid bill. In 1468, King Christian I, ruler of Denmark and Norway, arranged for his daughter Margaret to marry James III of Scotland. The dowry was set at a large sum of money, which Christian did not actually have. So he pledged his lands and rights in Orkney as security, and when that was not enough, he pledged Shetland too, in 1469. The idea was that he would pay the dowry and redeem the islands. He never did. The Scots annexed the islands to the Scottish crown in 1472, and they have been part of Scotland, and later the United Kingdom, ever since.

Danish and Norwegian monarchs raised the question of redeeming the pledge several times over the following two centuries, and were politely ignored. Lawyers and romantics have occasionally argued that the pledge has never been formally extinguished, and that if Norway turned up in Kirkwall with the right amount of money (plus five centuries of interest), things might get awkward. Nobody seriously expects this to happen. But it is pleasing to think that one of the closest friendships in Europe rests, at least in part, on an IOU from 1469 that both sides have agreed, very sportingly, never to mention.

Orkney and Shetland have never quite forgotten where they came from. Shetland in particular has a strong Norse identity. The islands' flag is a white Nordic cross on blue, a sort of cheerful merger of the Scottish saltire's colours and the Scandinavian design. Every January, Lerwick holds Up Helly Aa, a fire festival in which hundreds of men in Viking costume (and, recently, after a long and lively argument, women too) parade through the town with flaming torches and then set fire to a full-sized replica longship. The festival in its modern form is actually a Victorian invention, dating from the 1880s, when the Victorians were going through a serious Viking phase. But it is no less sincere for that. When Shetlanders say that they are not entirely sure whether they are Scottish or Scandinavian, they are only half joking.

## Timber, a Royal Wedding and a Very Bad Day in Gudbrandsdalen

For most of the early modern period, Norway was the junior partner in a union with Denmark, governed from Copenhagen. Britain, meanwhile, spent the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries getting a Protestant church, a single monarch for England and Scotland, a civil war and a navy that would eventually rule the waves. The relationship between the two lands became less about kings and more about something far less romantic and far more important: wood.

Norway had forests. Britain, by the sixteenth century, was running short of them. Scottish and English ships began crossing the North Sea in large numbers to buy timber, planks and masts from the Norwegian west coast. The Norwegians called this period *Skottehandelen*, "the Scots trade", because so many of the merchants were Scottish. They came from Dundee, Leith, Aberdeen and the little ports of the east coast, they brought grain, cloth, salt and coins, and they went home with the wood that built Scottish houses, roofs and ships. Some of them stayed. Families of Scottish origin settled in Bergen, Stavanger and the fjord towns, and their surnames, sometimes Norwegianised beyond recognition, survived for centuries. The most famous example is a later one. In the eighteenth century, a merchant named Alexander Greig left the north-east of Scotland and settled in Bergen, where his descendants dropped one letter and acquired another. His great-grandson was Edvard Grieg, Norway's national composer. *In the Hall of the Mountain King*, that most Norwegian of tunes, was written by a man with an Aberdeenshire great-granddad.

The timber trade reached its high point after 1666, when the Great Fire of London destroyed much of the medieval city. London needed rebuilding, quickly, and a great deal of the timber came from Norway. There is an old Norwegian saying that the Norwegians "warmed themselves by the fire of London", meaning that the disaster did wonders for their export income. It is the sort of joke you can only make about a friend.

Royal romance, too, took a Norwegian turn. In 1589, King James VI of Scotland, the future James I of England, was betrothed to Anne of Denmark, the sister of King Christian IV of Denmark-Norway. Anne set sail for Scotland, but storms drove her ship back to Norway, and she took refuge in Oslo. James, in what is often described as the only properly romantic act of his life, set sail himself to fetch her. He arrived in Norway in November, and the couple were married in Oslo on 23 November 1589. They then spent the winter in Denmark, enjoying Danish hospitality, before going home in the spring. The voyage was so stormy that James became convinced that witches had been trying to sink him, and his conviction fed directly into the North Berwick witch trials of 1590, one of the darkest episodes in Scottish history. It is a reminder that not every Anglo-Norwegian adventure ends well. A future king of England was married in Norway, but the honeymoon gave him an unhealthy interest in demonology.

Then there is the matter of Kringen. In 1612, during a war between Denmark-Norway and Sweden, a force of Scottish mercenaries was recruited to fight for the Swedes. They landed on the Norwegian west coast and tried to march across the mountains to Sweden through the Gudbrandsdalen valley. They did not get there. On 26 August, at a narrow pass at Kringen, local farmers and militiamen ambushed them, rolling boulders and firing from the hillside. The Scots were cut to pieces. Norwegian legend, embroidered lavishly in the centuries that followed, tells of a heroic farm girl named Pillarguri who blew a horn to signal the attack, and of a Scottish commander named Sinclair whose wife had come along and who died in the battle. The real commander was probably a man called Ramsay, and a handful of survivors were sent home. But the legend of the Sinclairs became one of the founding stories of Norwegian national pride, celebrated in a famous eighteenth-century song, and in Gudbrandsdalen there is a memorial at the spot. Norway, in other words, had its own Stamford Bridge, and for once the Norwegians were the ones holding the high ground.

The relationship hit its lowest point at the start of the nineteenth century, and it is important to be honest about it. During the Napoleonic Wars, Denmark-Norway ended up on the French side, largely because Britain had attacked Copenhagen in 1807 and seized the Danish-Norwegian fleet to stop Napoleon getting it. The British then blockaded Norway's coast. Norway depended on imported grain, mostly from Denmark, and the blockade brought real hunger, especially in the bad harvest years around 1808 and 1812. Ibsen later wrote a long narrative poem, *Terje Vigen*, about a Norwegian sailor who rows to Denmark to fetch barley for his starving wife and child, only to be captured by a British ship. His family dies while he sits in an English prison. In the poem, years later, he gets the chance to take revenge on the English captain, and chooses mercy instead. It is, if you think about it, a fair summary of the whole relationship: grievance, followed by forgiveness, followed by a very long friendship.

In 1814, as Napoleon fell, Denmark was forced to hand Norway to Sweden. The Norwegians had other ideas. In the spring of 1814, a constituent assembly met at Eidsvoll, north of Christiania (now Oslo), and wrote a constitution. Signed on 17 May 1814, it was one of the most liberal constitutions in Europe at the time, inspired by the American and French examples and by British parliamentary traditions, and it is still in force today. It is the second-oldest written national constitution in continuous use in the world, after the American one. Britain, which does not have a written constitution at all, supported the Swedes, blockaded Norway again, and helped push the Norwegians into a union under the Swedish king. But the Norwegians kept their constitution, their parliament, the Storting, and their own laws. The seventeenth of May is still Norway's national day, celebrated with children's parades, brass bands and an extraordinary quantity of ice cream, hot dogs and national costumes. Britain, which has no national day, has never really known how to compete with this.

## The Victorians Discover Norway

The nineteenth century transformed the relationship again, because it was the century in which the British discovered tourism, and Norway turned out to be perfect for it.

To a Victorian gentleman, Norway offered almost everything. It had mountains, which were becoming fashionable, and fjords, which were spectacular. It had rivers full of salmon, which the British upper classes regarded with something close to religious devotion. It had a sturdy, egalitarian peasantry, whom romantic Victorians found admirably picturesque. And it was, crucially, a long way from anyone else's mother-in-law.

The salmon came first. From the 1830s onwards, wealthy British anglers began leasing fishing rights on Norway's great rivers, such as the Namsen, the Alta, the Gaula and the Lærdal. The Norwegians called them *lakselorder*, "salmon lords". They arrived every summer with servants, rods, flies and a great deal of luggage, rented farmhouses, paid local farmers to row their boats, built fishing lodges, and caught fish of improbable size. Some stayed for decades. The relationship between the salmon lords and the locals was a mixture of mutual benefit and gentle mutual bafflement. The British were paying a fortune for the privilege of standing in cold water. The Norwegians were happy to take their money. Both sides felt they had got the better deal.

The mountaineers came next. Norway's mountains had been crossed and hunted on for thousands of years, but nobody had thought of climbing them for fun until the Victorians arrived. The most important of these was William Cecil Slingsby, a Yorkshire mill owner who made his first visit in 1872 and fell in love with the place. In 1876, he made the first ascent of Store Skagastølstind in the Hurrungane range, one of Norway's most dramatic peaks, finishing the final section alone after his companions turned back. He kept returning for decades, climbing peak after peak and writing a classic book about his adventures, *Norway: The Northern Playground*. The Norwegians still call him "the father of Norwegian mountaineering", which is a generous title for a man from Yorkshire, and a reminder that Norwegians have always been more relaxed than the British about sharing credit.

Thomas Bennett, an Englishman who settled in Christiania, set up one of the first travel agencies in Norway in the 1850s to cater for British tourists. He sold maps and guidebooks, arranged carriage rides along the mountain roads, and published *Bennett's Handbook for Travellers in Norway*, which went through many editions. Cruise ships followed, carrying British passengers up the fjords to admire the scenery and buy carved wooden souvenirs. The Norwegian tourism industry, in a sense, was founded on British curiosity and British money, and it has never looked back.

The traffic was not all one way. Britain gave Norway one of its most important pieces of infrastructure. The country's first railway, the Hovedbanen, opened in 1854 between Christiania and Eidsvoll, and it was built under the supervision of Robert Stephenson, son of George Stephenson and the engineer behind the *Rocket*. There is a nice symbolism in the fact that Norway's first railway was a British line to the birthplace of the Norwegian constitution. It was a small thing, but it was the start of a long tradition of British and Norwegian engineers working together.

Britain also gave Norway one of its future queens. In 1905, Norway peacefully dissolved its union with Sweden, and the Norwegians needed a king. They chose Prince Carl of Denmark, who was married to Princess Maud, the youngest daughter of King Edward VII. Carl insisted, very democratically, on a referendum first, and nearly eighty per cent of Norwegian voters said yes to a monarchy. He took the old Norwegian royal name of Haakon VII, and his little son Alexander became Olav. The boy had been born in 1903 at Appleton House on the Sandringham estate in Norfolk. Norway's royal family, in other words, began with a London-born queen and a Norfolk-born crown prince. Queen Maud kept a home in England, travelled back regularly, and died in London in 1938. She also introduced a certain amount of British style to the Norwegian court, including, by some accounts, a love of horses, dogs and garden parties that has never quite left it.

Meanwhile, Norwegian culture was beginning to invade Britain, and this time it brought something far more dangerous than an axe. It brought ideas.

In 1889, *A Doll's House* by Henrik Ibsen was performed in London, and the reaction was as if a bomb had gone off in a vicarage. The play, about a woman who walks out on her husband and children because she is tired of being treated like a doll, was considered shocking, scandalous and very possibly the end of civilisation. Two years later, *Ghosts*, which dealt with hereditary syphilis, was performed, and the critic Clement Scott of the *Daily Telegraph* famously described it as "an open drain; a loathsome sore unbandaged; a dirty act done publicly". This was, of course, excellent publicity. George Bernard Shaw leapt to Ibsen's defence with a book called *The Quintessence of Ibsenism*, and a young man in Dublin, then part of the United Kingdom, taught himself Norwegian so that he could read Ibsen in the original and wrote him a fan letter. His name was James Joyce. Modern drama in English, in other words, was given a shove into the twentieth century by a grumpy Norwegian with magnificent sideburns.

Grieg, too, was a sensation. He toured Britain repeatedly, played to packed halls in London, performed for Queen Victoria, and received honorary doctorates from Cambridge in 1894 and from Oxford in 1906. The Victorians, who liked their music melodious and their scenery dramatic, found in Grieg the perfect combination: tunes you could hum, and the promise of fjords. The British have never stopped humming them. A surprisingly large number of British television adverts, school concerts and film trailers still reach for *Morning Mood* whenever they want to suggest a sunrise, which would probably amuse Grieg, since in *Peer Gynt* the piece is set in the Moroccan desert.

## The Great Ski Debate

There is a rivalry at the heart of this story, and it is not over fish or land. It is over technology, and specifically over the correct way to move across snow.

For most of history, the British had no strong opinion on this question, because there is not much snow in Britain and what there is tends to melt into slush by lunchtime. The Norwegians, by contrast, have been skiing for at least four thousand years. Rock carvings of skiers survive in northern Norway, and the word "ski" itself is Norwegian, from the Old Norse *skíð*, meaning a stick of split wood. The words "slalom" (from *slalåm*, roughly "sloping track") and "telemark" are Norwegian too. Skis were how Norwegians hunted, travelled and fought in winter, and by the nineteenth century, in the hills around Christiania and in the county of Telemark, they had become a sport.

The British learned about skiing, like so much else, from a Norwegian book. In 1888, Fridtjof Nansen, a young zoologist, made the first crossing of the Greenland ice cap, on skis, with a small team. His account, *The First Crossing of Greenland*, was translated into English in 1890 and became a sensation. Among the people who read it was a doctor and writer named Arthur Conan Doyle, who was living in Davos in Switzerland for the sake of his wife's health. He ordered a pair of Norwegian skis, practised in secret until he could stand up on them, and in 1894 made a crossing of a mountain pass on skis with two local brothers. He wrote it up for *The Strand Magazine*, the same magazine that published Sherlock Holmes, and predicted that the day would come when hundreds of Englishmen would go to Switzerland for the skiing season. He was right, and Switzerland has been extremely grateful ever since. The creator of the world's most famous detective was, therefore, one of the pioneers of British skiing, and he got the idea from a Norwegian scientist.

The British, being British, then took the Norwegian sport and added rules. In 1922, an Englishman named Arnold Lunn laid out the first modern slalom race at Mürren in Switzerland, with pairs of flags that skiers had to pass between. The Norwegians had invented the word and the technique; a British man invented the gates. Lunn went on to campaign tirelessly for downhill and slalom racing to be accepted as Olympic sports, which they eventually were. To this day, there is a mild, good-natured disagreement between the Norwegian school, which holds that skiing is a way of getting across country under your own power, ideally for fifty kilometres while eating a packed lunch, and the Alpine school, which holds that skiing is a way of getting down a mountain as fast as possible before a hot chocolate. Norway, unsurprisingly, dominates the first kind. At the Winter Olympics, it routinely tops the medal table, which is extraordinary for a country of five and a half million people. Britain's most famous ski jumper is Eddie "the Eagle" Edwards, who finished last at Calgary in 1988 and became a national hero for doing so. That tells you everything about the two countries' respective attitudes to sport.

The technology contest did not end with skis. Norwegians are fond of pointing out their contributions to the inventions of everyday life. The cheese slicer, the *ostehøvel*, was patented by a Norwegian carpenter named Thor Bjørklund in 1925, and it is now found in practically every Norwegian kitchen, where it is used to shave wafer-thin slices of brown cheese onto bread. The British remain, by and large, a nation that cuts cheese in rough chunks with a knife, which tells you something about the national character. The aerosol spray can was patented in the 1920s by a Norwegian engineer called Erik Rotheim. And then there is the paper clip.

Many Norwegians will tell you, with pride, that the paper clip was invented by a Norwegian named Johan Vaaler, who did indeed patent a design for one around 1899. During the Second World War, the story goes, Norwegians wore paper clips on their lapels as a quiet symbol of resistance and solidarity, and a giant paper clip statue was later erected outside Oslo. All of this is lovely. The slightly awkward fact is that Vaaler's design was not the familiar double-loop clip that we all use. That one, known as the "Gem" clip, appears to have been developed in Britain, by the Gem Manufacturing Company, a few years earlier. So, in the great Anglo-Norwegian paper clip dispute, the British have the better claim to the clip and the Norwegians have the better story about it, which seems a fair division of the spoils.

Finally, there is the question of the weather. A famous Norwegian saying holds that *det finnes ikke dårlig vær, bare dårlige klær*: "there is no such thing as bad weather, only bad clothes". The British fell walker Alfred Wainwright said almost exactly the same thing, that there is no such thing as bad weather, only unsuitable clothing. Both countries claim it. Both countries are right. It is the closest thing the North Sea has to a shared national motto, and it is spoken every weekend on both sides of the water by people in waterproof jackets who are pretending to enjoy themselves.

## The Race to the Bottom of the World

The ski debate was about to get much more serious, because in 1910 it moved to Antarctica and became a matter of life and death.

The race to the South Pole between Roald Amundsen of Norway and Robert Falcon Scott of Britain is one of the great stories of exploration, and it has been told so many times, in so many different ways, that it has become a sort of national mirror. For a long time, the British version was a tragedy of heroic sacrifice. Then, in the 1970s and 1980s, a revisionist version emerged in which Scott was an incompetent bungler. More recently, historians have settled on something more nuanced, and the nuance is all about technology.

Amundsen had not been planning to go south at all. His ship, the *Fram*, had been lent to him by Nansen for an expedition to the North Pole, but in 1909 the American explorers Frederick Cook and Robert Peary both claimed to have got there first. Amundsen decided, privately, that the North Pole was no longer worth the effort and turned his attention to the South. He told almost nobody, including most of his own crew, until they were already at sea. When they reached Madeira, he sent Scott a famously terse telegram: "Beg to inform you Fram proceeding Antarctic. Amundsen." Scott received it in Melbourne, and it is fair to say it was not the message he had been hoping for.

The two expeditions represented two different philosophies. Amundsen was a specialist. He had spent years in the Arctic learning from the Netsilik Inuit how to dress in furs, how to travel with dogs, and how to survive extreme cold. His team were expert skiers and dog drivers, and his plan was ruthlessly practical: use dogs to pull the sledges, travel light and fast, and, when the load lightened, kill some of the dogs to feed the others and the men. This last part horrified British readers, who had a sentimental attitude to dogs that the Norwegians did not share.

Scott's expedition was larger, better funded and much more ambitious scientifically. It was carrying out serious research in geology, biology and meteorology, and the scientific results are still valued today. For transport, Scott used a mixture of technologies: motor sledges, which were a bold, early experiment with mechanised polar transport; Siberian ponies; some dogs; and, in the end, man-hauling, in which the men put on harnesses and pulled the sledges themselves. The motor sledges broke down. The ponies struggled in the snow and had to be shot. The dogs were sent back. And so the final journey to the Pole was made by men dragging their own sledges across the ice, which was the most exhausting and the most dangerous option available.

There is a lovely and slightly heartbreaking detail here. Scott had consulted Nansen before he left, and on Nansen's advice he had taken a young Norwegian ski expert, Tryggve Gran, with him to teach the British how to ski. Gran was a naval officer and an excellent skier, and he did his best, but many of the British officers never really took to it. Gran was not chosen for the final polar party.

Amundsen reached the South Pole on 14 December 1911. He and his men pitched a small tent, which they called Polheim, flew the Norwegian flag, and left a letter for King Haakon VII, along with a note asking Scott to deliver it in case they did not make it home. Scott's party arrived on 17 January 1912 and found the tent waiting for them. "Great God! this is an awful place," Scott wrote in his diary, "and terrible enough for us to have laboured to it without the reward of priority." All five men died on the return journey, of cold, hunger and exhaustion, the last of them only about eleven miles from a supply depot.

In November 1912, a search party found the tent with the bodies of Scott, Wilson and Bowers inside. Tryggve Gran, the Norwegian, was a member of that party. He skied back to base wearing Scott's skis, reasoning that at least they would complete the journey. It is one of the most moving moments in the whole history of the two countries, and it sums up something about the relationship: the rivalry was real, but so was the respect.

Amundsen was always rather resentful that the British seemed to admire Scott's heroic failure more than his own efficient success. He had a point. But when news of Scott's death reached Norway, Amundsen was genuinely distressed, and Norwegians generally treated Scott with great respect. The British, for their part, eventually came round to admiring Amundsen's methods, even if they were never quite comfortable with the dog-eating. Modern polar travel owes far more to the Norwegian approach of skis, dogs, furs and lightness than to the British one of ponies and harnesses. In the great technology war of polar exploration, Norway won.

But Britain and Norway were soon to be reminded how much they needed each other on the ice. In May 1916, Ernest Shackleton, whose ship *Endurance* had been crushed by the Antarctic ice months before, completed an extraordinary journey across the Southern Ocean in a small open boat and then crossed the unmapped mountains of South Georgia on foot with two companions. They stumbled, ragged and filthy, into the Norwegian whaling station at Stromness. The station manager, Thoralf Sørlle, had met Shackleton before, but did not recognise the bearded figure in front of him. By most accounts, he asked who on earth he was. "My name is Shackleton," came the reply. One of the old Norwegian whalers who was present is said to have turned away and wept. The Norwegians fed the men, bathed them, gave them clean clothes, and helped organise the rescue of the rest of the crew. Later, at a gathering at the station, the whalers came forward one by one to shake Shackleton's hand, and it is said that they regarded his feat as the greatest thing they had ever heard of. These were men who spent their lives in the Southern Ocean, and they knew exactly what he had done.

And here is a final twist to the polar story. In 1916, the same year as Shackleton's rescue, a baby boy was born in Llandaff, near Cardiff, to Norwegian parents, Harald Dahl and Sofie Magdalene Hesselberg. His father had come to Wales to work in the ship-broking business that served the great Welsh coal ports. The boy was christened in the Norwegian Church in Cardiff docks, and his parents named him Roald, after Amundsen. Roald Dahl grew up speaking Norwegian at home and spent his summers in Norway, and his Norwegian grandmother is the model for the wise, cigar-smoking grandmother in *The Witches*, who tells her grandson Norwegian folk stories about witches. One of the greatest British children's authors of the twentieth century was, in other words, the son of Norwegian immigrants and named after the man who beat Scott to the Pole. There is surely no better summary of how tangled these two national stories had become.

## Ninth of April

The most important chapter of all in this story began before dawn on 9 April 1940.

Norway had hoped to stay neutral in the Second World War, as it had in the First. But Norway's long coastline was strategically vital to both sides. Germany depended on Swedish iron ore, much of which was shipped from the Norwegian port of Narvik, and the Norwegian fjords offered superb bases for submarines and warships. Britain, for its part, was laying mines in Norwegian waters and planning operations of its own. Germany got there first. In the early hours of 9 April, German forces invaded Norway in a surprise attack by sea and air.

The first act of the invasion went badly wrong for the Germans, and Norwegians have been quietly delighted about it ever since. A German naval force, led by the brand-new heavy cruiser *Blücher*, sailed up the Oslofjord to capture the capital and the royal family. At the narrow point of the fjord, near Drøbak, stood the old fortress of Oscarsborg. Its commander, Colonel Birger Eriksen, did not have clear orders, and his guns were elderly German-built pieces from the 1890s, which the soldiers affectionately called Moses, Aron and Josva. He decided to open fire anyway, reportedly saying something to the effect that either he would be decorated, or he would be court-martialled. The guns hit the *Blücher*, and then torpedoes from a hidden battery, also decades old, finished the job. The *Blücher* sank, and the delay gave the King, the government, the parliament and Norway's gold reserves just enough time to escape from Oslo.

The King then refused to give in. When the German envoy demanded that Haakon VII appoint a puppet government under the Norwegian Nazi Vidkun Quisling, the King told his ministers that he could not accept the German demand, and that if they felt they had to, he would abdicate rather than do so. The government stood with him. The Germans responded by bombing the village of Nybergsund, where the King and his ministers were staying, and the King and Crown Prince Olav had to shelter in the snowy woods. A few weeks later, in Molde, a photographer took a famous picture of the two of them standing beneath a birch tree as German bombers attacked the town. The tree became known as the King's Birch, and the photograph became an icon of resistance. As for Quisling, his name entered the English language as a word for traitor, which is the kind of linguistic punishment that only the English language can really deliver.

British, French and Polish troops landed in Norway to help, and the fighting that followed was confused and, for the Allies, mostly unsuccessful. The bright spot was Narvik. In two naval battles in April 1940, British destroyers, and later the battleship *Warspite*, sank the entire German destroyer force in the fjords around the town, a heavy blow to the German navy. Allied troops eventually recaptured Narvik itself at the end of May. But by then, the Germans were overrunning France, and the Allies had to withdraw. On 7 June 1940, King Haakon, Crown Prince Olav and the Norwegian government sailed from Tromsø aboard the British cruiser HMS *Devonshire*. They arrived in Scotland three days later. The Norwegian campaign had another important consequence in Britain: the parliamentary debate over its failures, in May 1940, brought down Neville Chamberlain's government and made Winston Churchill prime minister.

For the next five years, London was the capital of free Norway. The King lived for much of the war at Foliejon Park, a country house in Berkshire not far from Windsor, and the government worked from offices in London. The King broadcast to his people on the BBC, and the BBC's Norwegian service became a lifeline for a population living under occupation. When the Germans confiscated radios in 1941, Norwegians hid them in barns, under floorboards and in haystacks and kept listening anyway. The King's monogram, H7, was painted on walls across Norway as a sign of defiance, and the occupiers kept painting over it.

Norway's greatest asset in the war was not on land at all. It was at sea. Norway had one of the largest merchant fleets in the world, and most of it was at sea, outside Norwegian waters, when the invasion came. The government in exile placed it under a single organisation, Nortraship, which became for a time the largest shipping company in the world, with around a thousand ships. Norwegian tankers carried a significant share of the fuel that kept Britain fighting, and Norwegian merchant ships took part in the Atlantic convoys and the dangerous Arctic convoys to Murmansk. It is often said, by British observers at the time, that the Norwegian merchant fleet was worth more to the Allies than a million soldiers. The price was terrible. Thousands of Norwegian merchant seamen were killed, and many of the survivors felt for decades afterwards that their contribution had not been properly recognised, even in Norway.

In Scotland, a new Norwegian army formed. Norwegian soldiers trained in Dumfries and elsewhere, Norwegian pilots joined the Royal Air Force, and two Norwegian squadrons, Nos. 331 and 332, flew Spitfires from bases in England and became among the most successful fighter units in the RAF. A special unit, the Norwegian Independent Company 1, better known as Kompani Linge after its first commander, Martin Linge, trained in the Scottish Highlands under the guidance of the British Special Operations Executive. Its members learned to ski, climb, shoot, blow things up and survive in the mountains, which, given that most of them had grown up skiing, climbing and surviving in the mountains, must have been a slightly odd experience for the instructors.

Two of the most remarkable joint operations of the war came out of this partnership.

The first was the Shetland Bus. From 1941, a group of Norwegian fishermen based in Shetland, first at Lunna and then at Scalloway, began sailing small wooden fishing boats across the North Sea to occupied Norway, mostly in the depths of winter, when the long nights gave them cover. They carried agents, radios, weapons and explosives in, and brought refugees and resistance fighters out. The crossing took around thirty hours each way, in some of the worst seas in the world, in boats designed for coastal fishing, with German aircraft and patrol boats waiting at the other end. The most famous skipper, Leif Larsen, known as "Shetlands-Larsen", made dozens of trips and became one of the most decorated naval officers of the war. The cost was high: a number of boats were lost and dozens of men died. In 1943, the United States Navy provided three fast submarine chasers, the *Hessa*, *Hitra* and *Vigra*, and after that, according to the usual account, not a single crossing was lost. The slipway in Scalloway where the boats were repaired was named after Crown Prince Olav, and a memorial stands in the town today. Ask a Shetlander about the Shetland Bus, and you will often find that their grandparents knew the men.

The second was the heavy water sabotage. At Vemork, near Rjukan in Telemark, stood a hydroelectric plant that produced heavy water, a substance that German scientists needed for their nuclear research. The Allies were worried, rightly or wrongly, that it could help Germany build an atomic bomb, and they decided the plant had to be destroyed. In November 1942, the British tried to attack it with Royal Engineers carried in gliders. The operation, codenamed Freshman, was a disaster. The gliders crashed in bad weather, and the survivors were captured and murdered by the Germans. It was one of the saddest episodes of the war, and the British soldiers who died are buried and remembered in Norway.

Then a team of Norwegian commandos from Kompani Linge, trained in Britain, took over. A small advance party, codenamed Grouse, had already parachuted onto the Hardangervidda plateau, where they survived for months on almost nothing, at one point eating moss and lichen and a reindeer they had shot. In February 1943, they were joined by a team of six more, led by a twenty-three-year-old named Joachim Rønneberg. On the night of 27 to 28 February, the combined group skied across the mountains, climbed down into a deep gorge, crossed a frozen river, climbed up the other side, entered the plant through a cable tunnel, set their explosives on the heavy water cells, and escaped. Not a single shot was fired, and nobody was killed. Most of the team then skied hundreds of kilometres to Sweden. A year later, when the Germans tried to ship the remaining heavy water to Germany, another member of the team, Knut Haukelid, sank the ferry carrying it on Lake Tinn.

The heavy water story was later turned into a 1965 Hollywood film, *The Heroes of Telemark*, starring Kirk Douglas and Richard Harris, which took enormous liberties with the facts. The Norwegian veterans were mostly amused, though they pointed out that in real life they had not needed an American film star to show them how to ski. A 2015 Norwegian and Danish television series, *The Heavy Water War*, told the story with considerably more accuracy and considerably less Kirk Douglas.

Norway was liberated in May 1945, and on 7 June 1945, five years to the day after he had left, King Haakon VII sailed back into Oslo, aboard the British cruiser HMS *Norfolk*, accompanied by an escort of British and Norwegian ships. Huge crowds lined the harbour. The King had left Norway on a British ship, and he came home on one.

## The Tree, the Penguin and the Alliance

After the war, Norway said thank you in the most Norwegian way imaginable: with a tree. Since 1947, the city of Oslo has sent a Norway spruce to London every year, to stand in Trafalgar Square. It is chosen months in advance by the city's foresters in the Nordmarka forest outside Oslo, often described as the "queen of the forest", and cut down in a ceremony attended by the Mayor of Oslo, the British ambassador, and usually a number of local schoolchildren. Every so often, the British press decides that the tree looks scrawny, and social media fills with cruel jokes about it. In one recent year, when the tree was mocked as looking half-dead, the Norwegians replied, with great patience, that this was what a natural tree looked like after a long sea journey, and that it was not supposed to be a plastic one. The British, suitably chastened, went on visiting it. It is one of the very few diplomatic gifts in the world that is given every year, and it is all the more charming for being so unglamorous.

The second great symbol of the post-war friendship is a penguin. In 1961, members of the Norwegian King's Guard visiting the Edinburgh Military Tattoo became fond of the king penguins at Edinburgh Zoo. In 1972, the Guard formally adopted one, and named him Nils Olav, after Nils Egelien, the officer who arranged it, and King Olav V. He was given the rank of lance corporal. He has been promoted regularly ever since, through a succession of penguins bearing the same name, and in 2008 he was knighted, with a citation read out in front of a guard of honour of Norwegian soldiers, who stood to attention while a penguin inspected them. He has since risen to the rank of major general. Sir Nils Olav is, as far as anyone knows, the highest-ranking penguin in the history of warfare. He is also an excellent example of what makes the relationship special: the two countries have reached a level of friendship at which a Norwegian regiment can knight a Scottish penguin and nobody on either side finds this strange.

Behind the penguins and the trees, the post-war partnership was deadly serious. Britain and Norway were both founding members of NATO in 1949. Norway had learned in 1940 that neutrality was no protection, and in the Cold War it found itself sharing a border with the Soviet Union in the far north. British forces trained in Norway for decades, and the Royal Marines in particular have spent winter after winter in the Arctic north of the country, learning to live, move and fight in extreme cold from Norwegian instructors, who presumably still find it amusing to watch British soldiers learning to ski. The Royal Navy and the Norwegian navy have watched the same stretches of water, and in recent years the two countries have worked closely together to monitor Russian submarines in the North Atlantic.

The royal connection also continued. King Olav V, who had been born in Norfolk, studied at Balliol College, Oxford. So did his son, King Harald V. Crown Prince Haakon studied at the London School of Economics. Olav V was a first cousin of George VI, and Harald V was a second cousin of Elizabeth II, and the two of them were close all their lives. Olav was, among other things, a keen ski jumper and an Olympic gold-medal-winning sailor, which, by the standards of European royalty, is an unusually useful set of skills.

## Black Gold and a Line in the Water

In the 1960s, the North Sea changed its character again. For centuries it had been a sea of fish, timber and sailors. Now it became a sea of oil.

Before anyone could drill, someone had to decide who owned the seabed. In 1965, Britain and Norway signed an agreement dividing the continental shelf between them along a median line, halfway between their coasts. This was more generous to Norway than it might have been. There is a deep trench in the seabed off the Norwegian coast, and some lawyers argued that Norway's shelf really stopped at the edge of it. Britain, keen to get on with exploration, agreed to the simple median line. It turned out to be one of the most consequential diplomatic decisions of the twentieth century, because a great deal of the oil and gas was on the Norwegian side.

In 1969, after years of disappointing exploration, the American company Phillips Petroleum found the giant Ekofisk field in Norwegian waters. The next year, BP found the Forties field in British waters. Then came the Brent field, east of Shetland, named, in an oddly charming tradition, after a species of goose. Shell named its fields after seabirds, and so the North Sea filled up with Brent, Cormorant, Dunlin, Eider, Fulmar and Tern. The price of a large part of the world's oil is still set by reference to Brent Crude, which means that every time you hear about the oil price on the news, you are in a sense hearing about a goose. It is a small but meaningful contribution to the comedy of international finance.

Britain and Norway soon found that the geology did not respect the median line. The Frigg gas field and the Statfjord oil field both straddled it, and the two countries had to agree on how to share them. They did, in a series of treaties that set out how to divide the reserves, how to run the fields, and how to adjust the split as new information emerged. These unitisation agreements became a model for countries all over the world. And because the deep trench made it hard to build pipelines to the Norwegian coast in those early years, much of Norway's oil and gas initially flowed to Britain: Ekofisk oil came ashore at Teesside, and Frigg gas came ashore at St Fergus in Aberdeenshire. Later, a pipeline called Langeled carried Norwegian gas all the way to Easington in Yorkshire, and at the time it opened it was the longest underwater pipeline in the world. In 2021, an electricity cable called the North Sea Link opened between Kvilldal in Norway and Blyth in Northumberland, allowing Norwegian hydroelectric power to flow to Britain when the wind was not blowing, and British wind power to flow to Norway when it was. Today, Norway supplies a large share of Britain's gas, and on a cold, still winter's day, there is a fair chance that the kettle you are boiling is being heated by a Norwegian.

The two countries then did something very different with the money, and this is where the gentle teasing begins.

Norway, cautious and Lutheran, decided early on that the oil wealth belonged to future generations as well as the present one. It created a state oil company, Statoil (now Equinor), and in 1990 a sovereign wealth fund, now officially the Government Pension Fund Global, into which the state's oil revenues are paid. The government may only spend a small fraction of the fund's value each year. The fund invests in thousands of companies around the world and has grown to be worth around two trillion US dollars, which works out at well over a hundred thousand pounds for every Norwegian man, woman and child. Norwegians sometimes say that they own a small piece of almost every listed company on earth, and they are not entirely wrong.

Britain, by contrast, spent its oil money. It was used to cut taxes, to pay for the costs of high unemployment during the economic upheavals of the 1980s, and to balance the books. Economists still argue about whether this was a mistake. Some say Britain had more pressing needs and no reason to save. Others look at Norway's fund and sigh. Either way, British visitors to Norway now regularly wonder aloud why a country with such cold weather can afford such nice tunnels, and Norwegians are generally too polite to say "oil" out loud.

The oil has also made Norway something of a paradox. It is one of the world's largest exporters of fossil fuels, and also the country with the most enthusiastic adoption of electric cars on earth. In recent years, the great majority of new cars sold in Norway have been fully electric, partly because of generous tax incentives paid for, in effect, by oil. Britain is moving in the same direction, but much more slowly. In the great Anglo-Norwegian technology contest of the twenty-first century, the question of who will be the first to stop using petrol is one Norway is winning by a distance.

## "Maggie Thatcher, Can You Hear Me?"

No account of Britain and Norway would be complete without football, and no account of football between Britain and Norway would be complete without Bjørge Lillelien.

On 9 September 1981, Norway played England in a World Cup qualifier in Oslo. England were a major footballing nation. Norway were not. Norway won 2 to 1. As the final whistle blew, the Norwegian radio commentator Bjørge Lillelien lost his mind with joy, and broadcast what is probably the most famous piece of football commentary in history. In a mixture of Norwegian and English, he listed the great figures of British history, declaring that Norway had beaten them all: "Lord Nelson, Lord Beaverbrook, Sir Winston Churchill, Sir Anthony Eden, Clement Attlee, Henry Cooper, Lady Diana!" Then he turned his attention to the Prime Minister. "Maggie Thatcher, can you hear me? Maggie Thatcher, I have a message for you in the middle of the election campaign. We have knocked England out of the football World Cup. Maggie Thatcher, as they say in your language in the boxing bars around Madison Square Garden in New York: your boys took a hell of a beating! Your boys took a hell of a beating!"

There was, it should be said, no election campaign under way in Britain in September 1981, Lady Diana had nothing to do with football, and Henry Cooper was a boxer. None of this mattered. England, in fact, qualified for the World Cup anyway. But the commentary has become legendary in both countries, has been endlessly imitated, and has been voted one of the greatest pieces of sports commentary of all time. The British, who love nothing more than a good joke at their own expense, have taken it entirely to heart. Norway beat England again in 1993, which helped knock England out of the 1994 World Cup, and that time it really did hurt.

What makes this all the more remarkable is that Norway is one of the most passionately pro-English footballing nations on earth. Since 1969, Norwegian television has broadcast English league matches live on Saturday afternoons, and generations of Norwegians grew up supporting Liverpool, Manchester United, Leeds, Arsenal and Tottenham, often with a fervour that startles British fans. Norwegian supporters' clubs for English teams are among the largest in the world, and thousands of Norwegians fly to England every season to watch matches. When Norwegians speak of football, they often mean English football. Norway has repaid the favour with players. Ole Gunnar Solskjær, the "baby-faced assassin", scored the stoppage-time winner for Manchester United in the 1999 Champions League final. Martin Ødegaard became captain of Arsenal. And Erling Haaland, who arrived at Manchester City in 2022, promptly broke the record for the most goals in a Premier League season, with thirty-six. If you ask a Norwegian whether this counts as revenge for anything, they will tell you it is merely cultural exchange.

Then there is the Eurovision Song Contest, where the two countries share an even more intimate bond: they are both very good at doing badly. Norway famously scored "nul points" in 1978 with Jahn Teigen's "Mil etter mil", which he sang while doing a sort of jump in the air in a pair of braces, and Norway has finished last more often than any other country. The United Kingdom scored nul points in 2003 and again in 2021. Both countries have also won: Britain five times, including with Bucks Fizz and Katrina and the Waves, and Norway three times, including with Alexander Rybak's "Fairytale" in 2009, which at the time received the highest score in the contest's history. In Eurovision terms, Britain and Norway are two old friends who meet up every May, share a bottle of wine, and commiserate about how nobody understands them.

Pop music has been kinder. In 1985, the Norwegian band a-ha released "Take On Me", with its astonishing pencil-sketch animated video, and it became a huge hit in Britain and around the world. Their follow-up, "The Sun Always Shines on TV", went to number one in Britain, and in 1987 they recorded the theme song for the James Bond film *The Living Daylights*, which is about as British an honour as a Norwegian band can receive. Monty Python, meanwhile, had already given Britain its most famous Norwegian bird: the Norwegian Blue parrot, "pining for the fjords", in the Dead Parrot sketch of 1969. It is a shame that the Norwegian Blue does not exist. If it did, it would surely be the national bird of the friendship.

Television has also made the crossing. In 2009, the Norwegian broadcaster NRK showed the whole seven-hour train journey from Bergen to Oslo, in real time, from a camera on the front of the train. Millions of Norwegians watched it. The genre, "slow TV", became a national obsession, with marathon broadcasts of ferry voyages, knitting, firewood and salmon swimming upstream. The BBC was so taken with the idea that it made its own versions, including a two-hour trip along a canal. The British, it turned out, were just as happy as the Norwegians to watch nothing happen very slowly, as long as it was nicely filmed and there were occasional cows.

And then there is the Beatles' "Norwegian Wood", which Norwegian people sometimes assume must be a romantic song about their forests. It is, in fact, about an ill-fated romantic encounter, and the "Norwegian wood" in question is generally thought to be cheap pine panelling, of the sort that was fashionable in London flats in the 1960s. This is perhaps the most British possible way of referring to Norway: affectionately, and in the context of interior design.

## What the Neighbours Have in Common

What, in the end, do Britain and Norway actually share, apart from a sea, a gas pipeline and a parrot sketch?

The first answer is a political tradition. Both are constitutional monarchies, in which a king reigns but parliament rules. Norway's monarchy is younger, more informal and, in the Norwegian way, considerably less fussy. The Norwegian royal family has a habit of being photographed on skis, on bicycles or on the Oslo tram, and when King Olav V was asked during the 1970s oil crisis why he had no bodyguards while travelling on the tram, he is said to have replied that he had four million of them. The British monarchy has more gold carriages. But the basic idea is the same: a hereditary head of state who embodies the nation, answers to its elected representatives, and turns up in a crisis. In 1940, King Haakon VII showed what that meant. His refusal to accept a puppet government, at the risk of his life, is still one of the most admired acts of any monarch in modern European history.

The second answer is a shared love of the outdoors, and a shared belief that people should be able to enjoy it. Norway has *allemannsretten*, the "everyman's right", a long tradition, written into law in 1957, that allows anybody to walk, ski, camp and pick berries on uncultivated land, whoever owns it, as long as they behave responsibly. Norwegians call the joy of being outdoors *friluftsliv*, "open-air life", and on any Sunday you will find a large proportion of the population on a *søndagstur*, a Sunday walk, often with a packed lunch and a flask of coffee. The British have their own version of this passion, expressed through rambling clubs, national parks and the long struggle for public access to the countryside. In 2003, Scotland passed a law giving the public a general right of responsible access to most land, a law often compared to the Scandinavian model. England and Wales have more limited rights, but the instinct is the same. Both nations believe, deep down, that a hill is a public good.

The third answer is a particular kind of humour and modesty. Norwegians have a concept called *Janteloven*, the "Law of Jante", named after a fictional town in a 1933 novel by the Danish-Norwegian writer Aksel Sandemose. Its basic principle is "don't think you're anything special". It is a sort of social code against boasting, and Norwegians both live by it and complain about it. The British have no single word for this, but they practise something very similar through understatement, self-deprecation and the art of describing a disaster as "not ideal". Both nations distrust show-offs. Both find it slightly embarrassing to be praised. And both, as the Eurovision results suggest, have learned to find a certain comfort in coming last.

The fourth answer is a shared stubborn independence, which can be infuriating to their neighbours. Norway voted twice, in 1972 and 1994, not to join what is now the European Union. Britain joined in 1973 and then, in 2016, voted to leave. During the long arguments that followed, British politicians spent a great deal of time discussing the "Norway model", in which a country has access to the European single market without being a member of the EU. Norwegians watched these debates with a mixture of amusement and alarm, as if a friend had announced that they wanted to borrow your house for an experiment. Both countries, in their different ways, have tried to stay close to Europe while keeping a little distance. Both are maritime nations with their faces turned to the sea, and both have long histories of looking outward, across the oceans, as much as inward, to the continent.

And the fifth answer, which underlies all of the others, is a shared sense of what is worth fighting for. In 1940, when most of Europe had fallen, Britain and Norway stood together because they both believed, at a level deeper than politics, in the same few things: that small nations have the right to govern themselves, that democracy is worth defending, that tyrants should not be appeased, and that you do not abandon a friend who is in trouble. Those convictions did not appear from nowhere. They were built over centuries of trade, marriage, rivalry and argument, of shared seas and borrowed words. When the King of Norway sailed into exile on a British ship and came home on another, he was not just making a strategic choice. He was trusting a neighbour.

## Lights in Trafalgar Square

So let us return to the tree.

It is December in London. The tree has made its way from the forest outside Oslo, across the North Sea, past the oil platforms named after geese and seabirds, past the place where the cable carries Norwegian water power to Northumberland, and into the Thames. It has been lifted onto its plinth by a crane while a small crowd of tourists watched and took photographs. A Norwegian choir has sung carols. The Mayor of Oslo has made a speech. The lights have been switched on, in vertical strings, in the Norwegian style. Someone on the internet has said it looks a bit sad.

And standing beneath it, if you listen carefully, you can hear echoes of the whole story. You can hear the oars of the longships off Lindisfarne, and the creak of the bridge over the Derwent. You can hear the timber merchants of Leith haggling on the quays of Bergen, and the scratch of Grieg's pen in the house his Scottish great-grandfather's family made. You can hear the swish of Conan Doyle's borrowed Norwegian skis, the crunch of Amundsen's dogs on the ice, and Tryggve Gran skiing home on a dead man's skis. You can hear the engines of the Shetland Bus, the King's voice on the BBC, the click of the paper clip on a resistance fighter's lapel, and the clatter of a penguin inspecting a guard of honour. And somewhere in the background, faint but unmistakable, you can hear a Norwegian commentator shouting at Margaret Thatcher.

Twelve centuries ago, the first Norwegians to come to Britain arrived uninvited and left with the silver. Now they send a tree every year to say thank you. That is not a bad trajectory for a friendship. It suggests that history does not have to be a story of grudges, and that two peoples who began as raiders and victims can become, over time, something more like family: the kind that argues about fish and football, laughs at each other's jokes, borrows each other's words, and turns up without fail when it matters.

The North Sea is still there between them, grey and cold and occasionally furious. But it has never really been a barrier. It has been a road, a market, a battlefield, an escape route and a shared back garden. And on a dark December evening in Trafalgar Square, beneath a slightly lopsided spruce, it feels very much like the shortest distance in the world.
