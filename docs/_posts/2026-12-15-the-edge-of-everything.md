## Poking Our Heads Through the Sky

There is a famous picture that turns up in almost every book about astronomy. A traveller in a long robe, with a pilgrim's staff, has reached the place where the sky meets the Earth. He has knelt down, pushed his head through the starry dome, and is gazing in wonder at what lies beyond: wheels within wheels, strange clouds and fires, the hidden machinery of the heavens.

It looks like a medieval woodcut, and is usually described as one. In fact it was first published in 1888, in a popular science book by the French astronomer and writer Camille Flammarion, and nobody knows who made it. It is a Victorian pastiche of the Middle Ages, a picture of what nineteenth-century people imagined medieval people had imagined. But it has lasted because it captures a feeling that every generation of stargazers has had: the urge to stick your head through the sky and see what is really out there.

This essay is the story of how we did exactly that. It is one of the greatest detective stories ever told. The clues were faint smudges of light, tiny shifts in the positions of stars, colours hidden in starlight and a hiss of static in a radio receiver. The detectives included a Polish canon, a Danish nobleman with a metal nose, a German mystic, an Italian who annoyed the Pope, an English genius who did not like people, a deaf American woman who measured the universe from a desk at Harvard, a lawyer from Missouri who liked to pretend he was English, a Belgian priest and two radio astronomers who blamed some pigeons.

What they found is astonishing. Four hundred years ago, educated Europeans believed that the universe was a set of nested spheres with the Earth at the centre, ending a little way beyond Saturn. A hundred years ago, most astronomers believed that our galaxy, the Milky Way, was the whole universe. Today we know that the observable universe is about 93 billion light-years across, contains hundreds of billions of galaxies, began about 13.8 billion years ago in a hot, dense state we call the Big Bang, and is made mostly of two things that nobody has ever directly detected and that we call, for want of anything better, dark matter and dark energy.

That is quite a journey. Let's take it from the beginning.

## The Sky Above the Fields

### Watching and counting

For most of human history the night sky was not a distant abstraction. Without electric lights, it was overwhelming: thousands of stars, the great glowing band of the Milky Way, the wandering planets, the phases of the Moon. People used it as a calendar, a clock and a compass. They knew when to plant by the rising of particular stars. They navigated by them. They built monuments aligned to the solstices.

The Babylonians kept careful records of the motions of the planets and the Moon for centuries, and learnt to predict eclipses. The Egyptians based their calendar partly on the annual rising of Sirius, which heralded the flooding of the Nile. But it was the ancient Greeks who first tried to build a physical model of the whole cosmos, explaining why the heavens moved as they did.

### The Greeks measure the world

The Greeks worked out a surprising amount. By the time of Aristotle in the fourth century BC, educated Greeks knew that the Earth was a sphere: they saw that its shadow on the Moon during a lunar eclipse was always round, and that travellers going south saw new stars rise above the horizon. (The idea that medieval people thought the Earth was flat is itself largely a myth, popularised in the nineteenth century by, among others, Washington Irving's romanticised biography of Columbus.)

Around 240 BC Eratosthenes, the librarian of Alexandria, measured the size of the Earth. He had heard that at noon on the summer solstice, at Syene (modern Aswan) in southern Egypt, the Sun shone straight down a well, so that it was directly overhead. At the same moment in Alexandria, further north, a vertical stick cast a short shadow, showing that the Sun was about seven degrees from overhead, roughly one-fiftieth of a full circle. Since he knew roughly how far it was from Syene to Alexandria, he multiplied that distance by fifty. His answer was close to the true circumference of about 40,000 kilometres; how close depends on exactly how long his unit of distance, the stade, was, which historians still argue about. Either way, it was a superb piece of reasoning with a stick and a well.

One Greek even got the solar system right. In the third century BC Aristarchus of Samos argued that the Sun was much bigger than the Earth, and that the Earth went round it. Almost nobody agreed. If the Earth were moving, the critics asked, why didn't we feel it? Why didn't the stars appear to shift position as we moved around the Sun? The second objection was a good one. The stars do shift, but because they are so far away, the shift is so tiny that nobody would be able to measure it for another two thousand years.

### Ptolemy's machine

The model that won was the one with the Earth at the centre. Aristotle described a universe of nested, transparent crystalline spheres carrying the Moon, the Sun, the planets and the stars around a motionless Earth. Everything below the Moon was made of the four elements, earth, water, air and fire, and was subject to change and decay. Everything above the Moon was made of a perfect fifth element, the *aether*, and moved in perfect circles forever.

The problem was that the planets did not behave. Every so often, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn appear to slow down, stop, go backwards for a while, and then resume their forward motion against the stars. (We now know this is because the Earth is overtaking them on the inside track, the same way a slower car on a motorway appears to go backwards as you pass it.) To explain this "retrograde motion" while keeping the Earth at the centre and everything moving in circles, astronomers added circles on top of circles.

The master of this system was Claudius Ptolemy, who worked in Alexandria in the second century AD. His great book, now known by its Arabic title, the *Almagest*, set out a model in which each planet moved on a small circle called an epicycle, whose centre moved on a larger circle around the Earth, with additional refinements to make the numbers come out right. It was complicated, but it worked. It could predict the positions of the planets well enough for practical purposes, and it remained the standard model of the heavens for about 1,400 years, which makes it one of the most successful scientific theories in history, even though it was wrong.

*Andreas Cellarius, Hypothesis Ptolemaica, from Harmonia Macrocosmica (1660). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

After the fall of Rome, Greek astronomy was preserved and improved by astronomers in the Islamic world. Scholars in Baghdad, Damascus, Cairo, Córdoba, Maragha and Samarkand translated Ptolemy, built observatories, compiled ever more accurate star tables and pointed out the flaws in his geometry. Nasir al-Din al-Tusi devised an ingenious mathematical device, now called the Tusi couple, to produce straight-line motion from circles; something very like it later appeared in the work of Copernicus. Many of the brightest stars still carry Arabic names: Aldebaran, Betelgeuse, Rigel, Altair, Deneb, Vega. When Europe rediscovered astronomy in the late Middle Ages, it was largely through Arabic texts translated into Latin.

## The Earth Starts to Move

### A cautious canon

Nicolaus Copernicus was a canon of the cathedral at Frombork in what is now northern Poland: a churchman, doctor, lawyer and administrator, who did astronomy in his spare time. Around 1510 he began to circulate a short manuscript proposing something radical: that the Sun, not the Earth, was at the centre, and that the Earth was a planet like the others, spinning on its axis once a day and travelling around the Sun once a year.

![Nicolaus Copernicus, from a portrait made around 1580 for the town hall of Toruń, the city where he was born in 1473.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/copernicus.jpg)
*Portrait of Nicolaus Copernicus, Toruń town hall (c. 1580). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

The idea had real advantages. It explained retrograde motion naturally: the planets only seem to go backwards because we are overtaking them, or they are overtaking us. It explained why Mercury and Venus always stay close to the Sun in the sky: their orbits lie inside ours. And it let Copernicus calculate the relative distances of the planets from the Sun for the first time, putting the solar system into a coherent order.

But Copernicus was cautious. He knew the idea would be ridiculed, and he still clung to perfect circles, which meant his system needed epicycles too and was not much more accurate than Ptolemy's. He held back publication of his full work for decades. *De revolutionibus orbium coelestium*, "On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres", was finally printed in Nuremberg in 1543. According to a well-known story, a copy was put into his hands on his deathbed. Without his knowledge, a Lutheran theologian called Andreas Osiander, who supervised the printing, had added an unsigned preface saying that the theory need not be true, only a convenient tool for calculation. It was a clever way to keep the book out of trouble, and it worked, for a while.

### The man with the metal nose

The next step came from the most colourful astronomer of them all. Tycho Brahe was a Danish nobleman, rich, arrogant and brilliant. As a student, he lost part of his nose in a duel with another nobleman, reportedly over a mathematical dispute, and wore a replacement made of brass, or possibly of gold and silver, for the rest of his life. He kept a dwarf jester called Jepp, who sat under the dinner table, and a tame elk, which, according to the story, died after drinking too much beer at a nobleman's house and falling down the stairs.

In 1572 Tycho saw a brilliant new star appear in the constellation Cassiopeia. We now know it was a supernova, an exploding star. Tycho showed, by careful measurement, that it did not shift against the background stars as the Moon would, so it was far beyond the Moon. This was a shock. According to Aristotle, the heavens beyond the Moon were perfect and unchanging. Yet here was change in the heavens.

The King of Denmark gave Tycho the island of Hven, in the strait between Denmark and Sweden, and the money to build an observatory there. Tycho called it Uraniborg, the castle of Urania, the muse of astronomy, and filled it with enormous instruments for measuring the positions of stars and planets by eye: the telescope had not yet been invented. Over twenty years he produced the most accurate astronomical observations ever made, with errors of a fraction of the width of the full Moon. He did not accept Copernicus's system; he devised a compromise of his own, in which the planets went round the Sun but the Sun went round a stationary Earth. But his data would destroy his own model.

### Kepler's ellipses

In 1600 Tycho, having fallen out with the new Danish king, moved to Prague as imperial mathematician to the Holy Roman Emperor, and hired a brilliant, poor, sickly German assistant called Johannes Kepler. Kepler was a convinced Copernican and a mystic who believed that God had designed the universe according to beautiful mathematical principles. In his first book, *Mysterium Cosmographicum* (1596), he had proposed that the spacing of the six known planets could be explained by nesting the five perfect geometric solids of Greek geometry, cube, tetrahedron and so on, inside each other between their orbits.

![Johannes Kepler's model of the solar system from Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596), in which the five Platonic solids nest inside one another to set the spacing of the planets' orbits. It was beautiful and completely wrong.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/kepler.jpg)
*Johannes Kepler, model of the solar system from Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

It was a lovely idea and it was entirely wrong. But Kepler had a quality that set him apart from many brilliant people: when the data contradicted his beautiful theories, he believed the data. Tycho died suddenly in 1601, and Kepler got hold of his precious observations, rather to the annoyance of Tycho's heirs. He spent years trying to fit the orbit of Mars to a circle. It would not fit; the error was only about eight minutes of arc, about a quarter of the width of the full Moon, but Kepler knew that Tycho's observations were better than that. So he abandoned circles altogether.

In 1609 he published his discovery that the planets move not in circles but in **ellipses**, slightly squashed circles, with the Sun at one focus. He also showed that a planet moves faster when it is closer to the Sun and slower when it is further away, sweeping out equal areas in equal times. Ten years later he published a third law, linking the time a planet takes to orbit the Sun to its distance from it. The epicycles were gone. For the first time, the solar system could be described by a few simple rules.

Kepler's life was not easy. He lost his wife and several of his children, and in 1620 his elderly mother was arrested and tried for witchcraft. Kepler spent months defending her himself, and she was eventually released. Meanwhile he worked on, half astronomer and half mystic, convinced that he was reading the mind of God.

### Galileo looks up

In 1609, the same year Kepler published his ellipses, an Italian professor of mathematics at Padua heard about a Dutch invention: a tube with lenses at each end that made distant things look closer. Galileo Galilei promptly built his own, improved it until it magnified about twenty times, and did something that changed history. He pointed it at the sky.

What he saw, over a few frantic months, overturned two thousand years of astronomy. The Moon was not a perfect, polished sphere: it had mountains and craters, and he could estimate the heights of the mountains from the lengths of their shadows. The Milky Way, that soft band of light across the sky, was made of countless individual stars too faint to see with the naked eye. And in January 1610 he saw four small "stars" lined up near Jupiter, which moved from night to night. They were moons, orbiting Jupiter. Here, plainly, were bodies that did not go around the Earth.

![Galileo's own drawings of the Moon, made in 1609 as he observed it through his telescope, showing the rough, cratered boundary between light and shadow.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/galileomoon.jpg)
*Galileo Galilei, original drawings of the Moon (1609), Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale, Florence. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Galileo rushed out a short book, *Sidereus Nuncius*, "The Starry Messenger", in March 1610. Shrewdly, he named Jupiter's moons the "Medicean stars" in honour of the Medici family, the rulers of Florence, and was rewarded with a job as their court mathematician. Later that year he observed that Venus showed a full set of phases, like the Moon, from a thin crescent to a full disc. That was impossible in Ptolemy's system, in which Venus always stays between the Earth and the Sun. It was exactly what the Copernican system predicted.

Not everyone wanted to look. Galileo's colleague at Padua, the philosopher Cesare Cremonini, famously refused to look through the telescope at all. We can imagine the conversation, though the words here are invented.

> **Galileo:** Cesare, you need only put your eye to the glass. Jupiter has four moons. You can watch them move.
>
> **Cremonini:** I have no need to look. Aristotle has explained the heavens perfectly well.
>
> **Galileo:** Aristotle did not have a telescope.
>
> **Cremonini:** And how do I know your tube does not put the moons there itself? Glass deceives the eye. Besides, looking through such things gives me a headache.

Galileo became increasingly outspoken in support of Copernicus, and the Catholic Church became increasingly alarmed. In 1616 the Church declared the idea that the Sun was stationary and the Earth moved to be contrary to Scripture, and Galileo was warned not to hold or defend it. In 1632 he published a brilliant, witty book, *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*, in the form of a conversation between a Copernican, an intelligent layman, and a defender of Aristotle called Simplicio, who was made to look foolish and who appeared to voice some of the Pope's own arguments. Pope Urban VIII, who had once been Galileo's friend and admirer, was not amused.

In 1633 Galileo, aged 69, was tried by the Inquisition in Rome, forced to recant his belief that the Earth moves, and sentenced to house arrest for the rest of his life. The story that he muttered "*Eppur si muove*", "And yet it moves", as he rose from his knees is almost certainly a later legend; it first appears in print more than a century later. He spent his final years at his villa near Florence, going blind, and wrote one more great book, on the science of motion, which was smuggled out and published in the Netherlands. In 1992 Pope John Paul II formally acknowledged that the Church had erred in its treatment of him.

### Newton ties it together

Galileo died in January 1642. Isaac Newton was born at Woolsthorpe in Lincolnshire on Christmas Day the same year, by the calendar then in use in England. He grew up to be one of the greatest scientific minds who has ever lived, and one of the most difficult: secretive, quarrelsome, obsessive, and much more interested in alchemy and biblical prophecy than his admirers like to admit.

![Sir Isaac Newton, painted by Sir Godfrey Kneller in 1689, two years after the publication of the Principia.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/newton.jpg)
*Sir Godfrey Kneller, Portrait of Sir Isaac Newton (1689). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

In 1684 the astronomer Edmond Halley visited Newton in Cambridge and asked him what shape of orbit a planet would follow if it were attracted to the Sun by a force that weakened with the square of the distance. Newton replied at once: an ellipse. He had worked it out years before, but had mislaid the calculation. Halley persuaded him to write it up properly. The result, published in 1687 at Halley's own expense, was the *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, usually known simply as the *Principia*.

Newton showed that the same force that makes an apple fall to the ground also holds the Moon in its orbit around the Earth, and the planets in their orbits around the Sun. Kepler's three laws, which Kepler had found by painstaking trial and error, dropped out of Newton's mathematics automatically. The heavens and the Earth, which Aristotle had divided into two separate realms with different rules, turned out to obey the same laws. It was the first great unification in physics.

Newton's theory was so powerful that it could predict the future. Halley used it to calculate that a bright comet seen in 1682 was the same one seen in 1607 and 1531, and predicted that it would return in late 1758. He did not live to see it, but on Christmas Day 1758 a German farmer and amateur astronomer spotted it, right on schedule. It has been called Halley's Comet ever since.

### How big is the solar system?

Kepler's laws told astronomers the relative distances of the planets: Mars, for example, is about one and a half times further from the Sun than the Earth is. But nobody knew the actual distance in miles or kilometres. Halley proposed a way to find it: observe Venus crossing the face of the Sun, a rare event called a transit, from widely separated places on Earth, and use the tiny differences in timing to calculate the distance by trigonometry.

Transits of Venus come in pairs eight years apart, separated by more than a century, and the next pair was due in 1761 and 1769. Halley was long dead by then, but scientific expeditions were sent all over the world to observe them. The most famous was the voyage of Captain James Cook, sent to Tahiti in 1769 to observe the transit, with secret orders to look for a great southern continent afterwards. The observations were less precise than hoped, partly because of an optical effect that blurred the moment Venus touched the edge of the Sun, but they gave a distance from the Earth to the Sun of about 150 million kilometres, close to the modern figure.

Meanwhile, the speed of light had been measured, by accident. In 1676 the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer, working in Paris, noticed that the eclipses of Jupiter's moons happened a few minutes earlier than predicted when Jupiter was near the Earth, and later when it was far away. He realised that the difference was the time light took to cross the extra distance. Light, it turned out, was fast but not infinitely fast. Today we know its speed is almost exactly 300,000 kilometres per second, and astronomers use it as a ruler: a **light-year** is the distance light travels in a year, about 9.5 trillion kilometres.

## Into the Stars

### The musician who found a planet

William Herschel was a German musician who moved to England as a young man, became organist at the Octagon Chapel in Bath, and took up astronomy as a hobby, grinding his own mirrors and building his own telescopes, which were among the best in the world. On 13 March 1781, sweeping the sky from his garden, he noticed an object that was not a point of light like a star but a tiny disc, which moved slowly over the following nights. He thought at first it was a comet. It was a new planet, the first discovered since ancient times, and it doubled the size of the known solar system.

Herschel wanted to name it *Georgium Sidus*, "George's Star", after King George III, who rewarded him with a pension that allowed him to become a full-time astronomer. Astronomers outside Britain were unenthusiastic about naming a planet after the King of England, and it was eventually called **Uranus**, after the Greek god of the sky. (This name has been a source of schoolchildren's amusement ever since.)

Herschel was one of the first to try to work out the shape of our galaxy, by counting stars in different directions. He concluded that the Sun lay in a flattened disc of stars, which was right, though he wrongly placed the Sun near the centre. He also built the largest telescope in the world, with a tube twelve metres long and a mirror 1.2 metres across, which became a tourist attraction in its own right. His sister Caroline, who came over from Germany to keep house for him, became a fine astronomer in her own right, discovering eight comets, and became the first woman to be paid a salary as a scientist in Britain.

![William Herschel's great "40-foot" telescope at Slough, completed in 1789: for half a century the largest telescope in the world.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/herschel.jpg)
*Herschel's 40-foot telescope at Slough, engraving from Popular Science Monthly, volume 9 (1876). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

### Measuring a star

How far away are the stars? Aristarchus's critics had asked the right question: if the Earth goes round the Sun, the nearer stars should appear to shift back and forth slightly against the more distant ones over the course of a year, just as your thumb appears to jump against the background if you close one eye and then the other. This shift is called **parallax**. Astronomers had looked for it for centuries without success.

In 1838 the German astronomer Friedrich Bessel finally measured the parallax of a star called 61 Cygni. The shift was minute, about a third of an arcsecond, which is like measuring the width of a coin from more than ten kilometres away. It put 61 Cygni about ten light-years away: roughly 100 trillion kilometres. The stars, it turned out, were not just far away. They were unimaginably far away. And the nearest of them were the easy ones.

### Reading the light

In 1835 the French philosopher Auguste Comte gave as an example of something humanity could never know the chemical composition of the stars. It was a reasonable bet. Within a quarter of a century he had lost it.

When sunlight is spread out into a rainbow by a prism, the spectrum is crossed by hundreds of fine dark lines, first carefully mapped by the German optician Joseph von Fraunhofer in 1814. In 1859 two German scientists at Heidelberg, the physicist Gustav Kirchhoff and the chemist Robert Bunsen (of the burner), discovered what they meant. Every chemical element, when heated, glows with its own pattern of bright lines, like a barcode; and when light passes through a cooler gas of that element, the same pattern appears as dark lines. The dark lines in sunlight were the barcodes of the elements in the Sun's atmosphere: sodium, iron, calcium, and others.

This technique, **spectroscopy**, became astronomy's most powerful tool. It let astronomers find out what stars were made of, how hot they were, and, as we shall see, how fast they were moving towards or away from us. In 1868 astronomers studying the Sun during an eclipse found a line that matched no known element. It was named **helium**, after *helios*, the Greek for "Sun", and it was not found on Earth until 1895. It is the only element discovered in space before it was found on our own planet.

### The women who measured the heavens

At Harvard College Observatory in the 1880s, the director, Edward Pickering, faced a mountain of data: hundreds of thousands of photographic plates of the sky that needed to be examined, measured and catalogued. He hired a team of women to do it. They were known as "computers", in the original sense of people who compute, and they were paid much less than men would have been. Pickering's first hire was his own Scottish maid, Williamina Fleming, who turned out to be a brilliant astronomer and discovered hundreds of variable stars and the famous Horsehead Nebula.

![Edward Pickering's "Harvard computers", the women who catalogued and classified hundreds of thousands of stars from photographic plates. Williamina Fleming stands in the centre.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/harvardcomputers.jpg)
*The women computers of Harvard College Observatory. Harvard College Observatory, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Another computer, Annie Jump Cannon, who had lost most of her hearing after an illness in her twenties, devised the system for classifying stars by their spectra that astronomers still use, sorting them into types labelled O, B, A, F, G, K and M, from hottest to coolest. Generations of astronomy students have memorised the order with the mnemonic "Oh Be A Fine Girl, Kiss Me". Cannon personally classified around 350,000 stars, at a rate that at her peak reached several a minute.

The most important of all, for our story, was Henrietta Swan Leavitt, a minister's daughter who was also increasingly deaf. Leavitt studied a type of star called a **Cepheid variable**, which brightens and dims in a regular cycle lasting days or weeks. Examining hundreds of them in the Small Magellanic Cloud, a faint patch of stars visible from the southern hemisphere, she noticed something remarkable, which she published in 1908 and confirmed in 1912: the brighter the Cepheid, the longer its cycle.

Because all the stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud were at roughly the same distance from us, this meant that a Cepheid's period revealed its true brightness. And if you know how bright something really is, and how bright it appears, you can work out how far away it is, just as you can judge the distance of a 100-watt light bulb from how dim it looks. Leavitt had found a cosmic measuring stick, what astronomers call a "standard candle". It would soon be used to measure the size of the universe. Leavitt died of cancer in 1921, aged 53. In 1925, a Swedish mathematician wrote to Harvard proposing to nominate her for the Nobel Prize. He had not heard that she was dead.

## The Universe Gets Bigger

### The Great Debate

By the early twentieth century, astronomers had catalogued thousands of fuzzy patches of light in the sky called "nebulae". Some were clearly clouds of glowing gas within our own galaxy. But others, such as the great nebula in Andromeda, had a spiral shape. What were they? Were they small clouds of gas and young stars within the Milky Way? Or were they, as the philosopher Immanuel Kant had suggested in 1755, entire separate systems of stars, unimaginably far away: "island universes", as they came to be called?

On 26 April 1920, at the National Academy of Sciences in Washington, two astronomers argued the question in public. Harlow Shapley argued that the Milky Way was enormous, far bigger than anyone had thought, and that the spiral nebulae were part of it. Heber Curtis argued that the Milky Way was smaller and that the spirals were separate galaxies. It became known as the **Great Debate**. In a sense, both men were partly right: Shapley about the size of the Milky Way and the Sun's position far from its centre, Curtis about the spirals. The question needed a measurement to settle it.

### The night Andromeda moved away

The man who settled it was Edwin Hubble. Born in Missouri in 1889, he was tall, handsome, athletic and a gifted self-promoter. He had been a Rhodes Scholar at Oxford, where he studied law and returned to America with a cape, a pipe and an English accent that he kept for the rest of his life, to the private amusement of his colleagues. After a brief and unenthusiastic spell in law and teaching, he took up astronomy, and in 1919 he joined the Mount Wilson Observatory in California, home of the 100-inch (2.5-metre) Hooker Telescope, then the largest in the world.

On the night of 5-6 October 1923, Hubble took a long-exposure photograph of the Andromeda nebula. Examining it, he noticed a spot of light that he first marked as a possible nova. Comparing it with older plates, he realised it was a Cepheid variable. He crossed out the "N" and wrote, in excited capitals, "VAR!" Using Leavitt's relation between period and brightness, he calculated its distance. It came out at about 900,000 light-years. Shapley had estimated the entire Milky Way at about 300,000 light-years across. Andromeda was far outside it. It was a separate galaxy.

When Hubble sent Shapley a letter with the news, Shapley is said to have held it out to a colleague and remarked: "Here is the letter that has destroyed my universe." The result was announced at a meeting of the American Astronomical Society on 1 January 1925. In a single stroke, the universe had grown from one galaxy to countless millions. (Hubble's distance was, in fact, too small. There turned out to be two different kinds of Cepheid, and when this was understood in the 1950s, the distance to Andromeda more than doubled. It is now put at about 2.5 million light-years.)

*The Andromeda galaxy (M31) in infrared light. NASA/JPL-Caltech/K. Gordon (University of Arizona), public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

## The Universe Begins

### Einstein's blunder

Meanwhile, the theory needed to make sense of the universe as a whole had arrived. In 1915 Albert Einstein completed his **general theory of relativity**, which described gravity not as a force pulling objects together but as the curving of space and time by mass and energy. As the physicist John Wheeler later summed it up, matter tells space-time how to curve, and curved space-time tells matter how to move.

When Einstein applied his equations to the universe as a whole in 1917, he found that they did not allow it to stand still. A universe full of matter should either expand or collapse. Like almost everyone else at the time, Einstein believed the universe was static and eternal, so he added a term to his equations, the **cosmological constant**, a kind of anti-gravity pushing outwards, to hold it in balance.

Others were less sure. In 1922 the Russian mathematician Alexander Friedmann showed that Einstein's equations naturally described an expanding universe. In 1927 a Belgian Catholic priest and physicist, Georges Lemaître, went further. He had noticed that the American astronomer Vesto Slipher, measuring the spectra of spiral nebulae between 1912 and 1917, had found that most of them were rushing away from us. The tell-tale sign was **redshift**: the spectral lines of a receding object are stretched towards the red end of the spectrum, just as the pitch of an ambulance siren drops as it drives away. Lemaître proposed that the universe was expanding, and that the further away a galaxy was, the faster it should be receding. He published in a little-read Belgian journal, in French, and hardly anyone noticed. Einstein, who met him at a conference that year, told him that his mathematics was correct but his physics was abominable.

### Hubble's law

In 1929 Hubble, working with his assistant Milton Humason, published a graph comparing the distances of galaxies with their speeds of recession. Humason was a remarkable character: he had left school at fourteen and come to Mount Wilson as a mule driver, carrying building materials up the mountain, then became a janitor at the observatory, then a night assistant, and finally one of the most skilful observers of his generation, without ever earning a degree.

Let us imagine them on the mountain. The words are invented; the work is real.

> **Humason:** That's another one, Dr Hubble. The fainter they are, the bigger the shift towards the red. Every time.
>
> **Hubble:** Fainter means further away. Further away means faster. It's a straight line, Milton.
>
> **Humason:** So everything's running away from us? What did we do?
>
> **Hubble:** Not from us especially. From everything. Imagine dots on a balloon as you blow it up. Every dot sees every other dot moving away from it.
>
> **Humason:** Then where's the middle of the balloon?
>
> **Hubble:** That, Milton, is the question.

Their result showed that the further a galaxy was, the faster it was moving away, in a straight-line relationship. This became known as **Hubble's law**, and the rate of expansion as the Hubble constant. In 2018 the International Astronomical Union voted to recommend calling it the Hubble-Lemaître law, to give the Belgian his due.

The implications were immense. If the galaxies are all moving apart, they must once have been closer together. Run the film backwards, and at some point in the past, everything must have been crammed together in an incredibly dense state. In 1931 Lemaître proposed that the universe began from what he called a "primeval atom", which had broken apart in a kind of cosmic fireworks display. This was the first version of what we now call the Big Bang.

Einstein accepted the expanding universe and dropped his cosmological constant. According to the physicist George Gamow, Einstein later called it the biggest blunder of his life. (Gamow is the only source for that remark, and some historians doubt Einstein ever said it.) The irony, as we shall see, is that the cosmological constant would come back.

## Big Bang or Steady State?

### A name meant as a joke

Not everyone liked the idea of a universe with a beginning. To some it sounded suspiciously like the Book of Genesis, which was perhaps not surprising given that one of its authors was a priest. In 1948 three Cambridge scientists, Fred Hoyle, Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold, proposed an alternative: the **Steady State** theory. The universe, they argued, was expanding, but it had no beginning and no end. As galaxies moved apart, new matter was continuously created in the gaps to form new galaxies, so that the universe always looked the same.

Hoyle was a brilliant, combative Yorkshireman and a gifted broadcaster. In a BBC radio talk in March 1949, describing the rival theory, he referred to it as the idea that all the matter in the universe was created in "one big bang" at a particular time in the remote past. He later insisted he did not mean it as an insult, merely as a vivid image for radio listeners. Whatever his intention, the name stuck to the very theory he spent his life opposing.

Meanwhile, the Big Bang was gaining serious support. In 1948 George Gamow and his student Ralph Alpher published a paper calculating how the lightest elements, hydrogen and helium, could have been forged in the first few minutes of a hot Big Bang. Gamow, an incorrigible joker, added the name of the distinguished physicist Hans Bethe, who had not been involved, so that the authors read Alpher, Bethe, Gamow, a pun on the first three letters of the Greek alphabet, alpha, beta, gamma. The calculations of the amounts of hydrogen and helium the Big Bang should have produced, later refined, match what we observe in the universe remarkably well: about three-quarters hydrogen and one-quarter helium by mass.

Alpher and another colleague, Robert Herman, also made a prediction. If the universe began hot and dense, the radiation from that early fireball should still be around, cooled by the expansion of the universe to a temperature just a few degrees above absolute zero, and filling the whole sky. Almost nobody went looking for it.

### The pigeons of Holmdel

In 1964 two radio astronomers at Bell Telephone Laboratories in Holmdel, New Jersey, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, were trying to use a large horn-shaped antenna, originally built for satellite communications experiments, to study radio waves from the Milky Way. They had a problem. There was a faint, persistent hiss of noise in their receiver that they could not get rid of. It was the same day and night, summer and winter, wherever they pointed the antenna.

![The Holmdel Horn Antenna at Bell Labs in New Jersey, with which Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson accidentally discovered the cosmic microwave background, the afterglow of the Big Bang, in 1964-65.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/holmdel.jpg)
*The Holmdel Horn Antenna, Bell Telephone Laboratories, New Jersey. NASA, public domain (restored), via Wikimedia Commons.*

They checked everything. They suspected a pair of pigeons that had been nesting in the horn and had coated it with what Penzias drily called "a white dielectric material". They removed the pigeons (which kept coming back, and were eventually, sadly, shot) and cleaned out the droppings. The hiss remained.

Penzias mentioned the problem to another astronomer, who told him about a group of physicists at Princeton, just down the road, led by Robert Dicke, who were building an instrument to search for the radiation left over from the Big Bang. Penzias telephoned Dicke. Dicke listened, put the phone down, and is reported to have turned to his colleagues and said: "Boys, we've been scooped."

Penzias and Wilson had found the **cosmic microwave background**: the afterglow of the Big Bang, light released when the universe was about 380,000 years old and had cooled enough for atoms to form and light to travel freely, now stretched by the expansion of the universe into microwaves. Its temperature is about 2.7 degrees above absolute zero. It is the oldest light in the universe, and it comes from every direction. A small part of the "snow" on the screen of an old analogue television, tuned between channels, was caused by it: you could literally watch the Big Bang on television. Penzias and Wilson won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1978. The Steady State theory, which could not explain the radiation, never recovered.

### Mapping the baby universe

Since then, a series of satellites has mapped the cosmic microwave background in ever finer detail. NASA's COBE satellite, launched in 1989, showed that its spectrum was a near-perfect match for the glow of a hot body, exactly as the Big Bang predicted, and in 1992 detected tiny ripples in its temperature, differences of only a few parts in 100,000. These ripples are the seeds from which galaxies and clusters of galaxies later grew. The COBE scientists John Mather and George Smoot won the Nobel Prize in 2006. Smoot told reporters that, for a religious person, it was like looking at the face of God, a line that his colleagues found rather embarrassing.

![The cosmic microwave background as mapped by NASA's WMAP satellite: the oldest light in the universe, emitted about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. The colours show temperature differences of a few millionths of a degree, the seeds of every galaxy.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/wmap.png)
*The cosmic microwave background, nine-year map from the Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe. NASA / WMAP Science Team, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

NASA's WMAP satellite (2001-2010) and the European Space Agency's Planck satellite (2009-2013) mapped those ripples with exquisite precision. From their patterns, cosmologists have been able to calculate the age of the universe, about 13.8 billion years, and its contents, with an accuracy of about one per cent. It is one of the great achievements of modern science that we can state the age of the universe with more confidence than we can state the date of the founding of Rome.

## The Dark Side of the Universe

### The missing mass

The Big Bang solved some mysteries and created others. The first was discovered surprisingly early. In 1933 a Swiss astronomer working in California, Fritz Zwicky, a brilliant and notoriously abrasive man who called his colleagues "spherical bastards" (because they were bastards whichever way you looked at them), measured the speeds of galaxies in the Coma Cluster. They were moving so fast that the cluster should have flown apart long ago, unless it contained far more mass than could be seen in its stars. He called the invisible stuff *dunkle Materie*: dark matter. Most astronomers ignored him.

In the 1970s the American astronomer Vera Rubin, working with the instrument-maker Kent Ford, measured how fast stars orbit the centres of spiral galaxies. If most of a galaxy's mass were in its bright central regions, stars on the outskirts should orbit more slowly, as the outer planets of the solar system orbit the Sun more slowly than the inner ones. Instead, Rubin found that stars far out in the disc orbit just as fast as those closer in. The only explanation was that each galaxy is embedded in a vast, invisible halo of matter, outweighing the visible stars several times over.

Rubin, who had been refused admission to Princeton's graduate astronomy programme because it did not accept women, became one of the most respected astronomers in the world. She was widely considered to deserve a Nobel Prize, but she died in 2016 without receiving one. The great new survey telescope in the Chilean Andes, which released its first images in 2025, is named the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in her honour.

Dark matter is now one of the central facts of cosmology. It does not emit, absorb or reflect light. We detect it only through its gravity: the way it holds galaxies together and bends the light of more distant objects, an effect called gravitational lensing. According to the best current measurements, there is about five times as much dark matter in the universe as ordinary matter, the stuff that makes up stars, planets and people. What it actually is remains unknown. Physicists have built ever more sensitive detectors, deep underground to shield them from cosmic rays, to catch particles of dark matter passing through the Earth. So far, they have caught nothing. Some physicists have proposed modifying the laws of gravity instead, though most cosmologists think dark matter is the better explanation.

### The accelerating universe

The second great mystery arrived in 1998. Two rival teams of astronomers, one led by Saul Perlmutter and the other by Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess, were using a particular type of exploding star, the type Ia supernova, as a standard candle to measure how fast the expansion of the universe was slowing down. It seemed obvious that it should be slowing down, as the gravity of all the matter in the universe pulled against the expansion.

It was not slowing down. It was speeding up. The distant supernovae were fainter, and therefore further away, than they should have been if the expansion had been decelerating. Something was pushing the universe apart, faster and faster. The two teams, each hoping the other had made a mistake, found the same result. They shared the Nobel Prize in 2011.

The something has been named **dark energy**, and nobody knows what it is. The simplest explanation is that it is a property of empty space itself: Einstein's cosmological constant, back from the dead. According to the current standard model of cosmology, the universe is about 5 per cent ordinary matter, about 27 per cent dark matter and about 68 per cent dark energy. In other words, everything we have ever seen with any telescope, every star, galaxy, planet and cloud of gas, makes up about one-twentieth of the universe. The rest is a mystery with a name. Recent results from a large galaxy survey, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument in Arizona, have hinted that dark energy may be weakening over time rather than staying constant, which, if confirmed, would be a major upset. The question is very much open.

### Inflation

There were other puzzles too. Why is the cosmic microwave background almost exactly the same temperature in every direction, even in regions of the sky so far apart that light could never have travelled between them to even things out? Why is space so remarkably flat, balanced on a knife-edge between eventual collapse and endless dispersal?

In 1980 a young American physicist, Alan Guth, proposed an answer. In the first tiny fraction of a second after the Big Bang, he suggested, the universe underwent a burst of extraordinarily rapid expansion, doubling in size again and again in far less than a trillionth of a trillionth of a second. This **inflation** would have stretched a tiny, evenly mixed region into everything we can now see, and flattened out any curvature, just as the surface of a balloon looks flatter the larger it gets. It would also have stretched tiny quantum jitters into the seeds of the ripples seen in the cosmic microwave background. Inflation has been developed and refined by many physicists since, and most cosmologists accept some version of it, though it has never been directly proven, and it has some vocal critics.

## New Eyes

### The telescope with the wrong glasses

On 24 April 1990 the space shuttle *Discovery* launched the **Hubble Space Telescope**, named after Edwin Hubble. Above the blurring and absorbing effects of the Earth's atmosphere, it was supposed to see the universe more clearly than ever before. The first images were a bitter disappointment. They were blurry. The telescope's main mirror, it turned out, had been ground to the wrong shape. The error was about two microns at the edge, about one-fiftieth of the thickness of a human hair, caused by a measuring device that had been assembled incorrectly. It was one of the most expensive mistakes in the history of science, and comedians had a field day.

In December 1993 a team of astronauts flew to the telescope on the space shuttle *Endeavour* and, in a series of spacewalks, installed corrective optics: in effect, they fitted Hubble with a pair of glasses. It worked perfectly. Over the next three decades, Hubble became perhaps the most productive scientific instrument ever built, and its images of nebulae, galaxies and star clusters became some of the most famous pictures in the world.

### Ten days staring at nothing

In December 1995 the director of the Space Telescope Science Institute, Robert Williams, made a bold decision. He used his own allocation of observing time to point Hubble at a tiny patch of sky near the Big Dipper that appeared to be completely empty, about the size of a tennis ball seen from a hundred metres away, and keep it there, collecting light, for ten days. Many colleagues thought he was wasting valuable telescope time.

The result, the **Hubble Deep Field**, contained around 3,000 galaxies, some of them so far away that their light had been travelling for most of the age of the universe. An even longer exposure in 2003-04, the Hubble Ultra Deep Field, showed about 10,000 galaxies in a patch of sky less than a tenth of the width of the full Moon. Extrapolate across the whole sky, and the observable universe contains hundreds of billions of galaxies, perhaps more; a 2016 study put the figure at two trillion, though later measurements have suggested a lower number. Every one of those galaxies contains, on average, many billions of stars.

![The Hubble Ultra Deep Field: about 10,000 galaxies in a patch of sky less than a tenth of the width of the full Moon. Almost every point of light in it is a galaxy.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/hudf.jpg)
*The Hubble Ultra Deep Field (2004). NASA and the European Space Agency, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

### Webb

On Christmas Day 2021, after years of delays and cost overruns, the **James Webb Space Telescope** was launched from French Guiana on a European Ariane 5 rocket. It is a joint project of NASA, the European Space Agency and the Canadian Space Agency. Its main mirror, 6.5 metres across and made of eighteen hexagonal segments coated in a thin layer of gold, was too big to fit inside any rocket, so it was folded up like origami and unfolded in space, in a sequence with hundreds of possible points of failure. It worked. The telescope now orbits the Sun around a point about 1.5 million kilometres from Earth, called L2, shielded from the heat of the Sun by a sunshade the size of a tennis court.

Webb observes mainly in infrared light, which is crucial for cosmology. Light from the most distant galaxies has been stretched by the expansion of the universe so much that it has shifted out of the visible range into the infrared. Webb can see galaxies as they were less than 300 million years after the Big Bang. The first full-colour image released, in July 2022, showed a galaxy cluster called SMACS 0723, whose gravity bends and magnifies the light of galaxies far behind it, in a picture packed with thousands of galaxies.

![Webb's First Deep Field, released in July 2022: the galaxy cluster SMACS 0723, whose gravity acts as a lens, bending and magnifying the light of far more distant galaxies behind it into arcs.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/webb.jpg)
*Webb's First Deep Field, SMACS 0723. NASA, ESA, CSA and STScI, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Webb has already produced surprises. It has found more bright, massive galaxies in the early universe than many models predicted, which has kept theorists busy. It has also sharpened one of the most important puzzles in modern cosmology, known as the **Hubble tension**. Measurements of how fast the universe is expanding today, using Cepheids and supernovae, give a value of about 73 kilometres per second per megaparsec. Calculations based on the cosmic microwave background, using the standard model of cosmology, predict about 67. The difference is small, but the measurements have become so precise that it is hard to explain away as error. Either something is wrong with the measurements, or something is missing from our understanding of the universe. Many cosmologists suspect the latter.

## Other Messengers

For most of history, all our knowledge of the universe came from light, in one form or another. In the last decade, we have started to listen to it in new ways.

On 14 September 2015 two detectors in the United States, Louisiana and Washington State, part of the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory, or LIGO, recorded a tiny tremor. It was a **gravitational wave**, a ripple in space-time itself, predicted by Einstein a century earlier, produced by two black holes, each around thirty times the mass of the Sun, spiralling together and merging about 1.3 billion years ago. The distortion of space it caused as it passed through the Earth was a fraction of the width of a proton over a four-kilometre detector. The leaders of the project won the Nobel Prize in 2017. Since then, gravitational-wave observatories have detected the collisions of hundreds of black holes and neutron stars.

In April 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope, a network of radio dishes spread around the world acting together as a single telescope the size of the Earth, released the first image of a black hole: a glowing orange ring around a dark centre in the galaxy M87, 55 million light-years away. In 2022 it released an image of the black hole at the centre of our own Milky Way, Sagittarius A\*.

And we have found other worlds. In 1995 the Swiss astronomers Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz discovered the first planet orbiting a Sun-like star other than our own, a giant world called 51 Pegasi b, so close to its star that its year lasts just over four days. (Planets had been found around a pulsar, a dead star, three years earlier.) Today more than 6,000 planets orbiting other stars have been confirmed, and astronomers think that most stars have planets. Whether any of them harbour life is one of the great open questions of science.

## A Pale Blue Dot

On 14 February 1990 the Voyager 1 space probe, having finished its tour of the outer planets and heading out of the solar system, turned its camera around at the request of the astronomer Carl Sagan and took a series of photographs looking back towards the Sun. In one of them, from a distance of about six billion kilometres, the Earth appears as a tiny speck, less than a single pixel, caught in a band of scattered sunlight.

![The Pale Blue Dot: the Earth, the tiny speck in the band of light on the right, photographed by Voyager 1 from about six billion kilometres away on 14 February 1990.](/assets/essays/the-edge-of-everything/images/paleblue.png)
*The Pale Blue Dot, Voyager 1, 1990. NASA/JPL, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

Sagan called it the "pale blue dot", and wrote a famous meditation on it: on how every human being who has ever lived, every king and peasant, every saint and sinner, lived out their lives on that mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam. It is the end point of the journey that began when the first Greek looked up and asked what the lights in the sky were made of. We once thought we were at the centre of everything. We have learnt that we live on a small planet orbiting an ordinary star, one of a few hundred billion in an ordinary galaxy, one of hundreds of billions of galaxies in an observable universe that is itself perhaps only a small part of something larger.

## What We Still Don't Know

It would be wrong to end on a note of completion, because the most striking thing about modern cosmology is how much it does not know.

**What are dark matter and dark energy?** Together they make up about 95 per cent of the universe, and we have no idea what either of them is. That is a little like a biologist admitting that 95 per cent of living things are unknown.

**Why does the Hubble tension exist?** Is it a measurement problem, or a crack in the standard model that will lead to new physics?

**What happened at the very beginning?** Our physics breaks down at the first instant of the Big Bang, where general relativity and quantum mechanics, the two great theories of twentieth-century physics, have to be combined, and nobody yet knows how to do it. It is not even clear whether "before the Big Bang" is a meaningful phrase.

**Is our universe the only one?** Some versions of inflation suggest that our universe is one bubble among a vast, perhaps infinite, number of others, a "multiverse". Whether this is science or metaphysics, since we may never be able to observe other universes, is fiercely debated.

**How will it end?** If dark energy stays constant, the universe will go on expanding forever, faster and faster. Galaxies beyond our local group will vanish from view, stars will burn out, and the universe will fade into a cold, dark, almost empty "heat death" over unimaginable spans of time. If dark energy changes, other fates are possible: a "Big Rip" that tears everything apart, or even an eventual collapse. The DESI results that hint at a changing dark energy have reopened the question.

The tools to answer some of these questions are being built now. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile will photograph the entire southern sky every few nights for ten years, building a time-lapse film of the universe. The European Space Agency's Euclid telescope, launched in 2023, is mapping billions of galaxies to trace the effects of dark matter and dark energy. NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, named after the agency's first chief astronomer, is due to join them soon.

## Back Through the Sky

The traveller in the Flammarion engraving pokes his head through the sky and sees wheels and fires and strange machinery. The real view, it turns out, is stranger. Beyond our sky lies a universe that is expanding, that had a beginning, that is filled with the cooling afterglow of its own birth, that is made mostly of things we cannot see, and that is so large that light from its most distant visible regions has been travelling for over 13 billion years to reach us.

What is perhaps most remarkable is how we found all this out: not by travelling there, but by thinking very hard about faint smudges of light. A librarian with a stick, a canon with a book he was afraid to publish, a nobleman with a metal nose, a mystic who trusted his data more than his dreams, a professor with a tube of glass, a recluse with a theory of gravity, a woman at a desk at Harvard, a priest with an equation, two radio astronomers and some pigeons. Each of them pushed their head a little further through the sky.

Kepler, writing about his own work, said that the mind could find in the heavens a pleasure greater than any on Earth. He was talking about the beauty of mathematical laws, but he might have been talking about the whole long adventure. We still do not know what is at the edge of everything. But we know far more than anyone could have dreamt when the first people lay on their backs in a dark field and wondered what the stars were. And the most exciting thing about the story is that it is nowhere near finished.
